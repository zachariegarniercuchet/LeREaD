Supreme Court of Prince Edward Island - Appeal Division

Reference re Remuneration of Judges of the Provincial Court of Prince Edward Island

Date: 1994-12-16

G.L. Campbell and E.P. Rossiter, Q.C., for Attorney-General of Prince Edward Island.

D. Sanderson, for accused persons generally.

P. Ghiz, for Omar Pineau, Robert Christie and Merlin MacDonald.

Court File No. AD-0570

The judgment of the court was delivered by

[1]                Mitchell J.A.:—The Lieutenant-Governor in Council, pursuant to s. 18 of the Supreme Court Act, R.S.P.E.I. 1988, c. S-10, referred the following questions to this court for hearing and consideration:

1. Can the Legislature of the Province of Prince Edward Island make laws such that the remuneration of Judges of the Provincial Court may be decreased, increased, or otherwise adjusted, either:

(a) as part of an overall public economic measure, or

(b) in certain circumstances established by law?

2. If the answer to 1(a) or (b) is yes, then do the Judges of the Provincial Court of Prince Edward Island currently enjoy a basic or sufficient degree of financial security or remuneration such that they constitute an independent and impartial tribunal within the meaning of Section 11(d) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and such other sections as may be applicable?

[2]                The hearing took place from November 29 to December 2, 1994, and during that time, the court heard submissions on behalf of the Attorney-General of Prince Edward Island as well as from counsel representing accused persons generally, who argued the opposing point of view, and from counsel representing three specific accused persons who had challenged the independence of the Provincial Court in other proceedings. As well, Chief Judge Plamondon (as he then was), of the Prince Edward Island Provincial Court, and the Canadian Association of Provincial Court Judges were given an opportunity to make submissions but declined to do so. The Attorney-General of Canada was also given notice, but he too declined the opportunity to make submissions.

[3]                The reference has come about because, following the coming into force of the Public Sector Pay Reduction Act, S.P.E.I. 1994, c. 51, numerous accused persons challenged the competence of the Provincial Court to exercise jurisdiction over them. These accused persons contend the court has lost its status as an independent tribunal within the meaning of s. 11(d) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms due to the fact the aforesaid legislation reduced the salaries of the Provincial Court judges (along with those of many others paid from the public purse) by 7.5%. These challenges have caused considerable controversy, uncertainty, and delay in and about the Provincial Court, and in the administration of justice in the province generally.

[4]                The Government of Prince Edward Island in 1994, like most others in Canada at the time, was faced with a severe deficit problem and saw an urgency to cutting its spending so as to get the province’s finances into acceptable order. Accordingly, as a deficit reduction measure, the government introduced, and the legislature passed, the Public Sector Pay Reduction Act. According to s. 2, the specific purpose of this legislation is: (a) as a deficit reduction measure, to reduce the pay rates of all those persons, except independent contractors, who on behalf of a public sector employer, perform duties and functions that entitle that person to pay, and (b) to implement certain other cost reductions in the public sector. The term “public sector employer” is very broadly defined in s. 1(d) of the Act. Section 2(2) declares that these reductions would be without compensation or any other offsetting considerations. Section 3 provides that, where there is a conflict with other legislation; the provisions of the Public Sector Pay Reduction Act prevail.

[5]                The Public Sector Pay Reduction Act is divided into five parts. Part I deals with interpretation. The heading of Part II is “Public Sector Employees”. This part includes ss. 5 to 8 of the Act and deals with reductions to employees of public sector employers, including those covered by collective agreements. Under s. 6, the pay of those public sector employees earning $28,000 or less is reduced by 3.5% and the pay of those earning more than that is reduced by 7.5% so long as they do not go below $26, 950. Part III is headed “Persons Paid From Public Funds”, and provides for reductions in the amounts paid to persons appointed as members of provincial tribunals, commissions, agencies, and to Provincial Court judges and medical doctors. Section 10 under this part contains the one and only specific reference in the entire Public Sector Pay Reduction Act to the Provincial Court. It simply purports to amend the existing salary-fixing formula (for those presently sitting, 100% of the average salary paid to Provincial Court judges in the other provinces) provided for in s. 3(3) of the Provincial Court Act, R.S.P.E.I. 1988, c. P-25, so that the salaries of the judges will be 7.5% less than they would otherwise have been had the formula remained unchanged. The effect of the amendment is to reduce the salary of each of the three currently sitting Provincial Court judges in Prince Edward Island from 100% to 92.5% of the average of that paid to the Provincial Court judges in the other provinces of Canada.

[6]                Similar deficit reduction measures have been introduced in other provinces. Recently, McDonald J. of the Alberta Court of Queen’s Bench in R. v. Campbell, November 15, 1994 (unreported) [summarized 25 W.C.B. (2d) 397], and Scollin J. of the Manitoba Court of Queen’s Bench in Manitoba Provincial Judges Assn. v. Manitoba (Minister of Justice), November 25, 1994 (unreported) [now reported 30 C.P.C. (3d) 1, 51 A.C.W.S. (3d) 1047], have held that public sector pay cuts affecting Provincial Court judges in those provinces violated the financial security of the judges and thus conflict with s. 11(d) of the Charter. However, while McDonald J. ruled the Alberta reductions to judges’ salaries were invalid, Scollin J. decided instead to uphold the constitutionality of the Manitoba legislation by reading it down so, he says at p. 5 of his judgment [p. 38 C.P.C.]:

… as to provide only for a temporary suspension of the actual payment of full compensation, subject, on the expiry of the legislation, to full retroactive reinstatement of all compensation that was suspended.

[7]                In this province, K.R. MacDonald C.J.T.D. in Lowther v. Prince Edward Island, September 21, 1994 (unreported) [now reported 118 D.L.R. (4th) 665, 24 C.R.R. (2d) 145 sub nom. R. v. Lowther, 122 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 221], held that it was not the reduction itself but the fact that the Public Sector Pay Reduction Act allowed for judges of the Provincial Court to negotiate with government the manner in which the reduction would be implemented that created a perception they lacked judicial independence. Subsequent to this decision, the Lieutenant-Governor in Council, on September 29, 1994, approved a regulation purportedly made pursuant to s. 13 of the Public Sector Pay Reduction Act which, among other things, renders the provisions for negotiation in the Act inapplicable to Provincial Court judges.

[8]                The issues raised in the aforementioned cases are not so novel as they may seem to some. Peter Hogg in vol. 1, 3rd ed., of his Constitutional Law of Canada (Scarborough, Ont.: Carswell, 1992) [updated to 1994], says at p. 7-6:

… during the depression of the 1930s, there was much controversy in Canada (and England) as to whether it was proper to reduce judicial salaries by ten percent (20 percent in England) by a statute which applied to the entire federal civil service as well. The argument that such a nondiscriminatory reduction would threaten judicial independence seems fantastic, but it was seriously advanced by constitutional lawyers, judges, government ministers and members of parliament.

More recently, these issues were raised in the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. Valente (1985), 23 C.C.C. (3d) 193, 24 D.L.R. (4th) 161, [1985] 2 S.C.R. 673, and in Canada v. Beauregard (1986), 30 D.L.R. (4th) 481, [1986] 2 S.C.R. 56, 26 C.R.R. 59. In Valente, the Supreme Court held that the judges of the Ontario Provincial Court were independent within the meaning of s. 11(d), even though one of the grounds of objection (see No. (14), p. 198 C.C.C., p. 166 D.L.R.) was that the judges had the same sick leave terms as civil servants and their salary would, therefore, be reduced in the same manner as a civil servant when sick. And in Beauregard, the Supreme Court of Canada held that an amendment to the federal Judges Act requiring judges to contribute to the cost of their pensions (which effectively reduced their salaries) was not prohibited.

[9]                With all the foregoing said as background, I will now restate and answer the questions posed in the reference for the opinion of this court.

Question No. 1

Can the Legislature of the Province of Prince Edward Island make laws such that the remuneration of Judges of the Provincial Court may be decreased, increased, or otherwise adjusted, either:

(a) as part of an overall public economic measure, or

(b) in certain circumstances established by law?

Answer

[10]           There is no law which explicitly prohibits the legislature of Prince Edward Island from reducing or otherwise altering the salaries and benefits of Provincial Court judges once they have been established, but that does not mean that it has unlimited power to do so. The supremacy of the legislature and its power to make laws affecting Provincial Court judges is subject to s. 11(d) of the Charter, which provides:

11. Any person charged with an offence has the right

. . . . .

(d) to be presumed innocent until proven guilty according to law in a fair and public hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal;

(Emphasis added.) Section 11(d) is part of the Constitution of Canada, and therefore, according to s. 52 of the Constitution Act, 1982, is the supreme law of Canada, and any law that is inconsistent with its provisions is to that extent of no force or effect. In Valente, supra (a case in which the Supreme Court of Canada dealt with the specific issue of whether a Provincial Court judge sitting as the Provincial Court (Criminal Division) in Ontario in December, 1982, was an independent tribunal within the meaning of s. 11(d) of the Charter), Le Dain J., writing for a unanimous seven-member panel, says at pp. 201-2 C.C.C., pp. 169-70 D.L.R.:

The word “independent” in s. 11(d) reflects or embodies the traditional constitutional value of judicial independence. As such, it connotes … a status or relationship to others, particularly to the Executive Branch of government, that rests on objective conditions or guarantees.

Then, at p. 204 C.C.C., p. 172 D.L.R., Le Dain J. says that the test for independence for purposes of s. 11(d) is an objective one whether the tribunal can reasonably be perceived as independent. At pp. 204-5 C.C.C., pp. 172-3 D.L.R., he says:

The perception must … be a perception of whether the tribunal enjoys the essential objective conditions or guarantees of judicial independence, and not a perception of how it will in fact act, regardless of whether it enjoys such conditions or guarantees.

(Emphasis added.) Le Dain J. then went on, at p. 216 C.C.C., p. 184 D.L.R., to identify “financial security” as one of the essential conditions of judicial independence for the purposes of s. 11(d). He says:

That means security of salary or other remuneration, and where appropriate, security of pension. The essence of such security is that the right to salary and pension should be established by law and not be subject to arbitrary interference by the executive in a manner that could affect judicial independence.

[11]           Currently, the salaries for Provincial Court judges in Prince Edward Island are established according to a formula adopted by the legislature. They are not left to be determined by the executive branch as they were in Ontario at the time of Valente, supra. Nevertheless, what the Supreme Court of Canada said about the essential conditions for judicial independence within the meaning of s. 11(d) also applies as a limitation on the power of the legislative branch: see Beauregard, supra, and R. v. Lippé (1991), 64 C.C.C. (3d) 513, [1991] 2 S.C.R. 114, 128 N.R. 1. Therefore, the legislature of Prince Edward Island does not have an unfettered right to deal with the salary and other benefits of Provincial Court judges. It does, however, have a limited constitutional authority to alter the salary and benefits of the judges of the Provincial Court and could reduce them as part of an overall public economic measure so long as in so doing: (1) it does not remove the basic degree of financial security which is an essential condition for their independence within the meaning of s. 11(d) of the Charter, and (2) there is no indication that the legislation amounts to arbitrary interference with the judiciary in the sense that it is being enacted for an improper or colourable purpose, or that it discriminates against judges vis-à-vis other citizens: see Valente, supra; Beauregard, supra, at p. 497, and R. v. Généreux (1992), 70 C.C.C. (3d) 1 at pp. 19-20, 88 D.L.R. (4th) 110 at pp. 128-30, [1992] 1 S.C.R. 259.

[12]           The essence of financial security for Provincial Court judges is the right to have salary and pension established by law and not subject to discretionary or arbitrary interference by the government in a manner that could affect judicial independence. As Le Dain J. says, at p. 218 C.C.C., p. 186 D.L.R. of Valente, supra:

The essential point … is that the right to salary of a provincial court judge is established by law, and there is no way in which the executive could interfere with that right in a manner to affect the independence of the individual judge.

[13]           A general pay reduction for all who hold public office, including judges, which is enacted by the provincial legislature as part of a broad public economic measure, and which does not discriminate against judges, would not in itself violate the degree of financial security essential for judicial independence within the meaning of s. 11(d). So long as the salaries of the judges, even though reduced as aforesaid, remain established by law and beyond arbitrary interference by the government in a manner that could affect the judicial independence of the individual judge, the reasonable and informed person, viewing the matter from an objective standpoint, should still perceive the court as enjoying the essential condition of judicial independence. That, the authorities say, is the test that must be applied to determine whether a tribunal is independent within the meaning of s. 11(d) of the Charter: see Valente, supra, at p. 204 C.C.C., p. 172 D.L.R.; Lippé, supra, at pp. 533-4, and Généreux, supra, at pp. 20-1 C.C.C., pp. 129-31 D.L.R. Accordingly, my answer to Q. 1 would be a “qualified yes”.

Question No. 2

If the answer to 1(a) or (b) is yes, then do Judges of the Provincial Court of Prince Edward Island currently enjoy a basic or sufficient degree of financial security or remuneration such that they constitute an independent and impartial tribunal within the meaning of Section 11(d) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and such other sections as may be applicable?

Answer

[14]           Since I have answered Q. 1 with a “qualified yes”, it is necessary to answer Q. 2. It is understood that this question is not intended to deal with the actual amount the judges receive, but rather with whether, in spite of the pay reduction effected by the Public Sector Pay Reduction Act, they still retain a sufficient standard of financial security to constitute an independent tribunal within the meaning of s. 11(d) of the Charter. It is my opinion that they do. I have reached that conclusion for the following reasons:

(1) the reduction is a coherent part of an overall public economic measure designed to meet a legitimate governmental objective;

(2) it is non-discriminatory in that it applies generally to all salaried public offices and indeed to virtually everyone who receives a paycheque from the provincial government;

(3) the right of judges to their salaries remains established by law and, therefore, beyond arbitrary interference by government, and

(4) there is no hint that the Public Sector Pay Reduction Act was enacted for any improper or colourable purpose.

[15]           All that being the case, I do not think that reasonable and right-minded persons, after applying themselves to the question, properly informing themselves, and then viewing the matter realistically and practically, and having thought the matter through, could conclude that the independence of the Provincial Court judges has been compromised. Question 2 is, therefore, answered in the affirmative.

Judgment accordingly.
