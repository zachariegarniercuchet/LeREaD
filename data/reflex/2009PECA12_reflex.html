FAMILY LAW - Divorce - Corollary Relief - Custody of Children - Mobility

 

Appeal by the father of a permanent custody order which granted day-to-day care and control of three children of the marriage to the mother and permitted the mother to move the children from Prince Edward Island to Quebec.

Reasons for judgment:

 

MURPHY J.A.:

 

[1]               This is an appeal from a decision of Mitchell J. on August 1, 2008 (D.P. v. R.B. 2008 PESCTD 33) where he ordered joint custody of the children with the mother having defacto day-to-day custody of the children.

 

[2]               The appellant and respondent were married August 17, 1996 and separated March 1, 2006. At separation the parties resided at Charlottetown, P.E.I. with their children. There were three children born of the marriage: P.C., born June 1994; A.P., born September 2000, and C.M., born June 2003.

 

[3]               The appellant is a native of Prince Edward Island and the respondent is a native of Quebec where she now resides with the three children.  This latter fact is the basis of the dispute between the parties.

 

THE PROCEEDINGS TO DATE

 

[4]               The respondent filed a Petition for Divorce on February 21, 2007 seeking an order dissolving the marriage, an order granting her and the appellant joint custody of the three children, an order granting her defacto custody and an order requiring the appellant to pay child support. In the Petition for Divorce the respondent indicated that she was moving to the Province of Quebec.

 

[5]               In May, 2007 the respondent made a motion for interim relief under the provisions of the Divorce Act.  Specifically she sought the following relief:

 

(1)        that she and the appellant be granted joint custody of their three children;

 

(2)        that she be granted defacto custody of the children;

 

(3)        that the appellant be granted access to the children at specified times;

 

(4)        that the appellant be ordered to pay child support;

 

(5)        that the respondent be granted possession of the family home; and

 

(6)        that the appellant and respondent each shall maintain and pay for an  insurance policy on the life of each other to be held in trust for the  children.

 

[6]               This motion was made pursuant to s.16(1) of the Divorce Act.  In support of her motion, the respondent filed an affidavit stating if she were granted defacto custody of the children, she intended to move with the children to the Province of Quebec in September 2007.

 

[7]               The motions judge granted the respondent’s motion in D.P. v. R.B. 2007 PESCTD 25; [2007] P.E.I.J. No. 33, and the respondent moved with the three children to the Province of Quebec.  The appellant was granted access rights over the summer months as well as at specified times during the school year.

 

[8]               The appellant filed an appeal from the above order as well as a motion for an order staying the operation of the interim order.  The motion for a stay was denied.  The appeal was expedited, and this court allowed the appeal on the basis that on an interim motion, the status quo should not be disturbed without compelling reasons.  The Court of Appeal decision (2007 PESCAD 25) was rendered on November 21, 2007 when the respondent and children were residing in Quebec.  Considering the potential disruption that another move could cause, this court ordered that the children not return until the end of the school year.

 

[9]               Before the school year ended, a full hearing was scheduled from June 9-13, 2008 to hear the issue of permanent custody of the children as well as a relocation to the Province of Quebec. The hearing included competing claims by the parties for day-to-day care and control of the children.

 

[10]           The trial judge ordered that the parties would have joint custody of their children with the defacto day-to-day custody, care and control of the children to remain with the respondent.  Essentially, the respondent was granted the relief she sought  and was permitted to keep the children in the Province of Quebec.  The appellant was provided as much access as could be provided given the distance between the parties.  The trial judge also varied the child support payable and ordered the appellant to pay the table amount of child support pursuant to the Child Support Guidelines based on his income.  It is this decision respecting defacto custody and child support that is the subject of this appeal.

 

NOTICE OF APPEAL

 

[11]           The appellant listed six grounds of appeal as set out below. Five of the grounds assert that the trial judge made errors in his consideration of custody:

 

1.             That the Learned Trial Judge erred in law by failing to properly consider the existing custody arrangement and relationship between the children and the custodial parent as well as the access parent.

 

2.             That the Learned Trial Judge erred in law by failing to properly consider the relevant facts when considering the desirability of maximizing contact between the children and both parents.

 

3.             That the Learned Trial Judge erred in law by giving more weight to the respondent’s views than the appellant’s.

 

4.             That the Learned Trial Judge erred in law by failing to properly considering [sic] the disruption to the children consequent upon removal from family, schools, and the community he or she has come to know.

 

5.             That the Learned Trial Judge erred in not fully considering the respondent mother returning to Prince Edward Island.

 

6.             That the Learned Trial Judge erred by failing to impute the income of the respondent mother when addressing the child support issue.

 

DISPOSITION

 

[12]           I would dismiss the appeal.

 

STANDARD OF REVIEW

 

[13]           The scope of appellate review of custody matters is very limited.  As discussed in Van de Perre v. Edwards, 2001 SCC 60, [2001] 2 S.C.R. 1014, a court may only intervene in a final award of custody where there has been a material error in law or a misapprehension of the evidence or the conclusions drawn from it.  A material error is further explained in para.15:

 

As indicated in both Gordon and Hickey, the approach to appellate review requires an indication of a material error. If there is an indication that the trial judge did not consider relevant factors or evidence, this might indicate that he did not properly weigh all of the factors. In such a case, an appellate  court may review the evidence proffered at trial to determine if the trial judge ignored or misdirected himself with respect to relevant evidence. This being said, I repeat that omissions in the reasons will not necessarily mean that the appellate court has jurisdiction to review the evidence heard at trial. ...

 

[14]           And further in para.15 , Bastarache J. stated:

 

...an omission is only a material error if it gives rise to the reasoned belief  that the trial judge must have forgotten, ignored or misconceived the  evidence in a way that affected his conclusion.  Without this reasoned  belief, the appellate court cannot reconsider the evidence.

 

[15]           Because custody decisions are inherently exercises in discretion, they should not be readily overturned. See: Van de Perre, supra.

 

THE LAW

 

[16]           Pursuant to s-s.16(1) of the Divorce Act, a court may make an order respecting the custody and access of children of the marriage.  Subsection 16(8) sets out the factors to be considered in making an custody order.  It provides as follows:

 

(8)           In making an order under this section, the Court shall take into consideration only the best interests of the child of the marriage as determined by reference to the condition, means, needs and other circumstances of the child.

 

[17]           Parliament has decreed that the task of determining child custody must be undertaken considering the best interests of the child taking into account the condition, means, needs and other circumstances of the child.  Additionally, in a custody determination, s-s.16(9) provides that the court shall not consider past conduct of a person unless it is relevant to his or her ability to act as a parent and s-s.16(10) which establishes the principle of maximum contact:

 

(10)         In making an order under this section, the Court shall give effect to the principle that a child of the marriage should have as much contact with each spouse as is consistent with the best interests of the child, and, for that purpose, shall take into consideration the willingness of the person for whom custody is sought to facilitate such contact.

 

[18]           In the case of Gordon v. Goertz, 1996 CanLII 191 (SCC), [1996] 2 S.C.R. 27, the Supreme Court of Canada set out the principles which are applicable in determining the best interests of a child in the context of a mobility case under the Divorce Act.  The Gordon v. Goertz, supra case was a variation of a custody case while the case here before the trial judge was an initial custody determination following trial. In the Gordon v. Goertz, supra case, the custodial mother decided to move from Saskatoon to Australia to study orthodontics.  The father made application to vary the custody arrangement and the mother applied to vary the access provisions.  McLachlin J. (as she then was) adopted a two-stage inquiry.  The first stage asked whether a material change in the circumstances of the child was demonstrated.  The second stage considered custody and access on a fresh inquiry into the best interests of the child, having regard to all the relevant circumstances relating to the child’s needs and the ability of each parent to satisfy them. In the case of an initial custody order, the first stage inquiry of material change of circumstances is not applicable; however, the Supreme Court of Canada directions for assessment for the second stage inquiry are instructive of the exercise to be employed in an initial custody determination.

 

[19]           In Gordon v. Goertz, supra, McLachlin J. stated that the ultimate and only issue when it comes to custody and access is the welfare of the child whose future is at stake.

 

[20]           In the case summary, Madam Justice McLachlin summarized the law as follows in paras.49-50:

 

1.             The parent applying for a change in the custody or access order must meet the threshold requirement of demonstrating a material change in the circumstances affecting the child.

 

2.             If the threshold is met, the judge on the application must embark on a fresh inquiry into what is in the best interests of the child, having regard to all the relevant circumstances relating to the child's needs and the ability of the respective parents to satisfy them.

 

3.             This inquiry is based on the findings of the judge who made the previous order and evidence of the new circumstances.

 

4.             The inquiry does not begin with a legal presumption in favour of the custodial parent, although the custodial parent's views are entitled to great respect.

 

5.             Each case turns on its own unique circumstances. The only issue is the best interest of the child in the particular circumstances of the case.

 

6.             The focus is on the best interests of the child, not the interests and rights of the parents.

 

7.             More particularly the judge should consider, inter alia:

 

(a)           the existing custody arrangement and relationship between the child and the custodial parent;

 

(b)           the existing access arrangement and the relationship between the child and the access parent;

 

(c)           the desirability of maximizing contact between the child and both parents;

 

(d)           the views of the child;

 

(e)           the custodial parent's reason for moving, only in the exceptional case where it is relevant to that parent's ability to meet the needs of the child;

 

(f)            disruption to the child of a change in custody;

 

(g)           disruption to the child consequent on removal from family, schools, and the community he or she has come to know.

 

[50]         In the end, the importance of the child remaining with the parent to  whose custody it has become accustomed in the new location must  be weighed against the continuance of full contact with the child’s    access parent, its extended family and its community. The ultimate   question in every case is this: what is in the best interests in all the circumstances, old as well as new?

 

[21]           In the case of Nunweiler v. Nunweiler, [2000] B.C.J. No. 935, 2000 BCCA 300, Madam Justice Saunders found, at para.27 of the decision, that although the first stage inquiry (requirement of a material change in circumstances) is clearly not applicable to a case of an initial custody order:

 

....the discussion of the second stage determination of custody and access is, in my view, instructive, and the factors enunciated by Madam Justice McLachlin should be considered, with the appropriate modifications.

 

[22]           The most instructive of the reasons is contained in para.28 where Saunders J.A. stated:

 

The significance of the reasoning in Gordon v. Goertz in an initial determination of custody is, I consider, three-fold. First, the decision directs the court to consider the motive for a parent's relocation only in the context of assessing the parent's ability to meet the needs of the child. This, in my view, is as relevant a direction on an initial custody hearing as on a variation hearing. Second, the decision confirms the significance of the instruction, found in s. 16(10), to consider the willingness of a parent to facilitate contact, but notes that this consideration is subordinate to over-all consideration of the best interests of the child. Third, and more broadly, it approaches the issue of a relocation of residence from a perspective of respect for a parent's decision to live and work where he or she chooses, barring an improper motive.

 

[23]           Madam Justice Saunders also discussed the role of maintaining the status quo. She rejected the approach of presenting the status quo as a default position such that failing a convincing case being made for a move, the status quo is the preferred position. In her view, such an approach re-inserts into custody discussions a presumption which is contrary to the instructions in Gordon v. Goertz, supra. McLachlin J., in that case, stated that each case should be assessed individually and that presumptions are inappropriate in custody cases. To detract from the individual justice to which every child is entitled is contrary to the instruction in Gordon v. Goertz. This case also directed that the view of the parent wishing to relocate should be respected, barring an improper motive. This same theme was applied by other appellate courts in Falvai v. Falvai 2008 BCCA 503, [2008] B.C.J. No.2365; Spencer v. Spencer 2005 ABCA 262, 2005 A.J. No.934.

 

[24]           In Bjornson v. Creighton, 2002 CanLII 45125 (ON CA), 2002 O.J. No. 4364, 62 O.R. (3d) 236, the Ontario Court of Appeal set aside a trial judge’s order where he had refused to permit the mother to return to Alberta with her child.  The Court of Appeal determined that it was in the child’s best interest for the mother to return to Alberta with the child.  The mother had lived and worked as a nurse in Alberta prior to moving to Ontario.  in considering the best interests of the child, the Court of Appeal took into account that the mother’s family, her “roots and connections” were in the west.  The Court of Appeal stated that the trial judge had overlooked or disregarded the social, psychological, and emotional aspects of the mother’s desire to return to Alberta with the child.  

 

ANALYSIS

 

[25]           The appellant’s five grounds of appeal regarding custody can be addressed together. They contend that the trial judge failed to consider matters that he should have considered and to give appropriate weight to various matters in evidence.

 

[26]           On first reading, it is difficult to discern whether the trial judge was approaching the case from a maintenance of the status quo (and that was the children living in Quebec) or looking at the matter as a fresh inquiry with the issue of mobility at the forefront.  The trial judge made little reference to the provisions contained in the Divorce Act. Most notably, he did not refer to s. 16(8) which sets out the factors for consideration in determining the best interests of the child. The trial judge, did however, refer extensively to the principles enunciated in Gordon v. Goertz ,supra. Nonetheless, in my assessment the trial judge did properly assess custody based on the application of the proper test of considering only the best interests of the child, by reference to the condition, means, needs and other circumstances of the child.  The reasons for judgment demonstrate that the trial judge appreciated the factors to be considered, understood the proper application of the summary in Gordon v. Goertz, supra to the assessment of custody, and employed that assessment to consider custody and mobility in an initial custody determination.  The trial judge identified the unique circumstances of the case, and how those circumstances affected each of the children.  His focus was on the best interests of the children, not the interests and rights of the parents.  He listed the factors for consideration directed by Gordon v. Goertz, supra, and then stated his observations about each of them in the context of the evidence at trial. Upon reading the judgment, the assessment by the trial judge was thorough and the conclusion was supported by the evidence and was reasonable.

 

[27]           The trial judge’s ultimate conclusion was that the defacto day-to-day custody, care and control of the children would “remain” with the respondent petitioner.  While that language initially gave me pause to question whether a presumption was being made in favor of the custodial parent, I concluded that it was not, and that it was only a reflection of the facts of the case.  Indeed, the children had always lived with their mother.  The father was involved in parenting too, a lot of the time, during the marriage; and since separation, the father exercised access.  But the mother was  constant and steadfast in the children’s lives.  She was recognized as the “go-to” parent during the duration of the marriage.  After separation, the father exercised access to varying degrees.  A few months after separation, he started to spend more of his time in Dieppe, New Brunswick, where his new personal relationship was developing, and the trial judge found that he resided in Dieppe at least from January to May, 2007.  It was in the spring of 2007 when the mother brought her interim motion for permission to move the children with her to Quebec.  At that time she felt there was nothing left for her on the Island. She did consider moving to Dieppe. Instead, she made a motion to the court and, with permission from the Court, moved with the children to the Province of Quebec where she was raised.  Although the interim order was overturned, the children remained there with the mother during the 2007-2008 school year with permission.  It is a matter of fact that at the time of trial, the mother had the primary care and control of the children, and the children were physically living with her in St-Prime, Quebec. 

 

[28]           The trial judge gave full reasons regarding his assessment of the parenting roles of the parties, both during the marriage and after separation. He was informed about the extended family and the new relationships of the parties. He considered the home study and gave his reasons for departing from it. He considered the existing custody arrangements and the relationship between the children and both the custodial parent and the access parent. The trial judge also considered the desirability of maintaining maximum contact between the children and both parents. He considered the views of the children to the extent that they were applicable considering the ages of the children. He was also mindful of the disruption to the children and the consequences of removing the children from family, schools and the community they had come to know. He also gave proper limited consideration to the mother’s reason for moving from Prince Edward Island to Quebec. In my assessment, it is not shown that the trial judge failed to properly consider any of the applicable factors.

 

[29]           I do not believe that the trial judge made any error of law.  While he placed substantial weight on the factors contained in the Gordon v. Goertz case, this was not a  misapplication of the law. Clearly, the provisions of s. 16 of the Divorce Act govern. The directions in Gordon v. Goertz, supra, are widely accepted as the proper application of the Divorce Act provisions in taking into account the means, needs and other circumstances of each child. In Nunweiler v. Nunweiler, supra, Saunders J.A. directed that a modified use of the Gordon v. Goertz factors should be applied.  The trial judge found that the respondent had been the primary care giver of the children since birth. He considered the parenting arrangements that were in place from time to time after separation and the circumstances by which the respondent  mother moved with the children from P.E.I. to Quebec.  He gave sufficient weight to all the relevant considerations regarding the children as stipulated by s. 16 of the Divorce Act, and directed by the Supreme Court of Canada in Gordon v. Goertz, supra.

 

[30]           This case required the trial judge to balance and weigh the benefits derived for the children in continuing in the custody of their mother against the desirability of the children having maximum contact with their father.  The trial judge concluded that the children had always been in the defacto custody of the mother since birth.  The trial judge was satisfied that the respondent mother demonstrated to be the preferred parent for primary care and control of the children in the period since separation, both on P.E.I. and the period of time since the children moved to Quebec.

 

[31]           It is in the province of the trial judge to find facts and make assessments on weighing the evidence. Appellate courts must be cautious in trammelling on a trial judge’s exercise of discretion in determining the best interests of the children. Very recently, the Supreme Court of Canada in Rick v. Brandsema, 2009 SCC 10 (CanLII), [2009] S.C.J. No. 10 reiterated that the trial judge has the job of judicial historian, and as a result, findings of fact and factual inferences made at trial are not to be reversed unless there is a ”palpable and overriding error or a fundamental mis-characterization or misappreciation of the evidence.”  Upon my review of the evidence, I do not hold the view that the trial judge inappropriately weighed the evidence.

 

[32]           The trial judge was aware of the testimony that the respondent mother gave where she stated that she would return to Prince Edward Island for the sake of the children. The trial judge did not make any errors in his treatment of that evidence. Various courts have cautioned that it is problematic to rely on representations made by the custodial parent that he or she will not move without the children should an application to relocate be denied. This inquiry is commonly called the “classic double bind.”  If a parent responds by stating they are not willing to remain behind with the children, this raises the prospect of the parent looking after their own interests and not having the interests of the children paramount . Then, on the other side of the equation, if a parent advises the court that they are willing to forego a move if unsuccessful, this suggests that such a move is not necessary for the well being of the parent or the children. If a trial judge mistakenly relies on a parent’s willingness to stay behind “for the sake of the children,“ the status quo becomes an attractive option for a judge to favour because it avoids the difficult decision the application presents. See: Spencer v. Spencer, supra.

 

[33]           In the case at bar, the trial judge approached the decision head on. He determined that the respondent mother did not have an improper motive in moving with the children to the province of Quebec. He concluded that requiring the petitioner to move back to P.E.I. would have a detrimental effect on the mother and that a happy parent is one who is more able to look after the needs of the children. What follows from this line of reasoning is that the trial judge approached the issue of relocation from the perspective of respect for a parent’s decision to live and work where he or she chooses barring an improper motive. See: Nunweiler v. Nunweiler. The trial judge did not err in his assessment of the factors involved or in making any of these conclusions.

 

[34]           I acknowledge that the children’s access to their father will be diminished. I realize that the appellant father provides a different perspective to the children when he has the children in his care and control such as sports and other activities. However, on viewing the evidence I am satisfied that the trial judge’s decision to provide defacto custody and care to the respondent mother is based on a proper analysis of the factors outlined in s. 16(8) of the Divorce Act, and is reasonable and supportable. The trial judge gave as much consideration as he could to the desirability of maximizing contact between the children and both parents. He decided that it would be in the best interest of the children to live with their mother and move with her to Quebec, and within that arrangement he made provision for as much parenting opportunity as possible for the father, including full summer times in Prince Edward Island, one week at both Christmas and March break, plus two additional weeks during the school year, reasonable visitation in the children’s home in Quebec, and reasonable contact by telephone, email, letters  and internet/web cam.

 

[35]           For the above reasons, I would dismiss the appeal and affirm the decision of the trial judge respecting custody and defacto care of the children of the marriage. Additionally, the trial judge’s decision respecting the payment of  child support is also confirmed.

McQUAID J.A. (dissenting):

 

[37]           I would allow the appeal and set aside the order of the trial judge.

 

[38]           The relevant evidence and facts are set forth in the reasons of the trial judge and in the reasons of Murphy J.A.  Furthermore, there are previous decisions of this court and the Supreme Court, arising from a motion and an appeal which preceded this appeal. These decisions also provide details of the evidence and background facts relevant to the appeal.  See: D.P. v. R.B. 2007 PESCAD 25; (2007), 289 D.L.R. (4th) 177; (2007), 44 R.F.L. (6th) 9 and D.P. v. R.B. 2007 PESCTD 25; (2007), 269 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 1.

 

[39]           Even within the narrow scope of appellate review applicable in cases where child custody and support are in issue, there is latitude for this Court to set aside the order of the trial judge. The trial judge made material errors of law and principle as well as palpable and overriding errors in his assessment of the evidence. Specifically, with respect to his palpable and overriding errors in the assessment of the evidence, he overlooked evidence and misapprehended evidence in a way which gives rise to a reasoned belief these oversights affected his ultimate conclusion.  See: Housen v. Nikolaisen, 2002 SCC 33 (CanLII), [2002] 2 S.C.R. 235; Hickey v. Hickey, 1999 CanLII 691 (SCC), [1999] 2 S.C.R. 518; Van de Perre v. Edwards 2001 SCC 60; [2001] 2 S.C.R. 1014. 

 

[40]           With respect, the trial judge’s order is manifestly contrary to the best interests of the three children of the marriage between the appellant and the respondent.  Also with respect, I am unable to agree with the conclusion reached by my colleagues and thus the following are my reasons for concluding that the trial judge’s order should be set aside.

 

[41]           From the outset, the trial judge considered the case before him to be a typical “mobility case” where a custodial parent in deciding to make a change in the location of his or her place of residence seeks to do so in the exercise of permanent custodial rights previously conferred by a court of competent jurisdiction.  See: Gordon v. Goertz, 1996 CanLII 191 (SCC), [1996] 2 S.C.R. 27.  The case before the trial judge was not such a case.

 

[42]           The children resided in the Province of Quebec at the time of the trial on the authority of an interim order of the Court of Appeal which conditionally provided for their residence in that province, pending the issuance of a permanent order.  See: D.P. v. R.B. 2007 PESCAD 25; (2007), 289 D.L.R. (4th) 177; (2007), 44 R.F.L. (6th) 9.  That order replaced a previous interim order of the Supreme Court which also provided that the children be in the interim custody of the respondent in the province of Quebec until a permanent order was made.  See: D.P. v. R.B. 2007 PESCTD 25; (2007), 269 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 1.

 

[43]            The trial judge failed to apply s. 16(8) of the Divorce Act which required him to take into consideration only the best interests of the children by reference to their condition, means, needs and other circumstances.  His focus was on the condition, means, needs and other circumstances of the respondent, and not the children.

 

[44]           Contrary to s. 16(9) of the Divorce Act, the trial judge took into account the past conduct of the appellant.  This included: an affair with another woman during the marriage; the commencement of a relationship with another woman after separation from the respondent; and taking up a short residency with that woman in New Brunswick while maintaining regular contact with this children in Prince Edward Island.  All of these events had a significant impact on the trial judge’s assessment of the appellant’s parental role during the marriage and after the parties separated. The  conduct was not relevant to the appellant’s ability to parent the three children and more importantly, it did not reflect in a negative way upon the meaningful contribution the appellant can make in the lives of the children.

 

[45]           There is no evidence the short term move of the appellant to Dieppe, New Brunswick, prior to the interim custody orders, was contrary to the best interests of the children.  They continued to have access to him, and he exercised the regular access which had been negotiated between the parties.  At the time of the trial for the permanent order, the appellant had, with his new partner, established a home in Prince Edward Island, and he was gainfully employed here as he was when he lived in Dieppe for a few months. The evidence of the move to Dieppe was only relevant to assessing the parenting role of the appellant and how that role might be fulfilled on the dissolution of the marriage. It did not demonstrate the appellant acted contrary to the best interests of his children.

 

[46]           The trial judge erred in considering and applying the principles of s. 16(10) of the Divorce Act which provide that, in making a permanent custody order, the court shall give effect to the principle that the children are to have as much contact with the parents as is consistent with their best interests. He measured maximum contact quantitatively and not qualitatively. The trial judge also measured the contact which the children would have with their father pursuant to his order by way of measuring this access against the access the children might have if they lived in the same community as their father.  The trial judge did so without considering the following evidence:

 

–          the time the children would have to spend traveling from Quebec to Prince Edward Island (12 to 14 hours each way) and the impact it would have on time spent with the appellant;

 

–           the holiday schedule of the appellant in the summer which would result in the children having to be placed in daycare during much of the time they spend with him during the summer season.

 

[47]           A trial judge is mandated to address maximum contact with both parents; however, that does not mean there is an absolute obligation to make an order which insures such contact.  If the evidence demonstrates it would not be in the best interests of the children to provide them with maximum contact, an order that might restrict contact with one parent does not offend s. 16(10) of the Divorce Act. See: Gordon v. Goertz at para.24.  There was no evidence before the trial judge which would indicate that it was necessary to restrict contact with the appellant. The evidence was to the contrary.  Despite a somewhat unsettled marriage relationship between the parties, the children had the benefit of the meaningful presence of both parents.  In all respects, the evidence is that the children thrived in this environment and to deprive them of at least a semblance of such an environment on the dissolution of the marriage is contrary to their best interests.

 

[48]            The trial judge overlooked the evidence of the negative impact which the move to Quebec had upon the children, particularly the eldest son. The trial judge overlooked evidence that all the children and particularly the eldest son had benefitted from the active involvement of the appellant as a parent both during the marriage and after the separation of the parties.

 

[49]           The trial judge rejected the recommendations of a carefully researched home study which recommended that the children be returned to Prince Edward Island where a shared parenting arrangement could be implemented. He also overlooked the wishes of the children, as conveyed by the writer of the home study, that they wanted a custodial arrangement where they could spend equal time with both parents.

 

[50]           The trial judge found that the cultural and linguistic heritage of the children had a role to play in their best interests when the evidence overwhelmingly established: (i) that the children were raised in a bilingual (French and English) environment, not a unilingual (French) environment; and (ii) that the children had little, if any exposure, throughout their lives to the culture and language of the Province of Quebec.  Accordingly, while the evidence clearly demonstrated that the cultural and linguistic heritage of the respondent may have played a role in her decision to move to Quebec, such factors had no role to play in determining the best interests of the children.

 

[51]           The decision of a custodial parent to live and work in the location of his or her choice is always to be respected, barring an improper motive which might indicate the decision is contrary to the best interests of the children.  This principle is to be considered and applied whether the application before the court is for an initial custody and access order or an application to vary an existing order.

 

[52]           Considering the best interests of the children, the respondent’s decision to move to Quebec and seek de facto custody of the children was made for an improper motive.  Her stated reason for doing so was to place distance between her and the appellant.  She also stated that she was moving to Quebec because, when the appellant decided to take up part-time residence with his then girlfriend in Dieppe, New Brunswick, she was left with no support in Prince Edward Island.  In the context of assessing the best interests of the children, the trial judge rationalized the respondent’s motives for moving the children away from their father on the basis that if respondent as the primary caregiver was happy, the children’s best interests would be satisfactorily addressed.  Not only does this conclusion overlook the preponderance of evidence as to the extent and quality of the appellant’s parenting but more importantly, it equates the best interests of one parent with those of the children.  The motive of the respondent to move should have been considered in the context of the best interests of the children and not in the context of the respondent’s motives and her best interests.  See: Falvai v. Falvai 2008 BCCA 503;  Nunweiler v. Nunweiler 2000 BCCA 300; (2000), 186 D.L.R. (4th) 323.

 

[53]           The respondent in taking up residence in Quebec was not relocating to an area where she had any more support for her and the children than she had in Prince Edward Island.  Indeed, the evidence is that she had little contact with her family over the years, and she appears to have had minimal contact with them since the move to Quebec. Furthermore, the respondent left a full-time tenured teaching position in Prince Edward Island with no prospect of similar employment in Quebec.  At the time of  trial, she had been employed part-time, and there is no evidence that full time employment was on the horizon.  Her decision to seek permanent de facto custody and move to the Province of Quebec does not promote  the economic best interests of the children.  While she now has a new partner who provides the respondent with support in caring for the children, her actual decision to move does reflect adversely on her perception of the needs of her children.  The purpose and the result of the move was to thwart contact with the appellant thereby, in the circumstances, showing a lack of appreciation for the benefits of the children having contact with their father.  See: Gordon v. Goertz  at para. 23.

 

[54]           Aside from their economic best interests, the children had significant support in the community in which they lived.  The support came from well-established relationships with both friends and relatives.  This support was similarly available to the respondent.

 

[55]           Determining the best interests of the children is primarily a fact finding exercise; however, the trial judge misapprehended the legal position of each parent and this constituted an error of law. As a result of this error of law, the trial judge viewed the best interests of the children from the wrong end of the telescope.  He assessed what was to be in their best interests as if the children were in Quebec in the permanent de facto custody of one parent rather than here in Prince Edward Island where both parents had an equal right to assert an initial claim for permanent custody.

 

[56]           The trial judge considered the best interests of the children as if the issue before him was a motion by the respondent seeking permission to relocate with the children from the Province of Quebec to the Province of Prince Edward Island.  To the contrary, the issue before the trial judge was the making of an initial permanent custody order and therefore, the best interests of the children should have been considered from the perspective of their being in the de facto custody of the respondent pursuant to the provisions of an interim custody order.  If he had considered the issue in this light, the trial judge would have considered the impact on the children of moving from their birthplace, their extended family, their life-long friends, schools and community activities in Prince Edward Island to Quebec.  The children were entitled to have their best interests assessed from the perspective of the abilities of two parents equally entitled to custody.  By reason of the trial judge’s error of law, the children were denied that opportunity.

 

[57]           In Gordon v. Goertz at para.49, the Court set forth the factors to be considered by a court in a mobility case where a parent with a permanent custody order decides to move his or her residence.  The particular factors to be considered are as follows:

 

7.             More particularly the judge should consider, inter alia:

 

(a)        the existing custody arrangement and relationship between the child and the custodial parent;

 

(b)        the existing access arrangement and the relationship between the child and the access parent;

 

(c)        the desirability of maximizing contact between the child and both parents;

 

(d)        the views of the child;

 

(e)        the custodial parent's reason for moving, only in the exceptional case where it is relevant to that parent's ability to meet the needs of the child;

 

(f)         disruption to the child of a change in custody;

 

(g)        disruption to the child consequent on removal from family, schools, and the community he or she has come to know.

 

[58]           The law is clear that to apply all these factors in an initial custody case where mobility is an issue, amounts to an error of law.  As Saunders J.A. pointed out in Nunweiler at para.28, the application of the above factors should be modified to an application of those which are particularly relevant to an initial custody application. Accordingly, factors (a) and (b) listed above do not apply in an initial application for permanent custody because there is no existing custody and access arrangement or order. The trial judge applied them, and this was an error of law.  Also see: Falvai v. Falvai at para.27.

 

[59]           The trial judge found the respondent to be the primary caregiver and on the basis of this finding, he gave “great respect” to her wishes to move to Quebec and start a new life. Implicitly, for purposes of his analysis, he considered the respondent to be the “custodial parent.”  There are two problems with this conclusion. 

 

[60]           First, it was a palpable and overriding error in the assessment of the evidence to conclude that during the marriage the respondent was the primary caregiver.  The appellant, as acknowledged by the trial judge in his reasons, was very involved in providing care to the children throughout the marriage.

 

[61]           Secondly, the trial judge used the period of time after separation when the children resided with the respondent to support his conclusion she was the primary caregiver. In doing so, the trial judge overlooked evidence that even though the appellant was not living in the home where the children resided, he was very much involved in their lives.  The children had daily access to their father for a period of time after the separation.  The appellant also exercised regular weekend access which was unilaterally altered from time to time by the respondent, most notably after she caused the petition for divorce to be issued and after she had decided to move to Quebec. 

 

[62]           The trial judge also used the period of time the children lived in Quebec with the respondent pursuant to the provisions of two interim custody orders as creating a status quo custody and access arrangement. This, in turn, caused him to place undue emphasis on the children’s circumstances resulting from the interim orders.

 

[63]           In considering initial custody applications, it constitutes an error of law to rely upon an interim custody order as creating a status quo situation, and then in adjudicating upon the merits of an application for a permanent order, weigh the circumstances of that status quo against circumstances which might give reason to alter it. Were this to be the approach, the status quo, absent exceptional circumstances, will almost always prevail.  More importantly, from a practical perspective such an approach will discourage parents from agreeing to interim custodial and access arrangements (as they did in this case, with court assisted mediation, prior to the interim orders) pending the determination of a permanent order.  Furthermore, it will discourage parents from making applications for interim custody and access orders.

 

[64]           In conclusion, I would allow the appeal and set aside the order of the trial judge.

 

[65]           This court has the jurisdiction to make any order the trial judge could have made, order a new trial or make any order considered just.  See: s.21(2)(a), Judicature Act, R.S.P.E.I. 1988 Cap. J-2.1.

 

[66]           In the present circumstances of this case where custody and access are in issue and applications for variation are a remedy should circumstances change materially, I have decided not to order a new trial in the interests of bringing some finality for the children.  It would be, therefore, in the best interests of the children for this court to make an order the trial judge could have made. 

 

[67]           Again, in the circumstances of this case the evidence is such that an appellate court is in as good a position as the trial judge to make the order. The evidence establishes on a balance of probabilities that it would be in the best interests of the children that a custody and access arrangement be put in place that would maximize, to the fullest extent possible, contact with both parents. Alternatively, the evidence establishes on a balance of probabilities that, presently, the children’s best interests are served by their being in Prince Edward Island.

 

[68]           I would, therefore, order that the appellant and the respondent have joint custody and shared parenting of the three children of the marriage and that the children reside in Prince Edward Island.  The shared parenting arrangement shall be as agreed upon between the parties and failing their agreement, it shall be determined by the Supreme Court.  Alternatively, if the respondent decides not to resume her residence and employment in Prince Edward Island so as to facilitate a shared parenting arrangement, the appellant and the respondent shall have joint custody of the children residing in the day-to-day care of the appellant in Prince Edward Island, with reasonable access to the respondent as agreed between the parties.  Failing an agreement, the terms of the respondent’s access shall be determined by the Supreme Court.
