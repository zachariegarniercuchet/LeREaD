REASONS OF JUSTICE GENDREAU

______________________________________________________________________

The Facts and the Judgment on Appeal

[7]    On February 27, 2003, a news report was published denouncing the policy of certain generic drug manufacturers that were providing pharmacists with premiums, discounts, rebates and other benefits.[1] Believing that the practice was unlawful and that it had the effect of raising the price of drugs sold to the Régie de l’assurance-maladie while simultaneously increasing the obligatory financial contribution of the users of the prescription drug insurance plan, the respondents (Option Consommateurs) named the appellants Pharmascience Inc.[2] as defendants in a class action. On behalf of all purchasers of these manufacturers’ products since January 1, 1995, they claimed damages equal to the value of the reduction in premiums, deductibles and co-insurance they would have enjoyed if the drug prices had excluded the allegedly unlawful benefits received by the pharmacists.

[8]               In response to the motion brought by Option Consommateurs, Pharmascience and the other defendant pharmaceutical corporations raised a number of preliminary exceptions, including one seeking a declaration of the constitutional invalidity of article 1002 C.C.P. They claimed that a recent amendment to this provision deprived them of their right to a full defence at the authorization stage of the action. Justice Carole Julien of the Superior Court dismissed these arguments and affirmed that the defendants were not deprived of any fundamental rights at this stage. She expressed herself in the following terms:

[translation]

[49] The respondents’ main argument is based on the premise that they have been deprived of their right to a full and complete defence in the discussion of the applicable criteria at the authorization stage.

[50] This premise is, however, erroneous. They are not deprived of any fundamental right at the preliminary stage, since they retain the freedom to offer a defence and to present evidence relevant to the hearing on the motion for authorization as set out in the present judgment.

…

[53] There is no fundamental right to a preliminary ruling on the grounds put forward.

[54] Moreover, the defendants retain all of their grounds, including those relating to the constitutional challenge in the context of the hearing on the motion for authorization.[3]

[9]    The judge nevertheless reserved the right of the drug manufacturers a) to submit their constitutional arguments again during the hearing on the motion for permission to institute the action and b) to seek permission to adduce evidence to counter the motion of Option Consommateurs.[4]

[10]           The judgment was not appealed. Two months after it was handed down, Pharmascience and the other defendant pharmaceutical companies resumed their constitutional challenge of article 1002 C.C.P. They also launched a separate attack in a  second motion, arguing that the Superior Court lacked the jurisdiction ratione materiae to rule on the class action, since the action Option Consommateurs had in mind would require the judge to set the price of the prescription drugs and the cost of the premiums, co-insurance and deductibles, matters properly falling to the Minister of Health or the National Assembly. The defendants maintained that the judge should rule on their claims at the outset. In addition, all of the manufacturers involved, with the exception of Pharmascience, sought permission to adduce evidence.

[11]           The judge refused to rule immediately on the constitutional issue. In her opinion, the debate on the validity of article 1002 C.C.P. did not put the jurisdiction ratione materiae of the Superior Court in issue. In addition, she concluded that even in the event of a declaration of invalidity, the class action procedure as a whole would not be struck out from Quebec law, but that only certain elements or aspects of the procedure would be eliminated.

[12]           Furthermore, with respect to the second motion impugning the jurisdiction of the Superior Court, she questioned the appellant’s characterization of the motion brought by Option Consommateurs. She perceived no risk that the Court seized of the merits would appropriate the role of the Minister. In her view, the procedure was nothing more than an action in damages.

[13]           Finally, she granted leave to the manufacturers who had sought permission to examine the applicants Gendron and Piro.

[14]           Dissatisfied, Pharmascience sought and obtained leave to appeal from the ruling on the two motions.

Grounds of Appeal

[15]           First, the appellant Pharmascience once again raises the arguments regarding the constitutional validity of the amendment to article 1002 C.C.P. Second, it maintains that the class action violates the principle of the separation of powers by seeking to confer on the Superior Court a role and jurisdiction in matters that properly fall to the executive or legislative branch. The respondents and the enjoined Attorney General, for their part, argue that the appellant’s two grounds in no way put in issue the jurisdiction ratione materiae of the Superior Court.

 

1>               Does the legislative amendment to article 1002 C.C.P violate section 23 of the Charter of human rights and freedoms?

[16]           The appellant’s position is based on the following syllogism. Instituting a class action requires the applicant to obtain judicial authorization, which requires a judicial decision whereby a judge recognizes that the motion meets the mandatory conditions set out in article 1003 C.C.P. Furthermore, the fundamental right to a hearing before an independent and impartial tribunal, set out in section 23 of the Charter of human rights and freedoms[5] (hereinafter the Charter), includes an obligation on the part of all applicants to establish the facts underlying the exercise of a private or civil recourse against a third party and, inversely, the fundamental right to demand evidence supporting such facts from the third party before a defence is produced. These are the two major premises presented by Pharmascience. The minor premise can be stated as follows: the amendment to article 1002 C.C.P. absolved the applicant from the obligation to prove the facts supporting its legal claims by removing the requirement of supporting the motion with an affidavit. The appellant therefore concludes that the amendment to article 1002 C.C.P. violates article 23 of the Charter. It adds that the proviso authorizing the court to admit relevant evidence cannot save the provision. This reasoning leads Pharmascience to arrive at the following conclusions:

a)     article 1002 C.C.P. must be declared invalid. This would in turn render article 1003 inapplicable and the Quebec class action regime inoperable;

b)     the action of the applicants, respondents on appeal, must be dismissed.

[17]           In my view, this reasoning is erroneous. For one, it confuses the nature and purpose of the motion for authorization of the institution of the class action and the consequent ruling with the action proper and the judgment on its merits. In addition, it misapprehends the scope of both section 23 of the Charter and the amendment to article 1002 C.C.P.

[18]           There is an age-old rule whereby “a person cannot use the name of another to plead, except the Crown through its recognized officers.”[6] The legislator, however, has gradually relaxed this principle. First, it was recognized that an association without “collective civil personality” could be collectively summoned[7] and that a legally recognized union could plead before the courts.[8] Later, with the enactment of the current Code of Civil Procedure, several persons with common interests were authorized to mandate one of their members to act as plaintiff on behalf of all of them.[9]

[19]           The introduction of the class action in 1978 marked another stage in the progressive easing of restrictions. The Code of Civil Procedure defines it as follows:

[T]he procedure which enables one member to sue without a mandate on behalf of all the members.[10]

[Emphasis added]

[20]           This is not an exceptional scheme. It embodies a social policy that encourages access to justice by enabling fair and comparable compensation for all members while avoiding a multiplicity of similar actions, in a manner that ensures an equal distribution of power between the parties.[11]

[21]           From this standpoint, the objective of the Code is to define the role of the representative at the outset and protect the rights of those who are absent. To this purpose, the applicant is required to obtain prior authorization to initiate the action from the Superior Court, the only competent jurisdiction in this matter.[12] To do so, the applicant must meet the criteria set out in article 1003 C.C.P., which reads as follows:

Art. 1003.  The court authorizes the bringing of the class action and ascribes the status of representative to the member it designates if of opinion that:

 (a) the recourses of the members raise identical, similar or related questions of law or fact;

 (b) the facts alleged seem to justify the conclusions sought;

 (c) the composition of the group makes the application of article 59 or 67 difficult or impracticable; and

 (d) the member to whom the court intends to ascribe the status of representative is in a position to represent the members adequately.

 

[22]           Herein lies the debate before the Court. Pharmascience argues that the amendment to article 1002 C.C.P gravely weakens the role of the Superior Court and violates the principle whereby the claimant is required to prove the facts supporting its legal claims. The two versions of the provision follow:

 

 Former provision

Amended provision

A member cannot institute a class action except with the prior authorization of the court, obtained on a motion.

 

The motion states the facts giving rise thereto, indicates the nature of the recourses for which authorization is applied for, and describes the group on behalf of which the member intends to act; the allegations of the motion are supported by an affidavit. It is accompanied with a notice of at least 10 days of the date of presentation and is served on the person against whom the applicant intends to exercise the class action.

 

A member cannot institute a class action except with the prior authorization of the court, obtained on a motion.

 

The motion states the facts giving rise thereto, indicates the nature of the recourses for which authorization is applied for, and describes the group on behalf of which the member intends to act. It is accompanied with a notice of at least 10 days of the date of presentation and is served on the person against whom the applicant intends to exercise the class action; the motion may only be contested orally and the judge may allow relevant evidence to be submitted.

 

 

[23]           First, it is apposite to recall the nature and scope of the motion for authorization and the judgment ruling on such motion.

[24]           In Thompson v. Masson,[13] Justice LeBel (then of our Court) defined the motion for permission to initiate a class action as [translation] “a filtering and verification mechanism”[14] and described the judicial decision ruling on such a motion as one of [translation] “verification and control”,[15] which, if favourable, [translation] “permits the bringing and exercise of the action” according to the usual rules (art. 1011 C.C.P.). Justice LeBel continued: [translation] “Before the judgment is rendered, the action does not exist, at least not on a collective basis.”[16] These conclusions have been reiterated on a number of occasions.[17] They have served, inter alia, as the basis for a judgment that concluded that all preliminary or incidental measures or demands must be pleaded and ruled on during the hearing on the motion for authorization, save in exceptional circumstances or if the jurisdiction ratione materiae of the Superior Court is challenged.[18] Similarly, this Court relied on Thomson when it ruled that the absence of right of appeal extended to interlocutory judgments rendered before the judgment authorizing institution of the action,[19] save, once again, in exceptional circumstances.[20]

[25]           The proceedings brought once the action is authorized should therefore not be confused with the proceedings seeking this authorization. The two have opposing objects and purposes. In the first, the court must rule on the actual merits of the case, and therefore all legally prescribed rules of procedure and evidence must be applied.  In the second, the judge merely verifies whether the conditions stipulated in article 1003 C.C.P are met, that is, the capacity of the representative, the similarity or nexus between the questions of fact or law, and the legal relationship between the allegations and the conclusion sought. The second type of proceeding imposes a burden of demonstration, not proof.

[26]           Before the amendment to article 1002 C.C.P., the applicant was required to support the motion with an affidavit, which opened the door to the examination of the deponent, pursuant to article 93 C.C.P. According to Pharmascience, eliminating the obligation to provide a sworn statement constitutes a violation of the principle requiring  proof of the facts supporting the demand.

[27]           We should first recall that the goal of this legislative amendment was to correct a certain tendency that had developed in authorization proceedings. The Civil Procedure Review Committee described it thus:

[translation]

Contrary to the initial objective, the parties too often argue the merits at this stage, and this is not without consequence on costs and wait times. In a way. current practice has transformed the original concept of authorization proceedings as developed in 1978 into a post-contestation certification procedure, like that which prevails in the legislative schemes of Ontario, British Columbia and the United States.[21]

[Emphasis added]

[28]           Contrary to what has been argued, therefore, the Review Committee’s recommendation to the Minister of Justice—and ultimately, to the legislator—was not to divest the judge of the role of verifier and to release the applicant from the obligation of meeting the criteria in 1003 C.C.P. Rather, the Committee recommended returning to a practice that complied more fully with the provision, which requires the judge to verify whether “the facts alleged seem to justify the conclusions sought”, or in French, “les faits allégués paraissent justifier les conclusions recherchées.” In effect, since the introduction of the class action, the case law of the Supreme Court of Canada has affirmed that the judge need only ensure that there was a good colour of right in light of the facts alleged, without making a determination on the merits of the action.[22] In Comité de l’environnement de la Baie Inc. v. Société d’électrolyse et de chimie Alcan ltée,[23] where over 2000 citizens sued a corporation in damages for alleged industrial air pollution, Justice Rothman wrote the following:

Vague as appellant's allegations may be, however, they do assert that the damage has been caused to the residents in question by air pollution emanating from respondent's port operations and they do allege respondent's fault and negligence. At this stage of the proceedings, I believe this is sufficient to satisfy the requirement of Article 1003(b) that the facts alleged seem to justify the authorization of a class action.[24]

[Emphasis added]

[29]           In summary, at this stage the judge should analyze the legal argument in light of the alleged facts, accepting the facts as though proven.  This is in fact why the Supreme Court of Canada rejected any connection between article 1003(b) C.C.P. and article 867 C.C.P. (now repealed[25]), which required authorization for a writ of evocation, because of a basic difference between the provisions.[26] The only requirement that the case law has recognized as analogous to article 1003 (b) C.C.P. is colour of right as a criterion for an interlocutory injunction.[27] We should specify that, contrary to what has been argued, this comparison does not establish a link between a ruling authorizing a class action and an order for interlocutory injunction, but merely highlights the similarity of the condition known as colour of right in both cases.

[30]           The judge, whose task it is to filter and verify, must therefore allow the motion and authorize the action if the allegations of fact seem to justify the claim. In all cases, there is no need for evidence. Consequently, the claim that the applicant must submit to a type of preliminary investigation on the merits does not comply with the requirements of the Code of Civil Procedure as interpreted in the case law. Consequently, removing the obligation to produce an affidavit and limiting examinations to those that have received judicial authorization results in a speedier and more flexible procedure without fundamentally modifying the Quebec class action regime. There is also no weakening in the role of the judge; not only must he still be satisfied that there is a good colour of right in addition to the other conditions in article 1003 C.C.P., the law also confers on him the discretion to admit relevant and appropriate evidence in the context of the class action authorization proceedings. Finally, the amendment to article 1002 C.C.P. fits perfectly within the new legislative regime created by the reform of the Code of Civil Procedure, which has increased the courts’ powers of intervention in case management in order to guide the dispute toward the essential stages of inquiry and the hearing on the merits.[28]

[31]           That being said, in the authorization of the class action, the substantive rights of the defendant are neither alienated nor withdrawn; any legal means are available to counter the claims of the party applying for authorization. Admittedly, as the appellant has argued, citing the opinion of a Florida court, being sued can cause financial difficulties, particularly if the claim is substantial. However, this has no relevance in Quebec law. The legislator has imposed criteria in order to prevent frivolous actions, not to enable a ruling on the appropriateness of the action.[29] In this way, the authorization procedure provides additional protection to the party against whom the action is intended since, contrary to the usual rule, he cannot be sued without prior judicial authorization.

[32]           In New York Life v. Vaughan,[30] this Court cited these rules when dismissing a constitutional challenge of article 1010 C.C.P., which limits the right of appeal to cases where the motion for authorization of the class action is dismissed. The Court stated the following:

[translation]

WHEREAS the judgment authorizing the class action is merely an interim judgment and in no way a decision on the merits of the dispute to be heard or on the rights of the parties;

WHEREAS this judgment is merely procedural and is in no way a judgment on the defences the respondents may invoke;

WHEREAS the mere fact of having been named as a defendant in a class action suit cannot constitute any prejudice whatsoever in and of itself;

WHEREAS in law, there is no right of appeal in the absence of a specific legislative provision and the conferral of this right is a function of Parliamentary supremacy; 

WHEREAS in certain cases and for the public interest, Parliament limits this right to only one of the parties;

WHEREAS in civil matters, this is so, for example, in a judgment that upholds an objection to evidence, where only this ruling is subject to appeal;

WHEREAS in criminal matters, this is so, for example, where in certain cases the Crown may appeal only questions of law;

WHEREAS in the present case, the submission calling on foreign law as a comparison cannot be supported, as the legislator has deliberately limited the right of appeal in order to avoid a multiplicity of proceedings, a slowing of the decision-making process, and a basis in law for the costs of justice;

WHEREAS this was a decision of internal public order.[31]

[33]           Pharmascience pleads that the amendment of article 1002 C.C.P. violated section 23 of the Charter. This provision and the one following are found in the title on judicial guarantees. They read as follows:

23. Impartial hearing before independent tribunal. Every person has a right to a full and equal, public and fair hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal, for the determination of his rights and obligations or of the merits of any charge brought against him.

Sittings in camera. The tribunal may decide to sit in camera, however, in the interests of morality or public order.

24. Grounds for deprivation of liberty. No one may be deprived of his liberty or of his rights except on grounds provided by law and in accordance with prescribed procedure.

[34]           The impartial hearing in section 23 of the Charter is concerned with procedural guarantees, commonly referred to as rules of natural justice.[32] As stated by Justice La Forest in R. v. Lyons, however, “the requirements of fundamental justice…vary according to the context in which they are invoked.”[33]

[35]           In the present case, the hearing is not concerned with determining the rights and obligations of Pharmascience, since the action is not yet brought. Rather, its purpose is to confer on a person a mandate to represent a group and to ensure that the legal claims logically flow from the facts alleged in the motion. This control, exercised in a public hearing, takes place after contestation, which, although oral, is real, vigorous and unconstrained. In this respect, nothing prohibits either party, and particularly the respondent, from seeking permission to adduce evidence by attempting to convince the judge that it is appropriate. In civil matters the rules regarding burden of proof are statutorily defined, in contrast to criminal matters, where some of these rules, such as the right to silence and the right to be proved guilty beyond a reasonable doubt, are constitutionally entrenched.

[36]           In Crane Canada Inc. v. Sécurité nationale, compagnie d’assurances and Attorney General of Quebec,[34] this Court recognized the validity of an amendment to the Code of Civil Procedure eliminating examination on discovery in matters involving less than $25,000. Madam Justice Rousseau-Houle, writing for the Court, stated the following:

[translation]

Article 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure establishes the principle of the public nature of hearings. Article 23 of the Quebec Charter constitutionalizes, in both penal and civil matters, the right to natural justice, the constituents of which are: 1) the right to a decision rendered by an independent tribunal and 2) the right to be heard. This article provides certain procedural guarantees, but it does not create a substantive right to a particular recourse. In principle, as Professors Brun and Tremblay have noted, article 23 adds nothing to judicial and administrative law besides the possibility of contesting explicit legislative standards.

Article 396.1 C.C.P., which limits examination on discovery to litigations involving $25,000 and more, does not infringe article 23 of the Charter.

In effect, only the right to be heard is at issue here. As the examination on discovery is part of neither the judicial record nor the trial, the appellant cannot allege that article 396.1 C.C.P. deprives him of a public hearing of his case.

When his case is heard, the appellant will have the right to examine his own witnesses (art. 294 C.C.P) and to cross-examine the plaintiff’s witnesses (art. 314 C.C.P.), pursuant to his right to be heard. The court of first instance will determine its rights and obligations within the meaning of the Charter following the debate on the merits.[35]

[Emphasis added]

 

[37]           These statements are fully applicable in the presence case since the motion for authorization is neither the trial itself nor a part of the trial; it is nothing more than a prerequisite to the bringing of the action.

[38]           In conclusion, the motion for authorization of the class action is a procedural mechanism that gives rise to a judgment confirming, if appropriate, that the position of the representative applicant is adequate to permit the bringing of the class action, which is then instituted, heard and ruled upon in the usual manner. Although applicants are not required to support their proceedings with an affidavit, they must nevertheless show that the criteria set out in article 1003 C.C.P. have been satisfied. To this purpose, the judge hearing the motion may, at the request of a party, order that relevant evidence be adduced.

[39]           Contrary to the rule in other jurisdictions, therefore, the superintending function of the judge is exercised before the action is instituted and is carried out in order to authorize the action. This is done by verifying whether the facts alleged, taken as proved, seem to justify the right claimed. The recent amendments to article 1002 C.C.P. change nothing with respect to the judge’s task as defined in the case law. Rather, they express a new approach to civil procedure, which confers on the judge a more significant role in the management of the case as it moves toward the trial, the stage at which the parties are required to prove facts and establish legal claims. This new approach has been described as a new judicial culture. In addition, the amendments do not violate section 23 of the Quebec Charter: on the one hand, they allow defendants to contest at the preliminary authorization stage while simultaneously preventing them from raising a defence on the merits; on the other, they leave unchanged and applicable all the rules of civil procedure after the bringing of the action and until the final ruling.

[40]           The appellant’s first motion should therefore be dismissed.

 

2>               Does the Superior Court have jurisdicition ratione materiae to hear the action of Option Consommateurs?

[41]           To better understand the action of Option Consommateurs and the objection raised by Pharmascience, a brief explanation of the legislative framework is required.

[42]           Quebec benefits from a general prescription drug insurance plan. For some, such as the elderly and the economically disadvantaged, the cost of the system is borne entirely by the State. Others are required to join the public plan unless they are already covered under a provincial or federal law or under a private plan. Such coverage must be at least equal to that offered by the public plan.

[43]           There are three ways in which beneficiaries contribute to the plan: an annual premium, a deductible, and co-insurance. The first two are fixed in the act and divided into monthly contributions, while the latter represents 27.4% of the cost of services required and obtained. In all cases, the law sets a cap on the beneficiary’s annual contribution and the State pays the surplus, if applicable.

[44]           The Minister of Health, on the recommendation of the Conseil du médicament [Medication Council], creates the list of prescription drugs whose costs are covered by the general plan.

[45]           To be included on the list, a prescription drug must be manufactured or sold by a manufacturer or wholesaler recognized by the Minister, unless the drug is unique and essential. To be recognized, the manufacturer or wholesaler must undertake to respect certain conditions established by regulation.[36] It sets the price of its drugs in accordance with the terms of its commitment, which include the explicit requirement to exclude from the price “any reduction awarded…in the form of a rebate, discount or premium, or any other property granted to a buyer by the manufacturer without consideration”.[37]

[46]           The selling price of a drug on the list is the price paid by the pharmacist. This is the price paid by the Régie de l’assurance-maladie, and it is the basis on which the financial contribution of the insured is calculated.[38]

[47]           The rule is different for certain generic products manufactured by at least two different manufacturers and included on the list for more than 15 years. The price paid by the Régie is the lowest price of those submitted by the manufacturers for equivalent products. If the price of the drug required by the insured exceeds the “lowest price”, the Régie pays the amount requested by the manufacturer only if the prescription prohibits substitution with an equivalent generic drug. If the insured refuses the substitution, he or she must pay the difference between the price of the drug prescribed and the price of the most inexpensive generic drug available.[39]

[48]           In summary, the plan provides the following:

·  a basic contributory plan for most beneficiaries, save for certain groups of beneficiaries;

·    a list of approved prescription drugs and their prices. The prices are established by the manufacturers and wholesalers and must exclude “any reduction awarded…in the form of a rebate, discount or premium, or any other property granted…without consideration”. The price is submitted to the Minister, who approves it after consultation with the Conseil du médicament;

·  the price is the amount paid by the pharmacist, who in turn requests it from the beneficiary, who is ultimately reimbursed by the Régie or the private insurer.

[49]           Option Consommateurs raises the following argument: the corporations have violated the regulation by including all manner of rebate in their suggested prices without revealing this fact. This unlawful practice increased the cost of the prescription drugs. The additional cost to the Régie had two consequences: first, the beneficiaries paid a higher premium and deductible and second, they paid a 24.7% co-insurance on the part of the price that reimbursed the unlawful benefits.

[50]           For its part, Pharmascience pleads that the Superior Court lacks the jurisdiction ratione materiae to hear this action on the ground that the resolution of such a dispute would require the court to determine the fair price of the medications, a matter entrusted by law to the discretion of the Minister of Health.

[51]           This argument is unsound, at least as the record now stands. According to the allegations in the motion, the manufacturers included prohibited costs in their prices. Determining the value of these costs does not require arbitration on the basis of political considerations nor put in issue the appropriateness of a ministerial or governmental discretionary decision. On the contrary, it involves qualifying and evaluating the benefits granted to pharmacists in light of the specific wording of the Regulation, defining the resulting prejudice to the beneficiaries, and determining the causal link.

[52]           Although the legal argument described in the proceedings is easily stated, there is still a marked want of proof behind the allegations in the motion for authorization. At this stage, however, the apparent complexity of the case is irrelevant under Quebec’s Act respecting the class action.[40] It does not fall to the judge hearing the motion for authorization to assess the risks and pitfalls faced by the applicant. Indeed, even if the judge did find that some of the claims were without merit, she would not be authorized to immediately exclude them from the debate because the motion for partial dismissal has been struck out of the Code of Civil Procedure.

[53]           In short, the facts alleged in the motion “seem to justify” the damages sought. The Superior Court is not called on to set the guaranteed prices of the prescription drugs offered; rather, its task is to determine whether the evidence shows that this price includes the value of elements prohibited by law. This exercise will not change the price set by the Minister, but merely define one of the factors by which to assess the damage.

[54]           I  would therefore dismiss the second motion of Pharmascience.

 

3>               Remarks on certain aspects of the respondents’ pleadings

[55]           The Attorney General and Option Consommateurs have argued that, despite the granting of leave to appeal, this Court should not have heard the dispute. They claim that the grounds raised by Pharmascience do not put the jurisdiction ratione materiae of the Superior Court in issue.

[56]           Ultimately, a judicial declaration of the constitutional invalidity of article 1002 C.C.P. could have caused a complete paralysis of class actions. It could also have resulted in an absence of jurisdiction, in the broad sense, of the Superior Court to rule on such questions. But there is more.

[57]           In the present case, this Court has been placed in an exceptional position. Whether or not it recognizes the validity of article 1002 C.C.P., the judgment will influence the conduct of hearings on motions for authorization. The Court must therefore dispose of the question without delay. The urgency is further compounded by the fact that a large number of class actions are presently suspended pending this Court’s ruling, with the number of cases increasing as each new motion is filed. In such a situation, public interest demands that the Court rule on the appeal at once, and the Attorney General and Option Consommateurs agree, despite their reservations.

[58]           It could be argued that the claim regarding the Superior Court’s jurisdiction ratione materiae to hear the dispute, based on the ground that the action would confer on the courts a role that properly falls within the discretion of the Minister or the National Assembly, fails to meet the same criteria as the constitutional challenge. I do not disagree. Nevertheless, given the present situation, it would be unjust to send the case back to the Superior Court. Here again, the Attorney General and Option Consommateurs agree that there must be a ruling on all the issues.

[59]           In short, the situation in the present case is unique and should remain so. It does not constitute a precedent.

 

Conclusion

[60]           I would declare valid articles 1002 and 1003 C.C.P. and therefore dismiss the appeal with costs.
