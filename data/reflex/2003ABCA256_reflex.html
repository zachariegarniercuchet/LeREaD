I.          INTRODUCTION

[1]               Alberta, like many other provinces in Canada (British Columbia, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island and Nova Scotia), has introduced an administrative licence suspension program for motor vehicle operators involved in drinking and driving. Essentially, the program provides for a 90 day licence suspension by a peace officer who has reasonable and probable grounds to believe that the person drove a motor vehicle while his ability to do so was impaired by alcohol. The appellants ask this Court to declare the program unconstitutional.

 

II.        FACTS

 

[2]               On April 6, 2001, Thomson was stopped at a Check Stop by the Calgary police and charged with impaired driving and operating a motor vehicle with more than 80 milligrams of alcohol in 100 millilitres of blood. He was served with a notice of suspension which effected a 90 day driver’s licence suspension starting 21 days after the issuance of the Notice. He filed an originating notice seeking a declaration that the Administrative Licence Suspension (“ALS”) program was invalid, illegal and of no force or effect and sought judicial review of the legal validity of the ALS program. His suspension was stayed pending final determination of the judicial review.

 

[3]               On May 10 and 11, and August 16, 2001, Slatter J. heard 270 applications challenging the constitutional validity of the ALS program found in the Traffic Safety Act, S.A. 1999, c. T‑6.4 (“Act”). Three of the applications were treated as representative cases and in a written judgment, Gonzalez et al. v. Alberta (Driver Control Board), 2001 ABQB 757 (CanLII), [2001] 11 W.W.R. 628, the chambers judge found the program constitutional. Most of the 270 applicants appealed to this Court. Ten of those appeals, involving approximately 190 individuals, were consolidated under one style of cause. Pending this appeal, trial courts have stayed approximately 1,000 licence suspensions issued under the legislation. None of the named applicants in Gonzalez have appealed, but as these are effectively representative cases, the appellants here rely on certain facts giving rise to allegations that ss. 8 and 10(b) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms were breached. One individual brought an application to the Court of Queen’s Bench as a court of competent jurisdiction under s. 24(1) of the Charter and bypassed an appeal of his suspension to the Board.

 

[4]               The appellants also ask this Court to reconsider two previous decisions which held that driving was not a liberty interest protected by s. 7 of the Charter.

 

 

 

III.       THE LEGISLATION

 

[5]               The Traffic Safety Act is a comprehensive legislative scheme covering virtually all aspects of the regulation of highways and motor vehicles in Alberta.  The Act was fully proclaimed in force on May 20, 2003, after the hearing of this appeal.  The Act replaces and consolidates the Highway Traffic Act, the Motor Vehicle Administration Act, the Motor Transport Act and other related legislation, and merges the Alberta Motor Transport Board and the Driver Control Board, continuing them as the Alberta Transportation Safety Board.

 

[6]               A general description of the Act follows:

 

Part 1 - General administration - establishes the Registrar of Motor Vehicle Services, empowers municipalities to pass bylaws and the Lieutenant Governor in Council to make regulations.

 

Part 2 - Alberta Transportation Safety Board - establishes the right to appeal to the Board on matters related to driver conduct including the cancellation or suspension of various licences and permits. It also provides for further appeal to the courts.

 

Part 3 - Motor Vehicle Administration - provides for the licensing and registration of drivers and vehicles, establishes equipment standards for vehicles, and other provisions relating to vehicle safety.

 

Part 4 - Disqualification from Driving- creates two divisions for disqualifications from either offences or judgments. The administrative licence suspension is in Part 4.

 

Part 5 - General Operation of Vehicles - provides for establishment of speed limits, traffic control devices, and the observance of rules of the road.

 

Part 6 - Off-highway Vehicles.

 

Part 7- Commercial Motor Transport - provides for the operation of commercial vehicles generally and with safety fitness certificates for commercial vehicle.

 

Part 8 - Enforcement, Rights, Remedies and Obligations - provides for motor vehicle immobilization or seizure.

 

Part 9 - Transitional Provisions and Consequential Amendments.

 

The Schedule to the Act.

 

[7]               The applications before the Court of Queen’s Bench and at the hearing of this appeal relied on the provisions in the Schedule which were then in force. The provisions of the Schedule are for all relevant purposes identical with s. 88 of the Act. With the proclamation of the remaining provisions of the Act, the only significant difference is that ALS appeals are no longer heard by the Driver Control Board but by the Alberta Transportation Safety Board. In this judgment, we refer to the corresponding identical provisions in the Traffic Safety Act, and refer to the Board whether that be the Alberta Transportation Safety Board or the previous Driver Control Board.

 

IV.       ADMINISTRATIVE LICENCE SUSPENSIONS

 

[8]               The operation of the program is triggered when a peace officer reasonably suspects an individual of “alcohol-related driving”. This is defined as driving with more than 80 milligrams of alcohol in 100 millilitres of blood, or refusing to provide a breath sample when demanded. Where a peace officer has reasonable and probable grounds to believe a person drove a motor vehicle while under the influence of alcohol, or refuses, without reasonable excuse, to provide a breath sample, the officer is required to take possession of the driver's licence on behalf of the Registrar of Motor Vehicle Services and issue a temporary licence valid for 21 days. Thereafter, an automatic three-month suspension of the licence occurs under s. 88(5), or where bodily harm or death has resulted, a six-month suspension under s. 88(6). This transitional period is designed to afford the driver an opportunity to make alternate transportation arrangements pending his suspension and, if he chooses, to launch an appeal to the Board.

 

[9]               The Board is required to consider a wide range of evidence and information on the appeal, and if satisfied that alcohol-related driving did take place, the Board must confirm the suspension. If satisfied there was no alcohol-related driving, the suspension is cancelled.

 

[10]           Subsections relevant to this appeal include:

 

88(1)(a) “alcohol-related driving of a motor vehicle” means those circumstances referred to in subsection (2)(a) and (b) of this Schedule; ...

 

   (2)     Where

(a)        a peace officer has reasonable and probable grounds to believe that a person drove a motor vehicle, and

(b)        in relation to that person driving that motor vehicle, the peace officer,

 

(i)         by reason of an analysis of the breath or blood of the person, has reasonable and probable grounds to believe that the person has consumed alcohol in such a quantity that the concentration of alcohol in that person's blood exceeds 80 milligrams of alcohol in 100 millilitres of blood, or

(ii)        has reasonable and probable grounds to believe that the person while having alcohol in that person's blood failed or refused, without a reasonable excuse, to comply with a demand made on that person to supply a sample of that person's breath or blood under section 254 of the Criminal Code (Canada),

 

the peace officer shall, on behalf of the Registrar,

 

(c)        in the case of a person who holds an operator's licence,

 

(i)         require that person to surrender to the peace officer that operator's licence and issue to that person a temporary operator's permit, and

 

(ii)        serve on that person a notice of suspension of that person's operator's licence. . . .

 

[11]           If an appeal is launched, the Board must consider certain information as required by the following subsections:

 

39(3)  In an appeal under this section, the Board shall consider

(a)        any relevant sworn or solemnly affirmed statements and any other relevant information;

(b)        the report of the peace officer;

(c)        a copy of any certificate of analysis under section 258 of the Criminal Code (Canada) without proof of the identity and official character of the person appearing to have signed the certificate or that the copy is a true copy;

(d)        where an oral hearing is held, in addition to the matters referred to in clauses (a), (b) and (c), any relevant evidence and information given or presentations made at the hearing.

 

(4)  An appellant is not compelled to give evidence in an appeal under this section.

 

(5)  If, after conducting an appeal under this section, the Board is satisfied that

(a)        the person having consumed alcohol in such a quantity that the concentration of alcohol in that person's blood exceeded 80 milligrams of alcohol in 100 millilitres of blood, drove a motor vehicle, or

(b)        the person, with respect to the driving of a motor vehicle, failed or refused, without a reasonable excuse, to comply with a demand made on that person to supply a sample of that person's breath or blood under section 254 of the Criminal Code (Canada),

 

the Board must confirm the suspension or disqualification.

 

(6)  If, after conducting an appeal under this section, the Board is satisfied

(a)        that the person did not drive a motor vehicle having consumed alcohol in such a quantity that the concentration of alcohol in that person's blood exceeded 80 milligrams of alcohol in 100 millilitres of blood, or

(b)        that, with respect to the driving of a motor vehicle,

(i)         the person did not fail or refuse to comply with a demand made on that person to supply a sample of that person's breath or blood under section 254 of the Criminal Code (Canada), or

(ii)        the person had a reasonable excuse for failing or refusing to comply with the demand referred to in subclause (i),

 

the Board must cancel the suspension or disqualification and direct the return to that person of any fees paid to the Government by that person in respect of the appeal conducted under this section.

 

[12]           In certain respects, the Act closely parallels the Criminal Code provisions on impaired driving. It adopts the Code standard of blood alcohol concentration and the method for obtaining a breath sample, and refers to the reasonable and probable belief of alcohol-related driving.

 

[13]           However, important differences also exist. The Code provides that two breath samples must be taken as soon as practicable after the driving, not later than two hours and within at least a 15 minutes interval. The Code presumes the breath test accurately portrays the alcohol concentration at the time of driving and where there are two readings, the lowest will be considered accurate. The requirement for two breath samples and the timing thereof are not found in the Act. The Code, being criminal law, requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, the standard of proof is the civil standard, on a balance of probabilities.

 

V.        ISSUES

 

1.         Should this Court reconsider its decisions in R. v. Neale (1986), 1986 ABCA 169 (CanLII), 28 C.C.C. (3d) 345 and Yehia v. Alberta (Solicitor General) (1992), 1992 ABCA 173 (CanLII), 40 M.V.R. (2d) 57, holding that driving a motor vehicle is not a liberty interest as defined under s. 7 of the Charter?

 

2.         Does the ALS program constitute valid provincial legislation?

3.         Does the ALS program offend s. 11(d) of the Charter?

 

4.         Does the ALS program offend s. 13 of the Charter?

 

5.         Does the ALS program authorize Charter breaches and, if so, is the Act

unconstitutional?

 

6.         Is Charter relief available under s. 24?

 

VI.       STANDARD OF REVIEW

 

[14]           This appeal challenges the constitutional validity of provincial legislation on pure questions of law and as such, the standard of review is correctness.

 

VII.     ANALYSIS

 

Issue 1.           Reconsideration of s. 7 Charter decisions

 

[15]           The appellants sought leave to re-argue that driving a motor vehicle is a liberty interest guaranteed under s. 7. This Court declined to grant leave in reasons issued separately at 2003 ABCA 112. We remain bound by our previous decisions in R. v. Neale and Yehia v. Alberta (Solicitor General), which accord with the weight of appellate authority across the country that driving a motor vehicle is not a liberty interest protected by s. 7.

 

Issue 2.           Does the ALS program constitute valid Provincial Legislation?

 

[16]           The appellants submit the ALS program is, in its purpose and effect, criminal law and therefore, ultra vires the jurisdiction of the Province. The intervener, the Attorney General of Alberta, and the respondent, the Board, submit that the program is a valid regulatory licencing program within provincial jurisdiction over property and civil rights.

 

A. Characterization - the pith and substance of the law

 

[17]           Resolving the first issue requires a division of powers analysis as outlined by the Supreme Court of Canada in  Reference re Firearms Act (Canada), 2000 SCC 31 (CanLII), [2000] 1 S.C.R. 783. The first stage of the analysis is to determine the “pith and substance” or essential character of the law by examining the purpose of the subject legislation, and its legal effect. A law’s purpose is often stated in the legislation, but may also be ascertained by reference to extrinsic material such as Hansard: Firearms Reference at para. 17. The purpose of legislation may also be determined by looking at the problems it is intended to address – the so‑called “mischief” approach: R. v. Morgentaler, 1993 CanLII 74 (SCC), [1993] 3 S.C.R. 463 at 483-4.

[18]           Determining the legal effects of a law involves considering how the law will operate and how it will affect Albertans. Whether the law is efficacious is not relevant to the analysis: see Firearms Reference at para. 18. In some cases, the effects of the law suggest a purpose other than that which is stated. Where the effects of the law diverge substantially from the stated aim, it is sometimes said to be “colourable”.

 

B. Classification - the division of federal and provincial powers

 

[19]           The second stage is to classify that essential character by reference to the heads of power under the Constitution Act, 1867 to determine whether the law is within the jurisdiction of the enacting government. If it is, then the law is valid.

 

[20]           The appellants submit the ALS program is a matter related to criminal law under s. 91(27) of the Constitution Act, 1867. The respondent and intervener submit the legislation, as a matter related to property and civil rights, falls within provincial jurisdiction under s. 92(13) of the Constitution Act, 1867.

 

[21]           Classification of legislation as criminal law, as a general rule, requires that the legislation possess three prerequisites: a valid criminal law purpose, backed by a prohibition and a penalty: see Firearms Reference at para. 27.

 

[22]           Classifying the legislation also recognizes, as Dickson, C.J.C. stated in General Motors of Canada Ltd. v. City National Leasing, 1989 CanLII 133 (SCC), [1989] 1 S.C.R. 641 at 669, that “overlap of legislation is to be expected and accommodated in a federal state”. Laws mainly in the jurisdiction of one level of government may overflow into, or have “incidental effects” upon, the jurisdiction of the other level of government. It is a matter of balance and of federalism: no one level of government is isolated from the other, nor can it usurp the functions of the other: see Firearms Reference at para. 26.

 

[23]           Thus, the double aspect doctrine permits both levels of government to legislate in one field for two different purposes: Provincial Secretary of Prince Edward Island v. Egan, 1941 CanLII 1 (SCC), [1941] S.C.R. 396. Where the resulting provincial and federal legislation truly conflict, federal legislation is paramount.

 

C. Application to this appeal

 

a)         Characterization

 

[24]           The stated purpose of the ALS program is contained in the extrinsic material filed by the intervener. Motor vehicle injuries and deaths caused by impaired driving exact a huge societal toll in Alberta. The evidence is overwhelming and undisputed. The extent of human suffering and loss, both personal and economic, continues to escalate. The evidence demonstrates that in Alberta, alcohol-involved motor vehicle accidents causing injury have remained at a higher level than, for example, in Ontario. Licence suspensions and specifically ALS programs are important instruments in provincial government efforts to combat a persistent and pressing public health and safety issue. The evidence supports the conclusion that the ALS program is designed to provide an immediate response to the identified problem. This is accomplished by immediately suspending the licence of impaired drivers. The immediacy of the suspension enhances specific deterrence by providing an immediate consequence to socially irresponsible behaviour. The timing of the suspension also acts as a general deterrent by reinforcing the significant consequences to the public. While these objectives are also largely present in post-conviction licence suspension legislation, only the ALS program satisfies one of the most important elements of deterrence – swiftness. The key feature of the ALS program remedies what, from a public safety and deterrence perspective, is seen as a lacuna in post-conviction suspension, that is, the inevitable delay in the imposition of a licence suspension when, and if, the driver is convicted of a criminal driving offence. The ALS program provides for the suspension of a licence regardless of the outcome of subsequent criminal proceedings.

 

b)         Classification

 

[25]           That the province has the jurisdiction to issue, suspend or cancel licences and regulate highway traffic safety by means of the administration of motor vehicle licences is well recognized. A long line of Supreme Court of Canada decisions has held that both the provincial and federal governments may legislate where criminal conduct and public safety overlap. Specifically, the imposition by a province of an automatic licence suspension upon a Criminal Code conviction for impaired driving was upheld by the Supreme Court of Canada in Egan, (supra). Similarly, provincial legislation suspending a driver’s licence upon refusal to provide a breath sample was upheld in Re Validity of Section 92(4) of the Vehicles Act, 1957 (Sask.), 1958 CanLII 61 (SCC), [1958] S.C.R. 608. Even where the provincial suspension provisions were more severe than the Criminal Code provisions, the provincial scheme has been upheld and not considered punishment within the exclusive federal sphere: Ross v. Registrar of Motor Vehicles, 1973 CanLII 176 (SCC), [1975] 1 S.C.R. 5.

 

[26]           The objective of the ALS program is public safety. The means chosen to achieve it is through licence suspensions. Licence suspensions are administrative in nature and while suspension of a licence may create a significant inconvenience, it does not violate a person’s liberty rights nor does it criminalize anti-social behaviour. Driving a motor vehicle remains a privilege in a civil society and while often taken for granted, carries with it obligations and qualifications which, if violated, may result in licence suspension or revocation.

 

[27]           The cases make clear that licence suspension has a legitimate place in a provincial safety program. Both provincial and federal governments can legislate where public safety and criminal law overlap. Compliance with provincial legislation does not result in conflict with federal legislation and vice versa. Further, the existence of sanctions in the federal Criminal Code does not so fully occupy the field that provincial safety programs suspending licences are precluded.

 

[28]           The appellants seek to distinguish the cases of provincial licence suspensions post-conviction from the case on appeal. The ALS program operates prior to conviction and therefore, it is argued, its legal purpose and effect is to fill what is characterized as a gap in the criminal law. The evidence supports the conclusion that the ALS program’s key feature is an almost immediate suspension in order to obtain a greater deterrent effect. However, I find nothing objectionable in the Province’s articulated goal of enhancing public safety through deterrence by suspending licences of those involved in alcohol-related driving faster than might be the result under criminal law.

 

[29]           The ALS program attempts to remove the delay in the suspension of a driver’s licence which occurs in the criminal law context. The evidence supports the conclusion that the effect of the delay undercuts the suspension’s deterrent effect and accordingly, at best, does nothing to mitigate the risk or, at worst, increases it. The primary concern of provincial legislatures is one of public safety as distinct from deterring or punishing criminal behaviour.  Where the province perceives that more can be done to increase highway and public safety in general, it is entitled to act accordingly. The objectives of the criminal law, as distinct from civil law, include deterring and sanctioning criminal behaviour. Criminal consequences are penal and may result in a deprivation of a liberty. Thus, criminal law aims to strike a careful balance of the individual’s rights and interest and broader societal goals. Where, as here, the consequences of the behaviour are civil in nature, the stringent procedural safeguards required under the criminal law are of less significance and the balance shifts somewhat in favour of the societal goal of deterrence and safety.

 

[30]           In the criminal law context, swiftness must yield to procedural safeguards. In a civil context, the balancing of societal interests against individual privilege changes the balance in favour of the former.

 

[31]           The appellants also raised arguments intended to demonstrate that the program does not accomplish the goal of public safety. However, as noted in the Firearms Reference, within its constitutional sphere, the legislators are the judges of whether a measure is likely to achieve its intended purpose. The intention here is to bring home to the driver and the public the immediate negative consequences of drinking and driving. Whether or not more drivers are actually deterred by the system is not relevant to determining legislative validity under a division of power analysis.


[32]           As noted by the chambers judge, the validity of Alberta’s ALS program is supported by decisions upholding similar administrative licence suspension systems in other provincial jurisdictions: in British Columbia, see Buhlers v. British Columbia (Superintendent of Motor Vehicles) (1999), 1999 BCCA 114 (CanLII), 170 D.L.R. (4th) 344, (B.C.C.A.); in Manitoba, see R. v. Leclair (1990), 1990 CanLII 11364 (MB QB), 67 Man. R. (2d) 265, (Q.B.); in Ontario, see Horsefield v. Ontario (Registrar of Motor Vehicles) (1999), 1999 CanLII 2023 (ON CA), 172 D.L.R. (4th) 43,(Ont. C.A.); in Prince Edward Island, see R. v. MacCormack (1999), 1999 CanLII 4559 (PE SCAD), 180 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 314,(P.E.I. C.A.); and in Nova Scotia, see White v. Nova Scotia (Registrar of Motor Vehicles) (1996), 1996 CanLII 5627 (NS SC), 147 N.S.R. (2d) 259, (S.C.); aff’d without reasons, May 27, 1996, No. C.A. 124997 (N.S.C.A.).

 

Issue 3:           Does the ALS program offend s. 11(d) of the Charter?

 

[33]           The appellants argue the ALS program violates the presumption of innocence under s. 11(d) of the Charter because it creates a burden on the applicant to prove his or her innocence before the Board. They submit that in appealing a licence suspension, the burden is on the applicant to offer evidence or make representations to satisfy the Board that no alcohol-related driving occurred or a sample was reasonably refused and as such, the appellant is not presumed innocent.

 

[34]           The chambers judge held that an appellant under the ALS program must satisfy the Board that he did not engage in alcohol-related driving in order to have the suspension automatically set aside under s. 39(4). Similarly, he held that the Crown must satisfy the Board that alcohol-related driving has occurred to maintain the suspension under s. 39(5). He held that if the Board is not satisfied on a balance of probabilities as to the presence or absence of alcohol-related driving, the suspension should be lifted. I agree. In my view, this does not create a reverse onus. However, even if it could be said that a reverse onus exists, it would not violate s. 11(d) given the civil nature of these proceedings.

 

[35]           Section 11(d) of the Charter provides:

 

11. Any person charged with an offence has the right

(d) to be presumed innocent until proven guilty according to law in a fair and public hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal;

 

[36]           In order to receive the protection of s. 11(d) of the Charter, a person must be “charged with an offence”. The rights in s. 11(d) do not apply to administrative or civil proceedings, but are guaranteed for persons prosecuted by the state for public offences involving punitive sanctions: Blencoe v. British Columbia (Human Rights Commission), 2000 SCC 44 (CanLII), [2000] 2 S.C.R. 307; R. v. Wigglesworth, 1987 CanLII 41 (SCC), [1987] 2 S.C.R. 541.

 

[37]           Section 11(d) does not apply to a hearing before the Board. A driver subject to a suspension under the ALS program is not charged with a criminal or quasi-criminal offence; a driver faces a withdrawal of a privilege, not a punitive sanction; cannot be compelled to give evidence; and is not, at the end of the day, found guilty of an offence. I agree with the decision of Melvin J. in Willock v. British Columbia (Superintendent of Motor Vehicles) (2000), 2000 BCSC 772 (CanLII), 23 Admin.L.R. (3d) 111 (B.C.S.C.), that s. 11(d) is not violated by the process and procedures for an appeal hearing under a provincial ALS program.

 

Issue 4:           Does the ALS program offend s. 13 of the Charter?

 

[38]           The appellants argue that an appeal before the Board requires revealing the appellant’s entire case prior to defending the subsequent criminal charges. This, they argue, violates s. 13 of the Charter.

 

[39]           Section 13 of the Charter provides:

 

13. A witness who testifies in any proceedings has the right not to have any incriminating evidence so given used to incriminate that witness in any other proceedings, except in a prosecution for perjury or for the giving of contradictory evidence.

 

[40]           A hearing before the Board, practically speaking, will occur before a first appearance in a criminal court. If any evidence is put before the Board, there is no provision in the legislation prohibiting the evidence from being used in the subsequent criminal trial. Additionally, the appellants argue, if an appellant reveals a good defence, for example, based on a defect in the certificate of analysis, the Crown will have time to remedy the defect prior to the criminal proceedings.

 

[41]           As seen in s. 39(4) of the Act, an appellant cannot be compelled to give evidence before the Board. Even if an appellant chooses to testify, the appropriate forum for raising the protection of s. 13 is during the criminal proceedings, not before the Board. As the chambers judge pointed out, there is no need to include in the ALS provisions a prohibition against the use of the testimony in later criminal proceedings because the right in s. 13 of the Charter, conceived broadly, already provides that safeguard.

 

[42]           As for the appellant revealing his or her defence prior to the criminal proceedings, that complaint is essentially about the claimed loss of a tactical advantage. Section 13 does not provide protection against any such loss. Instead, it protects the right against self incrimination. Further, the Charter itself does not guarantee the right of an individual to avail himself or herself of all potential or strategic advantages. The ALS program does not violate s. 13.

 

Issue 5:           Does the ALS program authorize Charter breaches?

 

[43]           The appellants argue that even if valid under a division of powers analysis, the program is unconstitutional because no procedural or constitutional safeguards are required to be recognized by the Board during its hearings. Thus, they submit, the legislation is an unlawful delegation of authority to infringe the Charter. This submission was cast more broadly in oral argument to suggest the legislation is invalid as it authorizes conduct that is contrary to the Charter. In considering whether legislation “authorizes” such conduct, it is necessary to determine:

 

(a)        whether the legislation itself authorizes infringement;

(b)        if not, whether the necessary effect of the legislation authorizes infringement; or

(c)        if the alleged infringement relates to a person exercising the delegated discretion.

 

In the third example, the legislation does not fail but the infringement may warrant Charter relief.

 

A. Analytical framework

 

[44]           Legislation may be unconstitutional on its face because it violates a Charter right and is not saved by s. 1. In such cases, the legislation will be invalid and declared of no force or effect pursuant to s. 52(1) of the Constitution Act, 1982. On the other hand, the Charter may be infringed, not by the legislation, but by the actions of a delegated decision‑maker in applying it. In the second case, the legislation remains valid, but a remedy for the unconstitutional action may be sought pursuant to s. 24(1) of the Charter: Eldridge v. British Columbia (Attorney General), 1997 CanLII 327 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 624 at para. 20.

 

[45]           As a corollary, s. 32 limits Parliament and legislatures from granting power or authority to a delegate to act in a manner which would result in an infringement of the Charter: Slaight Communications Inc. v. Davidson, 1989 CanLII 92 (SCC), [1989] 1 S.C.R. 1038 at para. 87.

 

[46]           In determining whether the law infringes a Charter right, the first step requires examination of the purpose and effect of the challenged legislation. More specifically, the analysis, as undertaken in Little Sisters Book and Art Emporium v. Canada (Minister of Justice), 2000 SCC 69 (CanLII), [2000] 2 S.C.R. 1120 at para. 125, requires determination of whether there is anything “on the face of the...legislation, or in its necessary effects” which contemplates or encourages infringement of Charter rights.

 

B. Purpose and effects of the legislation

 

[47]           The appellants’ submission parallels their argument advanced under the division of powers; that the legislation is a direct attempt to truncate or eliminate important Charter protections under the guise of public safety. I have earlier concluded that the Act has as its purpose the regulation of highways and the increase of safety on them. Nothing on the face of the impugned provisions infringes or authorizes infringement of any section of the Charter including ss. 8 and 10(b). The absence of certain procedural protections present in the criminal law does not alter the character or purpose of the legislation.

 

[48]           The unconstitutional effects alleged by the appellants must be the necessary effects resulting from the legislation itself. An infringement of the Charter by the actions of a delegated decision‑maker in applying the legislation, as distinct from the legislation itself, does not render the legislation invalid. In Little Sisters, for example, Binnie, J., for the majority found, at para. 125, that the differential treatment based on sexual orientation was not a necessary effect of the Customs legislation but was a differentiation made at the administrative level in the implementation of the legislation. The source of the Charter violation of s. 15, therefore, was not the legislation itself.

 

[49]           The appellants submit that the necessary effect of the legislation requires the Board to consider evidence obtained contrary to the Charter. This, they submit, is in effect authorization to breach the Charter. Section 39(3) of the Act makes it mandatory for the Board to review the police report, the certificate of analysis and any sworn or affirmed statements. It does not explicitly state that the Board has discretion whether or not to consider the police report or the certificate if, for example, the evidence was obtained improperly as a result of infringing the right to legal counsel or as a result of an unreasonable search and seizure. By implication, the appellants submit, the Act authorizes the Board to condone police violations of the Charter rights guaranteed by ss. 8 and 10(b).

 

[50]           This submission fails for two reasons. The first is that the argument assumes that the ability of a court or a tribunal to consider evidence obtained in violation of the Charter implicitly or explicitly “authorizes” Charter violations. This assumption is incorrect. Secondly, the submission fails to recognize the important distinction between Charter rights as they apply in a criminal context and their scope and application in civil and administrative proceedings.

 

[51]           As to the first submission, s.39(3) of the Act does not mandate acceptance of evidence obtained contrary to the Charter. Moreover, the Supreme Court of Canada in Mooring v. Canada (National Parole Board), 1996 CanLII 254 (SCC), [1996] 1 S.C.R. 75 has made it clear that evidence obtained in breach of a Charter right may nevertheless by considered by a tribunal. How it is to be considered need not be mandated by the legislation and its admissibility is properly determined by the board or tribunal. In Mooring, the court held the National Parole Board was not required to treat s. 24(2) Charter cases excluding evidence as determinative because it did not possess the power nor was it required to exclude evidence under s. 24(2).

 

[52]           The necessary effect of the Board giving consideration to evidence obtained contrary to the Charter is not authorization to breach the Charter any more than it would be the necessary effect of a court admitting evidence obtained in breach of Charter rights. As delegated authorities, the Charter applies to police or peace officers. They are not authorized, for example, to conduct unreasonable search and seizures contrary to s. 8. Nevertheless, whether intended or otherwise, breaches have occurred and may reoccur. Those breaches, however, do not invalidate legislation but may give rise to a Charter remedy. Where a Charter remedy is not granted, the subsequent consideration of evidence improperly obtained does not constitute an ex post facto authorization to breach a Charter right.

 

[53]           As to the second concern, the application of the Charter to the admission of evidence in civil and administrative proceedings is distinct from that in the criminal law. The tribunal is governed by the duty of fairness, as it may apply in each case. Its decision to consider evidence is determined subject to that duty.

 

Issue 6:         Availability of Charter relief

 

[54]           The appellants point to several cases where Charter breaches have been found in criminal proceedings flowing from roadside detentions conducted simultaneously under the Criminal Code and the ALS program. They give the example of breath samples being obtained in contravention of ss. 8 or 10(b). They submit that the legislation fails to provide a remedy and that such remedy must be available through a court of competent jurisdiction under s. 24(1). I disagree.

 

[55]           Section 24(1) of the Charter provides:

 

24. (1) Anyone whose rights or freedoms, as guaranteed by this Charter, have been infringed or denied may apply to a court of competent jurisdiction to obtain such remedy as the court considers appropriate and just in the circumstances.

 

[56]           In criminal proceedings, a person appealing a licence suspension would be seeking to exclude evidence as provided by the Charter at s. 24(2):

 

24(2) Where, in proceedings under subsection (1), a court concludes that evidence was obtained in a manner that infringed or denied any rights or freedoms guaranteed by this Charter, the evidence shall be excluded if it is established that, having regard to all the circumstances, the admission of it in the proceedings would bring the administration of justice into disrepute.

 

However, that remedy is not available under this legislation.

 

A. The Board is not a court of competent jurisdiction

 

[57]           Statutory tribunals may be courts of competent jurisdiction to grant Charter remedies: Weber v. Ontario Hydro, 1995 CanLII 108 (SCC), [1995] 2 S.C.R. 929. In this case, it was conceded the Board is not a court of competent jurisdiction and is not authorized by the legislation to exclude evidence as a result of a breach of Charter rights.

 

[58]           As Binnie J. noted in Little Sisters at para. 137, there is potential for Charter abuse in the administration of a legislated scheme, however, “a rule requiring Parliament to enact in each case special procedures for the protection of Charter rights would be unnecessarily rigid.” He noted that there are various methods to ensure respect by the public service for the Charter rights of citizens. One such example is the imposition of the duty of procedural fairness which applies to guide the Board when dealing with the rights, privileges and interests of individuals. While distinct from a s. 24(2) remedy, the duty in a particular circumstance may require a tribunal not to consider evidence.

 

B. The duty of fairness

 

[59]           Historically, the common law required tribunals, including administrators, to observe the principles of natural justice. Natural justice requires a tribunal, when reaching a decision, will do so with procedural fairness. Natural justice has two main rules: audi alteram partem - the requirement that a person know the case against him or her and be given the opportunity to answer it; and nemo judex in sua causa debet esse – the rule against bias. For administrative tribunals, the principles of natural justice have been extended and described as a duty to be fair.

 

[60]           The Supreme Court of Canada has repeatedly affirmed as a general common law principle that “there is a duty of procedural fairness lying on every public authority making an administrative decision which is not of a legislative nature and which affects the rights, privileges or interests of an individual”: Nicholson v. Haldimand‑Norfolk Regional Board of Commissioners of Police, 1978 CanLII 24 (SCC), [1979] 1 S.C.R. 311; Martineau v. Matsqui Institution Disciplinary Board (No. 2), 1979 CanLII 184 (SCC), [1980] 1 S.C.R. 602; Attorney General of Canada v. Inuit Tapirisat of Canada, 1980 CanLII 21 (SCC), [1980] 2 S.C.R. 735; Cardinal v. Director of Kent Institution, 1985 CanLII 23 (SCC), [1985] 2 S.C.R. 643 at 653. Clearly, the suspension of a driver’s licence affects an individual’s privileges and interests and the Board here is obligated to exercise a duty of fairness.

 

a)                  Content of the duty of fairness

 

[61]           The content of the duty of fairness is flexible and variable depending upon the context of the particular statute involved and the rights, privileges or interests affected. “[T]he purpose of the participatory rights contained within the duty is to ensure that administrative decisions are made using a fair and open procedure, appropriate to the decision being made and its statutory, institutional and social context, with an opportunity for those affected to put forward their views and evidence fully and have them considered by the decision-maker.”: Baker v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 1999 CanLII 699 (SCC), [1999] 2 S.C.R. 817 at para. 22.

 

[62]           The several criteria to determine what procedural rights the duty of fairness requires in the circumstances of the operation of this civil law program, as set out in Baker at paras. 23-28, include: (1) the nature of the decision being made and process followed in making it; (2) the nature of the statutory scheme and the terms of the statute pursuant to which the body operates; (3) the importance of the decision to the individual or individuals affected; (4) the legitimate expectations of the person challenging the decision; (5) the choices of procedure made by the agency itself. The list is not exhaustive.

 

b)         The content of the duties of the Board

 

[63]           As noted in Horsefield, the Ontario ALS program is an example where the common law duty of fairness may be overridden by the legislature. The Ontario program does not provide a right to a suspension hearing. In Alberta, the legislation recognizes the existence of the duty by providing, for example, the right to a hearing before the Board to confirm or cancel a driver’s licence suspension or disqualification.

 

[64]           While the effect of the ALS statute is licence suspension in the interest of public safety, the Board does not determine whether safety was at issue vis a vis the driver in question. It is restricted to satisfying itself that the statutory conditions for suspension are present. The evidence it is to consider is set out in s. 39(3)(a)-(d) of the Act. Discretion is granted to the Board to determine whether a person had a reasonable excuse for failure to comply with a demand for a breath sample, whether statements and other information are relevant, and at an oral hearing, whether evidence and information given or presented is relevant and therefore to be considered. Parenthetically, where the “defence” of reasonable excuse is proffered, the Board must be satisfied to a civil standard. It is therefore possible to have different results before the Board and a court of law dealing with criminal charges arising from the same event. This possibility, however, does not render the legislation unconstitutional.

 

[65]           The legislation specifically includes participatory rights which embody the principles of natural justice. It requires that a notice of suspension be served on the driver. A right to appeal the suspension by means of an oral hearing before the Board is provided. Appellants may be represented by counsel, but are not required to give viva voce evidence under oath or to testify at all. The relatively short period for the temporary licence, 21 days, also indicates the Board is required to make its determination with some swiftness.

 

[66]           Recognizing the role which motor vehicles play in our society, the decision of the Board may have a significant impact on the economic welfare of the individuals affected. The legitimate expectations of licence holders, however, cannot be placed as high as when constitutionally guaranteed rights are involved or when balanced against the public safety concerns arising from alcohol- related driving.

 

[67]           With this in mind, the Board’s duty of fairness may be described as follows: not to be arbitrary or capricious but “require a full and fair consideration of the issues, and the [appellant] and others whose important interests are affected by the decision in a fundamental way must have a meaningful opportunity to present the various types of evidence relevant to their case and have it fully and fairly considered”.: Baker at para. 32.

 

[68]           In summary, to comply with its duties of fairness, the Board must:

 

a) inform the appellant of the case against him or her,

b) permit the appellant a meaningful opportunity to answer the case against him or her,

c) give full and fair consideration to the issues,

d) consider the source of the evidence or information including whether it was gathered in a manner contrary to the Charter or Charter values,

e) consider relevant evidence and information, and as a corollary

f) not consider irrelevant or unreliable evidence or information,

g) not act arbitrarily, for an improper purpose or with malice.

 

[69]           Although the Board does not have the power under s. 24(2) to exclude evidence obtained in breach of Charter rights, the manner in which evidence was obtained remains a factor which must be considered when the Board exercises its duty of fairness. At a minimum, relevance and reliability remain necessary considerations before information or evidence is to be considered. Fairness may preclude consideration of information which is not reliable or relevant. Where the manner of obtaining the evidence raises issues of its reliability or relevance, the Board must decide whether it should consider such evidence and may decide it is not fair to do so. Again, as in Mooring, the Board here is not required to apply the traditional rules of evidence but is required to fulfill its fairness obligations while considering “evidence and information” which is “relevant” to satisfy itself that a person consumed alcohol, the concentration exceeded .08, drove a motor vehicle, or refused to supply a breath sample without reasonable excuse. In acting in accordance with these principles, the Board does not infringe Charter values nor is it acting on the basis of “unconstitutional evidence”.

 

[70]           Where the Board fails to meet its procedural fairness obligations or otherwise acts in contravention of the Charter, then resort may be had to judicial review: Campeau Corp. v. City of Calgary (1978), 1978 ALTASCAD 266 (CanLII), 12 A.R. 31 (C.A.). Such procedure was appropriately invoked by at least one appellant in the case at bar. Mr. Hay’s licence suspension was properly quashed by the chambers judge and remitted back for a consideration by the Board in accordance with its obligation of procedural fairness.

 

[71]           As noted above, it is indeed conceivable that a driver could have his or her licence suspended after the Board has considered improperly obtained evidence and then, at a subsequent trial, be acquitted of any charges under the Code because the same evidence was excluded under s. 24(2) of the Charter. This result is not incongruous, unreasonable or a legitimate basis for avoiding the Board and seeking remedy from a court of competent jurisdiction. The loss of a driver’s licence through the ALS program is a civil consequence and distinct from any criminal conviction or criminal penalty.

 

[72]           In this regard, I agree with the Nova Scotia Court of Appeal in Nova Scotia (Attorney General) v. Selig (1996), 1996 CanLII 5239 (NS CA), 141 D.L.R. (4th) 462, which held that the act of suspension and act of sustaining the suspension after review are lawful acts performed pursuant to valid provincial legislation. A subsequent acquittal on charges under the Criminal Code arising out of the same circumstances does not make the administrative licence suspension unlawful or void because the Board, in the exercise of its duty of fairness, considered the impugned evidence and, on that basis, confirmed the licence suspension.

 

[73]           The objective of the ALS program is public safety. The consequences of a breach of the statute are civil - a licence suspension. A breach does not threaten a liberty interest nor result in the consequences of a criminal conviction. The substantive and procedural differences between criminal law and civil law flow from their respective social purposes. The potential deprivation of a liberty interest in a criminal law context engages a plethora of rights and procedural safeguards that are not justified in a civil law context where the jeopardy is civil only and there are significant countervailing societal concerns at stake. The Legislature here has attempted to strike a balance between procedural safeguards and public safety. It has done so in a manner that accords with its legislative authority. 

 

VIII.    CONCLUSION

 

[74]           The ALS program is administrative in nature and designed to enhance public safety on highways. As such, it is intra vires the Province of Alberta and does not offend the Charter. The appeal must be dismissed.

[75]           As requested by counsel, in order to provide time to deal with the consequences of this decision, including the numerous stays of the licence suspensions and the Criminal Code charges granted pending this appeal, the suspensions will not commence until one month from the date of this decision.
