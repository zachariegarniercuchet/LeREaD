FAMILY LAW - DIVORCE - Corollary Relief, Maintenance & awards - Spousal Support - Retroactive awards - Pensions - Appeals

 

The Court of Appeal confirmed the order of the motion judge that the respondent was entitled to retroactive spousal support up to the date of the appellant=s retirement. However, the Court varied the date for the commencement of the payment of retroactive spousal support to the date when the respondent gave the appellant effective notice of her intention to seek such support. The court also confirmed the order of the motion judge that spousal support continue at a reduced amount following the appellant=s retirement and his receipt of pension income.

Reasons for judgment:

 

McQUAID J.A.:

 

[1]               Sterling Kier MacQuarrie (the "appellant") and Frances Elaine MacQuarrie (the "respondent") were married on October 16, 1976.  They separated on May 18, 1998, and entered into a separation agreement on August 3, 2001. The terms of the separation agreement provided for the division of their real and personal property.

 

[2]               The real property consisted of the matrimonial home which was sold. The net proceeds from the sale were divided equally and each received $39,343.55.

 

[3]               With respect to the personal property relevant to this appeal, they agreed to divide the appellant's interest in the pension plan available to him as a member of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (ARCMP@).  This resulted in a transfer to the respondent of the sum of $154,297.02.


 

[4]               The Registered Retirement Saving Plans registered in names of either the appellant or the respondent were valued at $20,000. and transferred to the respondent.

 

[5]               The separation agreement also provided that the respondent could make an application for a division of the appellant's Canada Pension Plan credits in accordance with the prevailing legislation.

 

[6]               In accordance with the terms of the separation agreement, the appellant agreed to pay the respondent spousal support in the amount of $1,700. per month. The agreement provided that payments of spousal support would continue on the following terms:

 

Such payments of a spousal support shall continue to be paid except for an order of variation based upon a material change in circumstances it [sic] being expressly acknowledge, [sic] that the quantum of spousal support shall be subject to review by the court at the time of the Husband's retirement.

 

[7]               There were three children of the marriage; however at the time of separation, only one was dependent. The separation agreement provided for the payment of child support in the amount of $300. per month for this dependent child as long as he resided with the respondent and was not working full time.

 

[8]               The appellant subsequently petitioned for divorce and on February 15, 2002, a divorce judgment was issued out of the Supreme Court. It dissolved the marriage between the parties and provided for the payment of spousal and child support. The amount and terms of this support provided for in the judgment were as agreed upon in the separation agreement as noted in paragraph 6 above.

 

[9]               By way of recital, the divorce judgment notes that the parties had entered into a separation agreement which settled the division of property, custody, access, child support and spousal support. Also by way of recital, the divorce judgment notes the appellant's salary at the time was $85,000. per year. Given the amount of spousal support and child support agreed to in the separation agreement, the judgment notes that the Court was satisfied the appellant had made reasonable and adequate provision for the support of the dependent child of the marriage.

 

[10]           On May 7, 2004, the respondent advised the appellant that the dependent child of the marriage no longer resided with her and that child support was no longer required.  The appellant discontinued child support payments at this time.

 

[11]           In 2005 the appellant voluntarily increased spousal support payments to $1,810. per month to provide Blue Cross coverage for the formerly dependent child.  These increased payments continued until October 2010 when the appellant resumed the payment of the amount of spousal support provided for in the divorce judgment.  There were no further adjustments to the amount of spousal support.

 

[12]           On November 30, 2010, the appellant attested to the contents of a "Financial Statement" which set forth the amount of salary he was then receiving as a member of the RCMP, as well as the amount of the monthly pension he would receive when he retired.

 

[13]           On December 21, 2010, the appellant swore an affidavit wherein he attested to the fact that he planned to retire on April 1, 2011, and that he would be seeking an order from the court to vary his spousal support obligation so that he would not be required to pay spousal support subsequent to his retirement. Neither the financial statement nor the affidavit was served on the respondent at this time.  It is also noted that on January 20, 2011, the appellant attested to an affidavit which contained the same information as that contained in the affidavit of December 21st.

 

[14]           On January 12, 2011, the appellant filed a Notice of Application in the Supreme Court.  He asked the Court to order that on his retirement his obligation to pay the respondent spousal support pursuant to the terms of the divorce judgment be varied such that his obligation would be terminated or, alternatively, to order that the monthly payment of spousal support be reduced with a provision as to the length of time his support obligation would continue.  The financial statement dated November 30, 2010, and the affidavit sworn December 21, 2010, were filed with the notice of application and served on the respondent with the notice of application.

 

[15]           An amended notice of application was filed by the appellant with the Supreme Court on February 10, 2010.  The effect of the amendment was to cite reliance upon sections 15 and 17 of the Divorce Act rather than reliance upon section 17 alone.

 

[16]           On January 25, 2011, the respondent, in response to the appellant's notice of application, filed a financial statement and an affidavit.  In her affidavit the respondent attested that the appellant's salary with the RCMP had increased since the date of the divorce judgment and that she was seeking retroactive spousal support increases from the date of each salary increase to the date of the appellant's retirement.  She stated the salary increases constituted a material change in circumstances, and this warranted a variation of the amount of spousal support ordered to be paid by the appellant pursuant to the terms of the divorce judgment.

 

[17]           The respondent also took the position in responding to the appellant's notice of application that on his retirement and receipt of his pension, the amount of spousal support should not be varied from that provided for in the divorce judgment. She set forth her monthly expenses and attests to the fact that with her employment as a pharmacy assistant she requires spousal support in the amount of $1,700. to meet those expenses.

 

[18]           The application was heard by Mitchell J. on March 29, 2011.  He gave his judgment and delivered his reasons orally on the same date. On April 27, 2011, an order was filed in the Supreme Court and it provided follows:

 

1.             That the appellant pay the respondent retroactive spousal support in the amount of $4,000.00 from November 1, 2010 to the date the appellant's retirement becomes effective.

 

2.             That the appellant provide the respondent with proof of his retirement and the retirement date.

 

3.             That commencing one month after the date of his retirement the appellant is to pay the respondent spousal support in the amount of $1,100.00 per month.

 

4.             That either party may apply for a variation of the order upon establishing a material change in circumstances.

 

5.             That the respondent shall have her costs of the application which costs were to be assessed by the motions judge.

 

[19]           On April 28, 2011, the appellant filed a notice of appeal in this Court. He asks this Court to set aside the order of the motion judge and specifically he seeks the following relief: (i) that the award of retroactive spousal support be varied or eliminated; (ii) that the amount of spousal support payable after the appellant's retirement be varied or eliminated; and (iii) that the costs of the proceedings in the Supreme Court and in this Court be awarded to the appellant.

 

THE REASONS OF THE MOTION JUDGE

 

[20]           The motion judge addressed the respondent's counter-application for retroactive spousal support from the dates of the appellant's various salary increases to the date of the appellant=s retirement. He found, as a general rule, that the date for the commencement of the payment of retroactive spousal support is the date which the receiving spouse gives effective notice to the paying spouse of his or her intention to seek such support. 

 

[21]           The motion judge found that an exception to the general rule arises when it is found as a fact that the paying spouse engaged in dishonest or deceitful conduct which shielded knowledge of the material change in circumstances (the salary increases) from the receiving spouse. The motion judge found as a fact that the respondent had not previously requested a variation in the amount of spousal support, and the appellant was not deceitful nor did he take steps to hide salary increases he had received since the date of the divorce judgment.  On the basis of these facts, the motion judge concluded it would be punitive to award retroactive spousal support effective from the date of all the appellant's salary increases ‑ essentially a ten-year term - to the date of the appellant=s retirement.

 

[22]           The motion judge found that the appellant's increase in salary constituted a material change in circumstances and he held the respondent was entitled to retroactive spousal support. He further held that the effective date of notice by the respondent to the appellant that she was seeking retroactive spousal support, was November 1, 2010, the first day of the month the appellant completed the above referenced financial statement filed in support of his application for variation.  Accordingly, he ordered the appellant to pay retroactive spousal support from November 1, 2010, to the date the appellant retires.

 

[23]           The motion judge calculated the amount of retroactive spousal support utilizing the Spousal Support Advisory Guidelines. He calculated the amount of the appellant's income at the date of the motion and deducted the respondent's income. To calculate the percentage of this difference upon which the amount of spousal support per year would be determined, the motion judge considered the length of the marriage, 21.5 years, multiplied it by 1.5% as well as by 2%. In accordance with the Spousal Support Advisory Guidelines, this provided the motion judge with the range of percentages ‑ 32.3% to 43% ‑ upon which spousal support would be fixed. In dollars and cents and based on the difference between the two incomes, this worked out to a range of $2,975. per month to $3,997. per month.

 

[24]           The motion judge then considered the respondent's need as well as the appellant's ability to pay and concluded the appellant should pay spousal support from November 1, 2010, to the date of his retirement in the amount of $4,000. per month which was at the highest end of the above range.  He deducted from this amount spousal support of $1,700. per month which the appellant had paid from November 1, 2010, in accordance with the provisions of the divorce judgment.

 

[25]           In the same manner, the motion judge also applied the Spousal Support Advisory Guidelines in calculating the amount of spousal support the appellant was obligated to pay the respondent following his retirement.  He applied the same formula utilizing the appellant's pension income except the portion applicable to the appellant=s pension that was divided upon separation and determined that following his retirement, the appellant would pay the respondent spousal support in the amount of $1,100. per month.

 

ISSUES

 

[26]           This appeal raises the following issues:

 

(1)               Is the respondent entitled to retroactive spousal support?

 

(2)               If the respondent is entitled to retroactive spousal support, what is the effective date for the commencement of the payment?

 

(3)               Do the spousal support guidelines apply to the calculation of the amount of variation of spousal support both retroactively and subsequent to the appellant's retirement?

 

(4)               Is the respondent entitled to receive spousal support after the appellant's retirement given that she received a capitalized portion of his pension at the time the parties separated?

 

STANDARD OF REVIEW

 

[27]           The order of the motion judge on each of the above issues cannot be overturned by this Court unless he made an error in principle, misapprehended the evidence, or unless the amount of spousal support he ordered to be paid by the appellant is clearly wrong. See: Hickey v. Hickey, 1999 CanLII 691 (SCC), [1999] 2 S.C.R. 518. Furthermore, with respect to findings of fact made by the motion judge, absent an error of law by the application of an incorrect legal standard, this Court may only interfere with his findings of fact or the inferences he extracted from the facts where he has made a palpable and overriding error. See: Housen v. Nikolaisen, 2002 SCC 33 (CanLII), [2002] 2 S.C.R. 235.

 

DISPOSITION

 

[28]           I would allow the appeal in part.

 

[29]           The motion judge made an error in principle when he ordered that the effective date for the payment of retroactive spousal support was November 1, 2010. With respect to the other parts of his order, the motion judge did not make a reversible error, and I would confirm the order in all other respects.

 

[30]           My reasons follow and in the context of the four issues identified above.

 

ANALYSIS

 

[31]           Section 17(1)(a) of the Divorce Act provides that a court may issue an order varying a previous order which provides for the payment of spousal support.  Before making such an order, the court is directed by the provisions of ss. 17(4.1) to "Y satisfy itself that a change in the condition, means, needs or other circumstances of either former spouse has occurred since the making of the spousal support orderY"

 

[32]           An order varying a previous order for payment of spousal support should recognize the following as provided for in s-s. 17(7) of the Divorce Act:

 

(a)           any economic advantages disadvantages to the former spouse arising from the marriage or its breakdown;

 

(b)           the apportionment between the former spouses of any financial consequences arising from the care of any child of the marriage over and above the provision of support for that child;

 

(c)           relief of any economic hardship for the former spouses arising from the breakdown of the marriage and;

 

(d)           as far as it may be practicable, the promotion of the economic self‑sufficiency of each former spouse within a reasonable period of time.

 

[33]           The uncontroverted evidence before the motion judge was that the pre‑retirement income of the appellant had increased significantly since the date of the separation agreement and the divorce judgment, both of which provided for the payment of spousal support in the amount of $1,700. per month. With respect to the appellant's post‑retirement income, the evidence also establishes that the appellant's income will be reduced. 

 

[34]           At the time of the motion, the respondent was 59 years of age.  She purchased another home after the marriage breakdown in which she and the dependent child of the marriage resided. She used some of the money paid to her from the division of assets as equity in the purchase of this home. She continues to own this home although she no longer has the dependent child residing with her.  The respondent did assist an adult daughter of the marriage by providing the home for the daughter and her child between 2005 and 2008 while the daughter completed her education.

 

[35]           The respondent attempted to assimilate herself into the workplace. She is qualified as a pharmacist assistant and earns $18,300. per year working at Walmart. Self-sufficiency has proved difficult and impossible to this point in time.

 

[36]           The evidence is clear that the respondent was at home during the marriage. Despite her good faith efforts to be self‑sufficient, the respondent's income remains low.

 

[37]           The evidence supports the finding of the motion judge there has been a material change in the condition, means, needs and the circumstances of the two parties since the original order of spousal support.  The evidence also supports the finding of the motion judge that the respondent has suffered a disadvantage as the result of the marriage breakdown and furthermore, that she continues to suffer economic hardship for the same reason. Finally, the evidence supports the motion judge=s finding the respondent has taken the steps so far as practicable in her circumstances to become self‑sufficient.

 

[38]           The motion judge did not err in principle nor did he misapprehend the evidence when he concluded that pursuant to the provisions of s.17 of the Divorce Act, a variation of the amount of spousal support provided for in the divorce judgment should be ordered.

 

(i)         Is the respondent entitled to retroactive spousal support?

 

[39]           Having correctly found a material change of circumstances, the first issue addressed by the motion judge was the respondent's entitlement to retroactive spousal support from the date of the appellant's various salary increases to the date of his retirement.  While the motion judge did not accede to the request of the respondent to have retroactive spousal support reach back to the date of the appellant=s various salary increases, he did, nevertheless, order the appellant to pay retroactive spousal support from November 1, 2010, to the date of the appellant=s retirement.  Furthermore, while the respondent has not cross-appealed from this part of the motion judge=s order, the appellant has appealed from that part of the order which requires the appellant to pay retroactive spousal support from the foregoing date.  Therefore, it is necessary to review that part of the motion judge=s order which entitles the respondent to retroactive spousal support from November 1, 2010, to the date of the appellant=s retirement.

 

[40]           In Kerr v. Baranow 2011 SCC 10; [2011] 1 S.C.R. 269, the court set down and applied the factors which are relevant when spousal support is sought for a period which predates the order the court is being asked to make. In the case at bar, this would be the period of time between the divorce judgment and the order for variation sought by the appellant.

 

[41]           At paragraph 207 of his reasons in Kerr v. Baranow, Cromwell J., writing for the court, in considering whether a retroactive spousal support order should be made, adopted considerations similar to those applicable where the order sought is for retroactive child support.  In doing so, Cromwell J. applied the decision of the Court in D.B.S. v. S.R.G.; L.J.W. v. T.A.R.; Henry v. Henry; Hiemstra v. Hiemstra 2006 SCC 37; [2006] 2 S.C.R. 231.  The factors include: the needs of the receiving spouse; the conduct of the payor; any hardship the payment of retroactive support might have on the payor, and the reason for the receiving spouse's delay in requesting the variation in the amount of support.

 

[42]           Cromwell J. points out at paragraph 207 of his reasons in Kerr v. Baranow these factors should be considered in "Y deciding the suitability of a 'retroactive' award of spousal support."  He went on to make it clear that child support and spousal support have a "different legal foundation." While concerns about lack of notice and delay have minimum impact where the request is for retroactive child support, these factors deserve more consideration when the application is for retroactive spousal support. Where the paying spouse takes the position the receiving spouse should have made the request earlier, the underlying interests for the consideration of the court are first, the need for the payor to have certainty surrounding his obligations and the need to create incentives for the receiving spouse to seek the required variation in support expeditiously.  At paragraph 208 and 209 Cromwell J. stated:

 

[208]       Spousal support has a different legal foundation than child support. A parent‑child relationship is a fiduciary relationship of presumed dependency and the obligation of both parents to support the child arises at birth. In that sense, the entitlement to child support is >automatic= and both parents must put their child's interests ahead of their own in negotiating and litigating child support. Child support is the right of the child, not of the parent seeking support on the child's behalf, and the basic amount of child support under the Divorce Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. 3 (2nd Supp.), (as well as many provincial child support statutes) now depends on the income of the payor and not on a highly discretionary balancing of means and needs. These aspects of child support reduce somewhat the strength of concerns about lack of notice and lack of diligence in seeking child support. With respect to notice, the payor parent is or should be aware of the obligation to provide support commensurate with his or her income. As for delay, the right to support is the child's and therefore it is the child's, not the other parent's position that is prejudiced by lack of diligence on the part of the parent seeking child support: see D.B.S., at paras. 36‑39, 47‑48, 59, 80 and 100‑104. In contrast, there is no presumptive entitlement to spousal support and, unlike child support, the spouse is in general not under any legal obligation to look out for the separated spouse's legal interests. Thus, concerns about notice, delay and misconduct generally carry more weight in relation to claims for spousal support: see, for example, M.L. Gordon, "Blame Over: Retroactive Child and Spousal Support in the Post‑Guideline Era" (2004‑2005), 23 C.F.L.Q. 243, at pp. 281 and 291‑92.

 

[209]       Where, as here, the payor's complaint is that support could have been sought earlier, but was not, there are two underlying interests at stake. The first relates to the certainty of the payor's legal obligations; the possibility of an order that reaches back into the past makes it more difficult to plan one's affairs and a sizeable >retroactive= award for which the payor did not plan may impose financial hardship. The second concerns placing proper incentives on the applicant to proceed with his or her claims promptly (see D.B.S., at paras. 100‑103).

 

[43]           In Marinangeli v. Marinangeli (2003), 2003 CanLII 27673 (ON CA), 38 R.F.L. (5th) 307; [2003] O.J. No. 2819 (Ont. C.A.), the Court reviewed the factors to be considered when deciding to order retroactive spousal support. At paragraphs 74 to 84, Weiler J.A. states that the basic principle is that retroactive support is to be ordered sparingly. She then adopts as applicable in a motion for retroactive spousal support, principles developed by the British Columbia Court of Appeal in a motion for retroactive child support. These are: (1) the need of the receiving spouse and the corresponding ability of the paying spouse to pay; (2) improper conduct by the payor spouse; (3) the need to encroach on capital or incur debt; (4) the excuse for the delay in bringing the motion for retroactive support; and (5) notice to the payor spouse of the intention to seek retroactive support.

 

[44]           In Kerr v. Baranow, Cromwell J. essentially affirms these principles and considerations at paragraph 212 of his reasons. He states:

 

[212]       Other relevant considerations noted in D.B.S. include the conduct of the payor, the circumstances of the child (or in the case of spousal support, the spouse seeking support), and any hardship occasioned by the award. The focus of concern about conduct must be on conduct broadly relevant to the support obligation, for example concealing assets or failing to make appropriate disclosure: D.B.S., at para. 106. Consideration of the circumstances of the spouse seeking support, by analogy to the D.B.S. analysis, will relate to the needs of the spouse both at the time the support should have been paid and at present. The comments of Bastarache J. at para. 113 of D.B.S. may be easily adapted to the situation of the spouse seeking support: >A [spouse] who underwent hardship in the past may be compensated for this unfortunate circumstance through a retroactive award. On the other hand, the argument for retroactive [spousal] support will be less convincing where the [spouse] already enjoyed all the advantages (s)he would have received [from that support].= As for hardship, there is the risk that a retroactive award will not be fashioned having regard to what the payor can currently afford and may disrupt the payor's ability to manage his or her finances. However, it is also critical to note that this Court in D.B.S. emphasized the need for flexibility and a holistic view of each matter on its own merits; the same flexibility is appropriate when dealing with >retroactive= spousal support.

 

[45]           In the case at bar, it is clear from the evidence that the respondent is in need of support from the appellant. Her earning capacity was diminished by the marriage, and she continues to suffer economic hardship as a result of the marriage breakdown. The motion judge found there was no improper conduct on the part of the appellant in the sense that he withheld information regarding his increase in salary.  While the respondent was aware of his promotions, she did not inquire or request information as to his salary increases.

 

[46]           The evidence also supports the finding of the motion judge that the appellant had the capacity to pay retroactive spousal support.

 

[47]           The respondent did not take any steps to obtain information about the appellant's salary after the divorce judgment, and she did not seek an increase in spousal support until she was confronted with the appellant's motion to reduce the amount of spousal support because of his impending retirement. Her stated reason for this delay is that the appellant would continually inform her that he was not paying any additional support.  Given her limited means and her limited ability to improve her economic situation, absent additional support her delay was found by the motion judge to be at least partially excusable, and this is reflected in his conclusion to limit the scope of the retroactive award back to only November 2010 as opposed to having it reach back to the dates of the appellant's various salary increases.

 

[48]           In reaching the conclusion that the respondent was entitled to a retroactive award of spousal support, the motion judge did not misapprehend the evidence nor did he make an error in principle.  He applied the foregoing factors. He weighed and assessed the evidence in the context of those factors.  As a result, this court does not have any jurisdiction to interfere with his order in this respect.

 

(ii)        If the respondent is entitled to retroactive spousal support, what is the effective date for the commencement of the payment?

 

[49]           The remaining question with respect to the payment of retroactive spousal support is the effective date for the commencement of the support. As I noted above,  the motion judge's order to have the retroactive support reach back to only November 1, 2010, has not been appealed by the respondent even though she initially sought to have the support payable from the date of each of the appellant's salary increases. 

 

[50]           With respect to the effective date for the commencement of retroactive spousal support, the appellant takes the position that if he is obligated to pay retroactive spousal support, payment should not commence any earlier than the date on which the respondent gave notice to the appellant she was seeking retroactive support.  This date the appellant argues is January 25, 2011, the date she attested to the contents of the affidavit she filed in response to the appellant's application for a variation of the spousal support ordered to be paid in the divorce judgment.

 

[51]           In D.B.S. v. S.R.G. supra, where the issue was the effective date for the payment of retroactive child support, Bastarache J. adopted as a general rule or as the default date, the date of "effective notice@ as the time when retroactive support should commence. He found the date of "effective notice" is that date upon which the receiving parent provides any indication to the paying parent that child support should be paid or if previously ordered or agreed upon, the child support should be varied or re‑negotiated.  Such notice does not require that the receiving parent  commence legal action but it does require that parent to communicate to the paying parent in some manner that the amount of support should be varied.

 

[52]           In the event however, evidence discloses blameworthy conduct on the part of the paying parent, the date of retroactivity will move the "presumptive date of retroactivity" back to the date upon which there was in fact a material change in circumstances. Actions which constitute blameworthy conduct include withholding information about an increase in income as well as acts of intimidation and lies toward the receiving parent. Bastarache J. concluded his reasoning at paragraph 125 by summarizing in the following fashion:

 

[125]       The proper approach can therefore be summarized in the following way: payor parents will have their interest in certainty protected only up to the point when that interest becomes unreasonable. In the majority of circumstances, that interest will be reasonable up to the point when the recipient parent broaches the subject, up to three years in the past. However, in order to avoid having the presumptive date of retroactivity set prior to the date of effective notice, the payor parent must act responsibly: (s)he must disclose the material change in circumstances to the recipient parent. Where the payor parent does not do so, and thus engages in blameworthy behaviour, I see no reason to continue to protect his/her interest in certainty beyond the date when circumstances changed materially. A payor parent should not be permitted to profit from his/her wrongdoing.

 

[53]           Also in D.B.S. v. S.R.G. supra, Bastarache J. addressed the issue as to how far back retroactive child support should reach.  He found considering that the Child Support Guidelines limited the obligation of parents to provide historical financial information up to three years, retroactive child support payments should not reach back further than three years from the date "formal notice" was given to the paying parent. See: paragraphs 118 to 123.

 

[54]           In Kerr v. Baranow supra at paragraphs 206 and 207, Cromwell J. adopted the considerations set forth in D.B.S. v. S.R.G. for purposes of determining whether a retroactive spousal support order should be made. At paragraph 211 of his reasons,  Cromwell J. refers to "the date of effective notice" set down by Bastarache J. in D.B.S. v. S.R.G. as being the default option for determining when notice was given by a receiving spouse to a paying spouse that spousal support should be varied based on a material change in circumstances. Cromwell J. goes on to state that the effective date of retroactivity as far as ordering spousal support is always a matter of judicial discretion. The date of the commencement of legal proceedings is the usual date of commencement unless there is a reason not to make the order effective from that date. If the order is sought from the date of the commencement of proceedings, that will be a significant factor in weighing the other considerations.

 

[55]           In summary, I understand the effect of direction provided by Cromwell J. is that the choice of the date of retroactivity for the payment of spousal support is a matter of judicial discretion exercised against the backdrop of the particular circumstances of the case and considering a number of relevant factors.  If one of the circumstances is that the order being sought requests retroactive support only from the date of the commencement of legal proceedings, that is a fact which bears heavily on the considerations of the other relevant factors. Additional relevant factors are: the conduct of the payor, the circumstances of the receiving spouse, and any hardship that may result from the payor having to make a retroactive payment. Conduct is failing to disclose assets and failing to make appropriate disclosure. At paragraphs 211 and 212 Cromwell J. stated:

 

[211]       In D.B.S., Bastarache J. referred to the date of effective notice as the >general rule= and >default option= for the choice of effective date of the order (paras. 118 and 121; see also para. 125). The date of the initiation of proceedings for spousal support has been described by the Ontario Court of Appeal as the >usual commencement date,= absent a reason not to make the order effective as of that date: MacKinnon v. MacKinnon (2005), 2005 CanLII 13191 (ON CA), 75 O.R. (3d) 175, at para. 24. While in my view, the decision [page363] to order support for a period before the date of the order should be the product of the exercise of judicial discretion in light of the particular circumstances, the fact that the order is sought effective from the commencement of proceedings will often be a significant factor in how the relevant considerations are weighed. It is important to note that, in D.B.S., all four litigants were requesting that child support payments reach back to a period in time preceding their respective applications; such is not the case here. (my emphasis)

 

[212]       Other relevant considerations noted in D.B.S. include the conduct of the payor, the circumstances of the child (or in the case of spousal support, the spouse seeking support), and any hardship occasioned by the award. The focus of concern about conduct must be on conduct broadly relevant to the support obligation, for example, concealing assets or failing to make appropriate disclosure: D.B.S., at para. 106. Consideration of the circumstances of the spouse seeking support, by analogy to the D.B.S. analysis, will relate to the needs of the spouse both at the time the support should have been paid and at present. The comments of Bastarache J. at para. 113 of D.B.S. may be easily adapted to the situation of the spouse seeking support: >A [spouse] who underwent hardship in the past may be compensated for this unfortunate circumstance through a retroactive award. On the other hand, the argument for retroactive [spousal] support will be less convincing where the [spouse] already enjoyed all the advantages (s)he would have received [from that support].= As for hardship, there is the risk that a retroactive award will not be fashioned having regard to what the payor can currently afford and may disrupt the payor's ability to manage his or her finances. However, it is also critical to note that this Court in D.B.S. emphasized the need for flexibility and a holistic view of each matter on its own merits; the same flexibility is appropriate when dealing with >retroactive= spousal support.

 

[56]           In the case at bar the divorce judgment provides for the payment of monthly spousal support in a fixed amount payable except when varied by order occasioned by a material change in circumstances.  Furthermore, the divorce judgment explicitly states that the appellant's retirement would constitute a material change in circumstances subjecting the payments to review by the court.  This entire provision respecting spousal support had been previously agreed upon by the parties in their separation agreement.

 

[57]           In her evidence the respondent states that since the divorce judgment, she did not request a variation of the amount of spousal support until she was served with the appellant's motion to vary the amount based on his imminent retirement.  She states she was unaware that she could seek an order varying the amount of spousal support.  She also stated that she did not request nor did the appellant voluntarily provide her with information of his promotion and salary increases subsequent to the date of the divorce judgment. She attested in her affidavit that she was advised by the appellant on more than one occasion he would not pay her any additional support nor should she expect more. She alleged the appellant was "forceful, intimidating and confrontational" in making these assertions.

 

[58]           While the motion judge did not expressly address the respondent's evidence that the appellant was intimidating and confrontational when he took the position that he would not pay any additional support, the motion judge did not find the appellant engaged in any blameworthy conduct. As a result, it is reasonable to infer the motion judge did not accept the respondent's evidence in this regard.

 

[59]           As I pointed out above, the motion judge found that the respondent did not request information from the appellant regarding his income subsequent to the divorce judgment, the appellant did not voluntarily disclose information as he had no obligation to do so, and the appellant did not by deceit or otherwise withhold such information from the respondent. The motion judge also found that the respondent was in need and the appellant had the ability to pay.

 

[60]           All of these findings of fact are consistent with the evidence before the motion judge, and they do not constitute a reversible error on his part.

 

[61]           The motion judge correctly concluded that absent blameworthy conduct on the part of the paying spouse, the date of retroactivity is the date upon which the receiving spouse gives the paying spouse effective notice of an intention to seek a variation in the amount of spousal support paid under an existing agreement or order. The motion judge also correctly concluded that effective notice does not have to be the commencement of legal proceedings. Such notice could be as simple as an e‑mail requesting financial information as to the appellant=s salary thereby putting the paying spouse on notice that it is time to rearrange his or her financial affairs in light of what may be a material change in circumstances.

 

[62]           The motion judge then went on to select the date of effective notice.  He reviewed the record and noted that the "issue was opened in the month of November" because on November 30, 2010, the appellant completed his financial statement. Accordingly, he adopted the first day of that month as the date of effective notice by the respondent to the appellant that she was requesting a retroactive variation in the amount of spousal support payable under the divorce judgment. 

 

[63]           With respect, this constitutes an error of law because it fails to appreciate the meaning of effective notice as set down by Bastarache J. in D.B.S. where he held that effective notice is "Y any indication by the recipient parent that child support should be paid, or if it already is, that the current amount of child support needs to be re‑negotiated." As I pointed out above this definition of Aeffective notice@ was adopted by Cromwell J. in Kerr v. Baranow.  Cromwell J. made this definition subject to the qualification that it applies as the default commencement date for the payment of retroactive spousal support unless there are circumstances where a judge might otherwise exercise his or her discretion and select another commencement date, such as the date upon which there was in fact a material change in circumstances.

 

[64]           The financial statement or disclosure prepared by the appellant and dated November 30, 2010, which was not served on the respondent until January 12, 2011, cannot be construed as an indication by the respondent to the appellant that she intended to ask the court to vary the amount of such support which had been ordered by the divorce judgment.  The first date on which such information was passed along to the appellant was January 25, 2011, when the respondent gave notice in her affidavit that she was requesting a retroactive variation in the amount of spousal support. Accordingly, this is the date of effective notice and the date upon which retroactive spousal support should commence.

 

(iii)       Do the Spousal Support Guidelines apply to the calculation of the amount of spousal support on a variation order?

 

[65]           The Spousal Support Advisory Guidelines (the "Guidelines") were prepared by Professors Carol Rogerson and Rollie Thompson with the support of Justice Canada. They are not applicable to the determination of a spouse's entitlement and, with respect to quantum they are advisory only, because unlike the Child Support Guidelines, they do not have the force of law.

 

[66]           The authors of the Guidelines contemplated they would apply to the initial award of spousal support as well as in certain other situations. They will apply, for example, on a motion for variation in situations where there was an increase in a recipient's income and a decrease in a payor's income.  On the other hand, the authors contemplated that they would not be applicable in situations where there are case-by-case discretionary determinations to be made such as where there is a post‑separation increase in the payor's income, re‑partnering, remarriage and second families.  See: the Guidelines at Chapter 14. 

 

[67]           In the case at bar, the motion judge was confronted with a situation where a variation and/or termination of spousal support was sought by the appellant on the ground that his income was decreasing. The motion judge was also confronted with a counter‑application by the respondent for retroactive spousal support on the ground that the appellant's income had increased between the date of the divorce judgment and the date of the respondent=s counter-application.

 

[68]           Therefore, with respect to the variation sought by the appellant on the grounds that his income will decrease upon his retirement, it is contemplated that the Guidelines would apply. On the other hand, with respect to the variation sought by the respondent for retroactive spousal support based on the appellant=s increase in his post-separation income, it is contemplated the Guidelines would not apply.

 

[69]           Nevertheless, I am satisfied that the factors present in the case at bar were not such as to prevent the motion judge from utilizing the Guidelines in the calculation of the amount of retroactive spousal support on the basis of the appellant=s post-separation salary increase.  See: Beninger v. Beninger 2007 BCCA 619; [2007] B.C.J. No. 2657; (2007), 47 R.F.L. (6th) 11, at paragraphs 50 - 55.  These factors were all considered above in reaching the conclusion that the motion judge did not err in ordering that the respondent was entitled to retroactive spousal support.  I wish to make it clear, as did the British Columbia Court of Appeal, that the decision to apply the Guidelines on a variation application is generally to be made cautiously and on the facts of each case.

 

[70]           In addition to questioning the use of the Guidelines by the motion judge, the appellant also asserts the motion judge erred in the application of the Guidelines as to the determination of the amount of retroactive spousal support as well as the amount of support to be paid by the appellant post‑retirement.

 

[71]           The Guidelines involve the application of two basic formulas. They are, the "without child formula" and the "with child formula." They both use income sharing as the method for determining the amount of spousal support. In cases, like the case at bar where there are no dependent children of the marriage, the "without child formula" applies.  This formula assists in the determination of the amount of support and the duration of support. 

 

[72]           Chapter 3 of the Guidelines provides an overview of their provisions. Paragraph 3.3.3 provides an explanation of the process to be applied under the "without child support formula" in determining the amount and duration of spousal support. The details of this process are provided in Chapter 7 of the Guidelines. Paragraph 7.1 reads as follows:

 

                                                The Without Child Support Formula

 

Amount ranges from 1.5 to 2 percent of the difference between the spouses= gross incomes (the gross income difference) for each year of marriage (or more precisely, year of cohabitation), up to a maximum of 50 percent.  The range remains fixed for marriages 25 years or longer, at 37.5 to 50 percent of income difference.  (The upper end of this maximum range is capped at the amount that would result in equalization of the spouses= net incomesB the net income cap).

 

Duration ranges from .5 to 1 year for each year of marriage.  However support will be indefinite (duration not specified) if the marriage is 20 years or longer in duration or, if the marriage has lasted five years or longer, when years of marriage and age of the support recipient (at separation) added together total 65 or more (the rule of 65).

 

 

[73]           The motion judge did not use the Guidelines in establishing the duration of spousal support either retroactively or post‑retirement.  In my view they would not be applicable in establishing the duration of retroactive support as this is established in accordance with the foregoing case law.

 

[74]           The motion judge did use the guidelines in determining the amount of support in both situations before him for consideration. At page 5 of the transcript of his oral reasons, lines 8 to 15, the motion judge stated as follows:

 

...The formula which would apply in this case is called the without child formula, and so the parties understand what the without child formula is, you take the difference between the parties= income, in this case Mr. MacQuarrie=s income in 2010 was $129,806 plus the $1800 rounded off, $149.86 times 12, less Mrs. MacQuarrie=s income of $20,035.  The difference is $111,571.  So you take that sum of money, you take a percentage of it, and the percentage is arrived at by multiplying 1.5 by the number of years of the marriage and then multiplying 2 by the number of years of the marriage and I took 21 and a half years of marriage and that gives you a range.  The range is 32 to 43% of $111,571.  Works out to, on a monthly basis, 2975 to 3997.

 

[75]           Chapter 9 of the Guidelines highlight the factors which are typically taken into account in the use of the ranges and thus in fixing the precise amount of spousal support. These factors are: the strength of any compensatory claim; the needs of the recipient; the needs and the ability of the payor as self‑sufficiency incentives for the recipient.  The motion judge considered all these factors in the application of the without child formula provided for in the guidelines which are advisory and not mandatory.  Consequently, he did not err in law or principle, and he did not misapprehend the relevant evidence in determining the amount of spousal support payable, either retroactively or subsequent to the appellant's retirement. 

 

(iv)       Is the respondent entitled to receive spousal support after the appellant's retirement given that she received a capitalized portion of his pension on the equalization of property at the time of the separation of the parties?

 

[76]           There is no dispute as to the amount of the pension utilized by the motion judge to calculate the amount of spousal support. Suffice to say the motion judge based his calculations of the amount of spousal support subsequent to the appellant's retirement on the un‑capitalized portion of the appellant's pension. Accordingly, he took into account and excluded the portion of the appellant's pension which was divided at the separation and transferred to the respondent in cash.  The motion judge calculated the appellant's pension income, excluding the pension previously divided, as $4,486.18 per month plus his Workers Compensation pension of $149.86 per month for a total of $630. per month or $55,632. per annum.  He then applied the spousal support guidelines and came up with a monthly spousal support payment in the amount of $1,100. per month.

 

[77]           The respondent invested the portion of the pension paid to her on separation in a locked‑in RRSP account.  The terms of this account are such that she does not have access to the capital or the income until she attains the age of 65. The motion judge found this to be a reasonable course of action as he accepted the respondent's evidence that, at the time she received the cash payment, she did not intend to retire until that age.

 

[78]           The appellant takes the position that upon his retirement the respondent has an obligation to generate sufficient income to support herself from that part of the pension she received. The appellant's further position is that for him to now pay support to the respondent from his pension income after she has received a portion of his pension on equalization of their property, constitutes double recovery on her part. The appellant's position is premised on the proposition that post‑ retirement, the respondent is not entitled to any further spousal support because double recovery is prohibited.  The appellant contends this position is supported by the decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in Boston v. Boston 2001 SCC 43; [2001] 2 S.C.R. 413.

 

[79]           It is necessary to briefly review the facts of that case.

 

[80]           The parties separated after a 36-year marriage. The wife never worked outside the home.  Accumulated assets were divided by agreement, each of the husband and the wife receiving an equal amount.  The husband's pension was included which is considered as part of net family property. He agreed to pay his wife spousal support. At the time of the agreement he was earning $115,476./year.

 

[81]           The husband retired and his income was decreased to the amount he was earning on his pension. His income was $7,600./mo. from his pension plus an amount from his CPP.

 

[82]           After the agreement, the wife did not earn any employment income. She invested her assets prudently, and they were worth substantially more at the time of the application than they were on the date of the agreement.  On the other hand, the husband's capital assets exceeded his debts by only $7,000.

 

[83]           The husband applied to reduce the amount of spousal support.

 

[84]           The pension had two components.  The larger part of the $7,600.(5,300) came from the asset he retained on the equalization, and he claimed this should not be used in calculating support.  The second component (2,300) was earned since separation and was not part of the equalization.

 

[85]           The husband took the position that in light of the earlier division of the assets, the wife had an obligation to contribute to her own support and only the un‑equalized portion of his pension should be considered when determining support on a material change in circumstances.

 

[86]           Writing for the majority of the Court, Major J. at paragraph 28 of his reasons stated the issues to be resolved in Boston v. Boston as follows:

 

1.             Is a retired payor spouse entitled to seek to reduce the support obligation to a former spouse on the basis that the pension now being received was previously considered in the distribution of matrimonial property?

 

2.             Does the spouse who received assets in exchange for a share of the capitalized value of the other spouse's pension have an obligation to invest those assets in order to produce an income? If those assets are not invested to produce an income, should the court impute to the spouse an income based on what those assets could produce if invested and thereby reduce the spousal support obligation?

 

[87]           These are essentially the questions which need to be addressed in this appeal.

 

[88]           The pension entitlement the respondent received at the date of the separation involved a determination of a future income stream.  It was valued and a lump sum amount representing that value was paid to the respondent.

 

[89]           Double recovery on the part of a receiving spouse arises in a situation where the pension, having been equalized as property, is subsequently treated as income from which the paying spouse must make spousal support payments. The receiving spouse gets an amount on separation which takes into account the capital value of the paying spouse's future pension income.  If the receiving spouse later shares in the pension income by way of continued spousal support, that spouse can be said to be receiving from the pension twice, at equalization and again as support from the pension income.

 

[90]           In Boston v. Boston at paragraph 43 of his reasons, Major J. adopts as a foundational principle that the starting point for addressing double recovery issues is to determine how to fairly avoid double recovery.

 

[91]           Simply put, the challenge is to avoid double recovery when it is fair to do so.  This is not to say that in all cases double recovery will be eliminated because, in some cases double recovery may be the only fair way to continue support.

 

[92]           Another basic principle is that the receiving spouse should strive toward becoming economically self‑sufficient.  Nevertheless, the law does recognize that from a practical perspective and in a long term marriage where the spouse has been outside the workforce for a long period of time, self‑sufficiency may not be attainable.  Other factors such as age, education and child-rearing responsibilities must, therefore, be considered.  When all these are considered, it may be unrealistic in certain cases to expect that the receiving spouse will be capable of returning to income earning employment after marriage breakdown. Nevertheless, the receiving spouse must make a reasonable effort to use the assets received on the equalization of a pension for income generation. This obligation is satisfied if the receiving spouse invests those assets in a capital depleting fund that would allow that spouse to receive a regular income.  See: Boston v. Boston at paragraphs 54 to 58.

 

[93]           As we see in the case at bar, when the pension holding spouse retires, the issue as to whether spousal support should continue frequently arises when the pension-holding spouse applies for a variation of the support order or agreement on the ground that his reduced pension income constitutes a material change in circumstances. It is important to recognize there is no fundamental rule that spousal support cannot continue after the retirement of the paying spouse.

 

[94]           Several factors must be considered in making that decision. The pension-holding spouse's ability to pay, the needs of the receiving spouse as well as the best way to avoid double recovery are all factors to be considered. This returns to the starting point question and that is: How to fairly avoid double recovery?  See: Boston v. Boston at paragraphs 61 to 63.

 

[95]           At paragraph 64 of his reasons in Boston v. Boston, Major J. stated:

 

[64]         To avoid double recovery, the court should, where practicable, focus on that portion of the payor's income and assets that have not been part of the equalization or division of matrimonial assets when the payee spouse's continuing need for support is shown (see Hutchison, supra, at para. 9). In this appeal, that would include the portion of the pension that was earned following the date of separation and not included in the equalization of net family property.

 

[65]         Despite these general rules, double recovery cannot always be avoided. In certain circumstances, a pension which has previously been equalized can also be viewed as a maintenance asset. Double recovery may be permitted where the payor spouse has the ability to pay, where the payee spouse has made a reasonable effort to use the equalized assets in an income‑producing way and, despite this, an economic hardship from the marriage or its breakdown persists. Double recovery may also be permitted in spousal support orders/agreements based mainly on need as opposed to compensation, which is not the case in this appeal.

 

[96]           As Major J. states, double recovery cannot always be avoided; and it may be permitted where the payor has the ability to pay, where the payee spouse has made a reasonable effort to use the equalized assets in an income producing way, and despite these steps, an economic hardship from the marriage continues to persist.  He also makes it clear that double recovery will be allowed to exist where a spousal support order or an agreement providing spousal support is based mainly on need as opposed to compensation.

 

[97]           In the case at bar, the respondent was in a long-term marriage where she had the primary child-rearing duties. She did not pursue a career but remained at home to care for the three children of the marriage.  This allowed the appellant to pursue his career with obviously some measure of success given his salary at the time of his retirement.

 

[98]           The motion judge found that the respondent took reasonable steps with respect to the investment of the lump sum payment of cash she received on separation to equalize the pension.  She anticipated her retirement to be 65 years of age, and in accordance with this assessment, invested the money in a locked-in RRSP which means that until she reaches age 65, she does not have access to the income from this investment.

 

[99]           The respondent has also taken reasonable steps to assimilate herself back into the workforce. As Major J. acknowledged in Boston v. Boston, the real possibility of this occurring is remote.  Also, there is the real possibility that a spouse who has been outside the workforce for a long period of time will be unable to generate self‑sufficiency.

 

[100]       The motion judge considered all these factors in his assessment of the appellant's continued responsibility to pay spousal support after his retirement. In calculating the amount of the continued support, the motion judge did not include that portion of the pension capitalized and paid to the respondent in a lump sum at the date of the separation.

 

[101]       In the result on the disposition of this issue, the motion judge did not err in principle, he did not make errors of law and he correctly assessed the relevant evidence.  Therefore, this court is unable to interfere with his order that post‑retirement, the appellant shall pay the respondent $1,100. per month in spousal support.

Furthermore, counsel did not move to amend the notice of appeal in this respect. He did not appeal from the motion judge=s exercise of discretion to award costs to the respondent. Accordingly, I do not consider this is an issue to be resolved on the appeal.

[108]       In conclusion, I would allow the appeal in part.  I would vary the order of the motion judge setting the date for the commencement of retroactive spousal support from the 1st day of November, 2010, to the 25th day of January, 2011.  I would dismiss the appeal from the remaining terms of the motion judge=s order, and confirm the order of the motion judge in all other respects.
