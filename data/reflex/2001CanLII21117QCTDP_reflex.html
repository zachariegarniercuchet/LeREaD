J U D G M E N T

 

 

[1]           Acting on behalf of Ms. S. N.[1] (complainant), the Commission des droits de la personne et des droits de la jeunesse (Commission) has referred to the Human Rights Tribunal (Tribunal) an application that claims unlawful discrimination violating the dignity of the complainant, in contravention of sections 4, 10, 16 and 18.2 of the Charter of human rights and freedoms (Charter).

 

[2]           The Commission contends that the defendant refused to consider S.N.'s candidacy for the position of police officer with the Service de police of the Communauté urbaine de Montréal (SPCUM) owing to a previous conviction, on the basis of which the SPCUM denied S.N. the opportunity to participate in the selection process.

 

[3]           As regards damages, the Commission is claiming from the defendant $5000 for the moral damage sustained by the complainant, the whole with interest from February 4, 1998, as well as costs.

 

[4]           Furthermore, the Commission is asking the Tribunal to order the SPCUM to cease to consider a penal or criminal offence for which the offender was pardoned as a violation of the criterion of "good moral character", stipulated under paragraph (b) of section 2 of the By-law respecting standards of the Sûreté du Québec and municipal police forces for the hiring of constables and cadets[2] (Regulation 14).

 

[5]           The SPCUM contends that it acted in accordance with Regulation 14 and that S.N.'s candidacy was rejected in keeping with it.

 

[6]           The SPCUM also contends that it was justified in rejecting S.N.'s candidacy since the offence was directly related to the work of a police officer and she was not pardoned for it.

 

[7]           Exhibits P-1, P-2 and P-3 were admitted.

1.         OBJECTIONS TO THE EVIDENCE

 

[8]           During the hearings, the Tribunal disposed of objections, a number of which were the object of an application for judicial review. It is important to take stock of these elements, since they have an impact on the recitation of the facts.

 

[9]           The SPCUM filed a bound document entitled [TRANSLATION] "List of the defendant's exhibits", which contained 28 exhibits. The Commission objected to the filing of exhibits D-16, D-17 and D-18 on the ground that they were part of the Commission's investigative process and their filing violated section 95 of the Charter. Given their inclusion in the bound document filed by the SPCUM, the Commission agreed that those three exhibits be considered technically filed, but it maintained its objection as regards their merits under section 95, as well as their relevance. The Tribunal overruled the objection concerning Exhibit D-16, since that was a SPCUM document filed in accordance with the rules of evidence by witness Cailler. As for exhibits D-17 and D-18, the Tribunal allowed the objection. Those exhibits had no relevance to the case, being no more than an exchange of correspondence between the Commission's investigator and the SPCUM's attorney during the hearing.

 

[10]        Counsel for the defendant asked that documents relating to the investigation by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) be filed as Exhibit D-31 in this case and that the complainant be examined in that regard. Counsel for the plaintiff objected to their filing on the ground that those documents were not relevant: the application submitted to the Tribunal dealt with the decision of the SPCUM, not the RCMP. Moreover, the record assembled by the RCMP was not an element that the SPCUM took into account in its decision.

 

[11]        The Tribunal upheld the objection on the ground that that evidence was not relevant.[3] The Tribunal was seized of an application related to the decision of the SPCUM, not the RCMP. The objections to the questions asked S.N. about elements related to the RCMP file were upheld on the same grounds.

 

[12]        Although the parties agreed to the filing of the RCMP's confidential record as Exhibit D-31 for the specific purpose of allowing the attorneys to take cognizance of its content and of checking whether it contained a copy of the letter in which the RCMP refused S.N.'s candidacy, the Tribunal maintained its decision concerning the objection by the plaintiff of the admissibility of that evidence. Because of its very nature, the investigation record cannot constitute hearsay evidence. In addition, the Tribunal noted the lack of relevance of that document to this case, which solely concerns the decision of the SPCUM as the only body contemplated by the Charter and as it was rendered at the time.

 

[13]        The Superior Court (500-05-054821-994) dismissed the application for judicial review of those two interlocutory decisions of the Tribunal, on the basis of the admissibility as evidence of Exhibit D-31 and of the limited cross-examination of S.N. It said:

 

[TRANSLATION]

[27] It must be stressed that, in the framework of a motion for judicial review, the CUM must convince the Court not only that the decisions of the HRTQ are erroneous but especially that the evidentiary elements are crucial, given the circumstances of the dispute. The CUM must also convince the Court that the fairness of the trial is in jeopardy because of the refusal to admit as evidence statements attributed to people the identity of whom we are unaware of for the most part and which is indicated in a report by a person who did not appear at the hearing (double hearsay). After weighing the two opposing concerns, which are often incompatible, of the fair treatment of S.N. and the interests of society in the use of conclusive evidentiary elements with a view to bringing out the truth in this case, the Court is of the opinion that the decision of the HRTQ not to accept the RCMP's hiring record is not unreasonable.

 

…

 

[33] A necessary corollary of the exclusive jurisdiction of the HRTQ to limit the dispute is its exclusive jurisdiction to then direct the debate accordingly. Among other things, it can choose not to allow the cross-examination of S.N. to continue on anything other than the RCMP's letter of refusal to hire her. In this case, the limitations that the HRTQ placed on the cross-examination of S.N. did not have such an impact on the fairness of the process that the only conclusion possible was a violation of natural justice.

 

[14]        On August 23, 2000, the Court of Appeal (500-09-009979-006) dismissed the motion for leave to appeal from the decision of the Superior Court.

 

2.         THE FACTS

 

[15]        The complainant, now 30 years of age, is a social worker and holds two certificates, one in law and the other in delinquency and criminality. She has mainly performed the duties of a correctional service officer for about two years.

 

2.1       THE APPLICATION AND REFUSAL TO HIRE

 

[16]        In May 1995, she submitted her candidacy for the position of police officer with the SPCUM. She testified that, since secondary school, she had been interested in police work and questions of delinquency. In 1993-1994, she even underwent surgery to correct her vision so that she would meet the standards for that type of work. It was precisely because of that operation that she waited to submitted her candidacy to the SPCUM (Exhibit D-7) and the RCMP. Her candidacy was rejected by both the RCMP and the SPCUM. However, these proceedings concern only the SPCUM's hiring process, since it is the only one of the two bodies that falls under provincial jurisdiction and is therefore subject to the Charter.

 

[17]        On November 13, 1995, she received a letter from Philippe Gohier of the hiring section of the SPCUM personnel management division (P-2), informing her that her candidacy had been rejected because [TRANSLATION] "following a preliminary judicial investigation, you were found not meet the requirement laid down in Regulation 14 of the Québec Police Act, to which we are subject in regard to the hiring standards for our police officers".

 

[18]        S.N. telephoned Mr. Gohier to find out why her candidacy had been rejected. He explained that the investigation had found that she had a criminal record. S.N. then mentioned section 18.2 of the Charter, and the SPCUM resumed the analysis of her file. Shortly after that conversation, Mr. Gohier contacted S.N. to ask her to undergo the third stage in the process, namely, the psychometric test.

 

[19]        However, although an appointment was made for the test, she did not take it. Instead, she received a second letter on July 17, 1996 that reiterated that her candidacy had been rejected because she did not meet [TRANSLATION] "the requirement laid down in Regulation 14" (P-3). She again contacted Mr. Gohier, who told her that the decision had been maintained because the SPCUM considered her not to be of good moral character.

 

[20]        S.N. stated that that was because she had pleaded guilty to a shoplifting offence, for which she had received a conditional discharge according to the terms of the Criminal Code. Those events occurred on August 28, 1990. S.N. was then 21 years of age, was attending CEGEP and was working part time. She explained to the Tribunal that she was revolting against her parents' authority when she committed the offence. She had gone to secondary school at a private girls' school run by nuns, an environment that she called conformist, in which she was given very little leeway. The same was true in her relationship with her parents. CEGEP showed her other facets of life.

 

[21]        On August 28, 1990, she went to La Baie and proceeded to hide a sweater and a coverall in her bag as she made her way to the fitting rooms. Then she concealed a belt and an elastic band for her hair before she left without paying. The total value of the goods was about $200. S.N. said she had not gone to the store with the intention of stealing. But once she was there, in the first fitting room, the idea came to her that she would defy authority, i.e. she would steal something. She said she was, however, very ambivalent about what she was doing, which explains why, according to her, she went into three fitting rooms before heading for the exit. She was not aware of those around her, particularly the security officers, because, she said, [TRANSLATION] "I didn't know anything about that, about stealing. To my mind, there were no security officers. I was really in my own little world. I was … I didn't check to see whether I was under surveillance or anything". She was arrested when she went through the checkout counter without paying.

 

[22]        On May 13, 1991, S.N. pleaded guilty to the charge of shoplifting and was sentenced to a discharge under the following conditions: she had to pay $200 to a foundation and was on probation for one month.

 

[23]        S.N. took steps to receive a pardon for that sentence (P-5). In response to her requests, she was informed of the amendments to the Criminal Records Act made through Bill C-71 of July 24, 1992. The substance of the letter from the head of the RCMP's pardon and human rights section, Sergeant J.L.G. Laviolette (P-4) was as follows:

 

[TRANSLATION] … A pardon is no longer required for an offence for which the offender is sentenced to an absolute discharge or a conditional discharge. Hence, your request for your criminal record with a view to an application for a pardon was considered a request to delete that information from your criminal record. All discharges, be they absolute or conditional, were deleted.

 

[24]        From her understanding of the letter she received from the RCMP, S.N. believed that, according to the Act, the event of 1990 was deleted. That was why she responded in the negative to the question on the candidacy form (D-7) about whether she had been found guilty of an offence under the Criminal Code, since the form specified offences other than those for which a pardon had been granted.

 

[25]        It was with this in mind and without a second thought that she also signed the form (Exhibit D-8) authorizing any person to disclose to the SPCUM any information concerning her in regard to her conduct, her employee record or her health, as well as any other information deemed useful.

 

[26]        The complainant said how very disappointed she was when her candidacy was rejected after the SPCUM had agreed to re-examine her file in application of section 18.2 of the Charter and the automatic pardon granted in her case. Having made an appointment for the psychomotor test of the third stage, she was then told that she lacked "good moral character", because of the offence that was still in her record.

 

[27]        She added that she was devastated by the definitive rejection, whereas, for a year, she had thought she had a chance of being hired and would make her career in the SPCUM. She also said that the rejection meant as well that the door was closed to any career in a police force, or even in the judicial sector. It also meant she had to change the direction of her studies, since, until then, she had prepared for that career, which determined her academic choices, i.e. a certificate in delinquency and criminality, and a certificate in law.

 

[28]        She stressed her humiliation at being labelled as a person without "good moral character", especially since no one from the SPCUM had even met with her personally or conducted an investigation of the people around her. She felt she was being stigmatized by a label attributed by people who did not know her.

 

[29]        S.N. said at the hearing that she no longer wanted to be a police officer. She explained that the events surrounding her candidacy for a position with the SPCUM were humiliating, as they brought back memories of an event in the past that she thought had been forgotten.

 

[30]        In the Tribunal's view, S.N.'s credibility is in no way in doubt, particularly considering that the facts related date back more than nine years from the time of the trial, which was itself spread over 15 months, before S.N. completed her testimony. The apparent contradictions raised by counsel for the SPCUM are due primarily to a few errors in the stenographic transcription used by the attorney and the passage of time in this case. As for the values required of a police officer, which the complainant acknowledged in the case, they are certainly not in basic contradiction to her own values simply because, at 21 years of age, she committed the offence for which she is reproached.

 

2.2       THE SPCUM HIRING PROCESS

 

[31]        Nicole Cailler, SPCUM personnel selection advisor, described to the Tribunal the SPCUM's hiring process at the time of her testimony on September 14, 1999. The process consisted of eight stages: 1. verification of criminal records, 2. a medical examination, 3. a language assessment, 4. a physical abilities test, 5. a session at an assessment centre consisting of a multimeasure test/observation and an interview, 6. a reputation investigation, 7. a driving test, 8. the psychometric evaluation. The process culminated in a general evaluation of the file. Ms. Cailler indicated that, in 1995-1996, there were no specific stages for the language assessment (3) or the session at the assessment centre (5).

 

[32]        Ms. Cailler explained that the first stage was governed by Regulation 14, and more specifically, by paragraphs (b) and (c) of section 2, which read as follows:

 

2. To become a constable or a cadet of the Police Force or of a municipal Police force, a person must:

 

(a)  ...

 

(b)  be of good moral character according to the conclusions of an inquiry to be made by using the form prescribed for such purpose by the Commission particularly as regards his family, social, financial and judicial background; [My emphasis]

 

(c) never have been found guilty or pleaded guilty of an offence under the Criminal Code (R.S.C. 1970, c. C-34) upon prosecution by way of indictment, or have pleaded guilty upon an information for an offence under the Criminal Code which, according to the information, should be prosecuted by way of indictment;

 

...

 

[33]        Ms. Cailler testified that the SPCUM considered judicial background to refer to the existence of a criminal record that would result in the automatic exclusion of the candidate from the hiring process. She said that, during her 14 years in personnel selection, she was not aware of any exception having been made, apart from possible convictions for impaired driving.

 

[34]        Hiring is the responsibility of the hiring section of the personnel management division. But the inquiry into the candidate's judicial background is conducted by the professional ethics service, which is part of the internal affairs division. The investigator's recommendations are followed automatically by the hiring section. According to Ms. Cailler, that procedure explains the rejection of S.N.'s candidacy in the first stage, given the recommendations of investigator Lafrenière (D-12).

 

[35]        Ms. Cailler related that her colleague Philippe Gohier handled the file. He did the same work as she did in the hiring section. He signed and sent the standard letter in such cases (D-13). Ms. Cailler was not aware of S.N.'s new application, but, according to her, the letter of July 17, 1996 (D-13 and D-14) was consistent with the letter sent after a file was re-examined.

 

[36]        Document D-19 describes the security check, formerly called a reputation or character investigation, which was aimed at gathering and verifying information on the candidate's judicial background, creditworthiness, driver's licence, family environment, references, and academic and employment environment. Ms. Cailler explained that that security investigation cross-checked stages 1 and 6 of the hiring process. She stated that a candidate was automatically eliminated if he or she did not pass stage 1, i.e. the preliminary judicial background investigation.

 

[37]        The investigation was conducted by the internal affairs division, which was responsible for making recommendations to the hiring section. According to the witness, the hiring section always made the decision. However, in 1995, investigator Lafrenière was directly responsible to the hiring section of the personnel management division, not to the professional ethics section of the internal affairs division, which did not exist at the time.

 

[38]        Ms. Cailler explained the impact of the investigators' recommendations:

 

[TRANSLATION]

They relied on that report ... because it was ... their own work ... we were not investigators.

 

... Following that recommendation, we made the decision based on it.

 

... I could not see myself going against a decision of that nature.

 

[39]        The Tribunal notes that the aspects of the investigation listed in Exhibit D-19 cover all the prescriptions in paragraph (b) of section 2 of Regulation 14, as regards good moral character and, in terms of judicial background, make no distinction between offences prosecuted by way of summary conviction and those prosecuted by way of indictment.

 

[40]        Witness Claude Gauthier, chief inspector, who was in charge of the internal affairs division, explained that the preliminary security check consisted of two stages: (1) verification of the police information system, and (2) study of the case by the person in charge of hiring investigations. The witness indicated that, if verification of the computerized police information system (known by the French acronym SIIP) showed criminal records, the practice was to distinguish the types of criminal record. A candidacy was rejected only when a criminal offence prosecuted by way of indictment was involved.

 

[41]        In the case of an offence prosecuted by way of summary conviction, the computer investigation continued in order to determine the nature of the offence. In the case of "impaired driving" or "public mischief" offences, the facts revealed by the computer investigation determined the recommendation. The same was true of shoplifting. According to the witness, mitigating factors such as youth, pregnancy, kleptomania or illness were in the Court record. That was why Inspector Gauthier said the following:

 

[TRANSLATION]

When we looked at the Court record, we had enough information to make a decision in a case.

 

[42]        The witness also explained that the SIIP contains information about the involvement of an individual in an event as a complainant, victim, suspect or accused. With that information, one can communicate with the police force concerned, using the file number, and obtain the police record on that person. The judicial district where the person appeared can also be obtained this way, and the court record can be provided at the request of the police.

 

[43]        Mr. Gauthier indicated that, in the event of a pardon, the only information system from which the record is deleted is that of the Centre de renseignements policiers du Québec (CRPQ), i.e. the criminal identification file and its counterpart, the RCMP file. In other words, according to the witness, the deletion of the criminal record in no way affects the police information system or the court record.

 

[44]        Mr. Gauthier added that, even if there has been a pardon, information concerning an offence cannot be disregarded in determining the eligibility of a candidate in the hiring process. He said the following in this regard:

 

[TRANSLATION]

[Deletion] cannot be automatic. I do not know what is in the record, whether there is a pardon. Hence, I must go further and analyse the elements that meant … that led to a pardon … But it is the examination of the record, in the same way as all other cases, that will mean that I am going to render a decision. …

 

The fact that we have a record means that, when we query the system and it informs us that the individual committed armed robbery, or something like that, … we reject [the candidacy] automatically.

 

In some cases, we have to go further into the record, even at the preliminary stage. Hence, we do that at the preliminary stage. And if, at that stage, we decide to disregard the record, we take it up at the final stage, but with many more elements, since we will have looked at the family environment and so on.

 

[45]        Mr. Gauthier said that his service considered the investigation of "good moral character" to be essentially a security check, formerly known as a reputation investigation (Exhibit D-19). It consisted once again of checking the police information systems for any form of complaint, and checking financial institutions, the Société de l'assurance automobile du Québec, educational institutions, family environment, acquaintances, workplace and so on. Where necessary, the investigation included a meeting with the candidate. The security check led to conclusions about good moral character and to final recommendations, which were sent to the person in the hiring section who was in charge of the file.

 

[46]        In S.N.'s case, Mr. Gauthier said he was asked by the director to analyse the file in 1998. He said that, in 1995, the preliminary check was done by officer Lafrenière, who was in the hiring sector. The professional ethics division had not yet been set up.

 

[47]        According to the process that Mr. Gauthier described, officer Lafrenière first found a record number in the SIIP. That number constituted the only trace of an offence if a pardon was granted. Officer Lafrenière contacted the police force concerned, sent it the authorization for disclosure of information signed by S.N. and thereby obtained the police record concerning her (Exhibit D-10). That record contained exhibits D-1, D-2, D-3, D-4 and D-5. He also obtained the court minute-book and the decision rendered in 1991 (exhibits D-6 and D-11). After examining those documents, he recommended to Mr. Gohier that the candidacy be rejected (exhibits D-12 and D-13). Furthermore, according to the witness, [TRANSLATION] "Mr. Gohier, who was in charge of that file, made his decision".

 

[48]        Mr. Gauthier explained that, after the file was studied for the second time, in 1998, he recommended that the decision be maintained. He justified that by saying:

 

[TRANSLATION]

The established criteria are based on those applied to our police officers. A police officer about whom a criminal complaint for shoplifting is filed, whether he is guilty or not when the disciplinary process is over, if we recognize that his conduct is a breach of standards, the bond of trust is broken and he is dismissed and fired.

 

...

 

Therefore, if I want to be consistent, before taking someone into the police force who has shoplifted in that way, if he had been a police officer, I would have fired him, so I'm not about to hire him.

 

[49]        Mr. Gauthier added that the modus operandi of the person at the time the shoplifting occurred is decisive. According to him, the act, i.e. the evidentiary facts of the commission of the offence, are scrutinized, not the person. The age of the person is taken into consideration only if there are doubts about the modus operandi, but, in his opinion, at 21 years of age, a person knows what he or she is doing. So in S.N.'s case, it was not necessary to investigate beyond the evidentiary facts of the offence, as they were described in the documents (D-1, D-2, D-3, D-4, D-5 and D-10).

 

[50]        In cross-examination, Mr. Gauthier acknowledged that S.N.'s criminal record was deleted on December 30, 1992, and he said:

 

[TRANSLATION]

In my opinion, that means that the criminal record kept by the forensic identification section of the RCMP and the criminal record that may be kept by our forensic identification section were destroyed, both the fingerprints and the content.

 

[51]        He added that all that remains is a number preceded by the letters FPS, which mean that an empty file exists. With regard to the preliminary investigation process for hiring, Mr. Gauthier declared that, regardless of the reasons for the deletion of the criminal record, the investigator is unable to report a case of automatic exclusion or recommend such exclusion, since he is unaware what the case involved, as long as he has not taken cognizance of the facts. Therefore, he has to continue his investigation of the facts related to the existence of that record before making his recommendation.

 

[52]        In S.N.'s case, the decision was therefore based on the police reports concerning the offence she committed in 1991 and provided by the police force concerned. No other element of the security check was verified either by officer Lafrenière in 1995 or by Mr. Gauthier when the file was reviewed in 1998.

 

[53]        Lastly, Mr. Gauthier reiterated that the hiring decision was up to the hiring advisor. The investigator, whether he was from the professional ethics section, as now, or from the hiring section, as in 1995, can only make a recommendation.

 

2.3       THE PROCESS OF OBTAINING A PARDON

 

[54]         Colette Galipeau, of the Pardon Division of the National Parole Board, explained that, prior to the 1992 amendments, the National Parole Board was responsible for the investigation to determine the merits of an application for a pardon. Once the investigation was over and the recommendation for a pardon was made, it was up to the Solicitor General to recommend to the Governor in Council that a pardon be granted.

 

[55]        Ms. Galipeau pointed out the following:

 

[TRANSLATION]

A pardon is official recognition that a person who was found guilty of an offence served his sentence, say in the form of probation, payment of a fine or imprisonment, and has paid his penalty, and is now ... can now be acknowledged to be a law-abiding citizen.

 

... Being found guilty and receiving a pardon should not tarnish that person's reputation.

 

[56]        Ms. Galipeau explained that, prior to 1992, the National Parole Board conducted two distinct types of investigations, depending on whether the criminal offences were prosecuted by way of indictment or by way of summary conviction. At that time, anyone found guilty had to file an application for a pardon by completing a form to that effect accompanied by that person's criminal record and a list of references. In the case of indictable offences, the RCMP interviewed the applicant and the applicant's employer, and a community investigation was conducted, which was not the case for an offence prosecuted by way of summary conviction, for which merely the SIIPs were checked. The situation was the same for people given an absolute discharge or a conditional discharge, since, during that period, the witness said, the person was found guilty of the offence and a sentence was handed down. Hence, the investigation was, in those cases as well, determined according to the method of prosecution.

 

[57]        After 1992, pursuant to amendments to the Criminal Records Act,[4] cases giving rise to a pardon under the Criminal Code no longer required an application for parole. In fact, in situations where the person was found guilty but was not sentenced, since the decision was a pardon, that person could no longer file an application for a pardon under the Act. Rather, the Act provided that information about the conviction had to be deleted from the record automatically, either one year after the decision if the pardon was an absolute discharge, or three years later, if it was a conditional discharge, regardless of the method of prosecution.

 

[58]        Convicted persons still have to file an application for a pardon and provide the required supporting documents. Three types of investigations are conducted, depending on the nature of offence, which determines the type of prosecution. The type of prosecution determines both the investigation and the decision-making authority to issue or grant a pardon. The first case is an offence prosecuted by way of summary conviction. The application for a pardon results in a simple check with the RCMP and, in the absence of other convictions, it gives rise to a direct decision by the officer in charge of the record to issue a pardon.

 

[59]        The second case concerns a criminal offence or a hybrid violation prosecuted by way of indictment. In those cases, the application for a pardon triggers a check with the RCMP, but also with the Canadian Police Information Centre (CPIC) and the Personal Information Retrieval System (PIRS) of the RCMP, which contains all kinds of information on a person's conduct. The investigation is supplemented by a check with any police forces involved. In the absence of relevant elements, a recommendation is made by the officer in charge to the commissioners of the National Parole Board, which either grants or does not grant a pardon.

 

[60]        In cases of criminal offences prosecuted by way of indictment, the investigation also included, until 1997, a community check and a check with the employer. Since that date, there has been no investigation apart from a check of police information systems. It is up to the applicant to provide a complete file with all relevant documents, including the court record and an attestation by the local police covering the applicant's previous five years of residence. Once the study of the file is over, a recommendation is made to the commissioners to grant or not to grant a pardon.

 

[61]        Ms. Galipeau explained that there is also a royal prerogative of mercy, for those who are not eligible for a pardon under the Criminal Records Act. However, as she is part of the pardon division, she cannot testify on that subject, since she does not know much about it. She also said that anyone convicted of a crime set forth in the Criminal Code can apply for a pardon in the five years following the end of the period covered by the sentence. Only people who have been sentenced to life in prison are not eligible to file an application for a pardon.

 

[62]        Ms. Galipeau indicated that, under the Criminal Records Act, a pardon is granted on the basis of the applicant's good conduct since the end of the sentence and the applicant's criminal record as a whole, not on the basis of the conviction or the victim, since there has already been a judicial investigation and sentencing in regard to those two elements. Therefore, the reason for the conviction serves only to establish the nature of the offence, which, in turn, determines the type of investigation and the jurisdiction to dispose of the application.

 

[63]        Lastly, Ms. Galipeau said that, on the basis of the documents filed[5] (exhibits D-25, D-29 and D-30), the number of applications for a pardon, after increasing constantly between 1988 and 1995, stabilized at around 22 000 a year. Pardons granted by the Governor in Council numbered some 11 000 to 9000 a year, whereas from 1992 to 1998, their total varied from 23 000 to 12 000 granted by the National Parole Board for criminal or hybrid offences, to which must be added about 5000 pardons granted in cases of offences prosecuted by way of summary conviction. Since 1998, there have been around 5000 pardons granted and nearly 3000 issued, given the new obligations imposed on applicants in assembling their files. She also mentioned that the number of refusals is higher, i.e. more than double the number prior to 1992. She pointed out that the process has become more demanding, particularly because an additional waiting period of one year is required before an application can be filed.

3.1.      THE PROTECTION OF SECTION 18.2 OF THE CHARTER

 

[70]        In Therrien,[10] the Supreme Court of Canada analysed section 18.2 of the Charter. Gonthier J. wrote the following on behalf of the Court:

 

[140] … Section 18.2 provides that no one may dismiss, refuse to hire or otherwise penalize a person in his employment owing to the mere fact that he was convicted of a penal or criminal offence, if the offence was in no way connected with the employment or if the person has obtained a pardon for the offence. Whether this section applies will depend on whether four essential conditions are met: (1) a dismissal, a refusal to hire or any kind of penalty; (2) in the person's employment; (3) owing to the mere fact that the person was convicted of a penal or criminal offence; (4) if the offence was in no way connected with the employment or if the person has obtained a pardon for the offence ...

 

[71]        After noting the restrictive nature of section 18.2 in that the provision does not have an unlimited scope given that it does not apply to the issuing of permits to practice by professional orders, Gonthier J. broached the third and fourth conditions of the implementation of section 18.2. Considering the particular circumstances in Therrien, in which the notion of employment relates to the function of a judge, only the analysis of the fourth condition is relevant to the present case. In this regard, Gonthier J. wrote:

 

[144] … This condition in fact contains two separate conditions: (a) the offence was in no way connected with the employment, or (b) regardless of any connection with the employment, the person has obtained a pardon for the offence. The appellant falls within the second situation since on August 20, 1987, the Governor General granted him a pardon under the Criminal Records Act and since the Quebec Charter makes no distinction based on which type of pardon may have been obtained.

 

[145] The second situation is one in which no exception to the application of that provision is possible. If a pardon has been granted and the other three conditions are also met, the probable finding will be that there was discrimination against the person who received the pardon. It should be noted that s. 20, which provides that a distinction based on the aptitudes or qualifications required for an employment is deemed non-discriminatory, has no application in respect of s. 18.2. Section 18.2 is a self-contained provision and has its own rules governing exceptions. This internal justification mechanism would otherwise duplicate the mechanism in s. 20... [My emphasis]

 

[72]        In another case, the Court of Appeal also studied the interpretation of section 18.2 in the particular context of imprisonment resulting from a sentence. Dussault J. said the following in Maksteel:[11]

 

[TRANSLATION]

[46] It is true that section 18.2 pursues an objective similar to that of section 10 in that it is also aimed at maintaining equality, in this case in labour relations, by prohibiting discrimination against "convicted" persons, and that it also promotes the integration of these persons into the labour market. The clarity of its wording, which provides that "No one may dismiss … a person in his employment owing to the mere fact that he was convicted of a penal or criminal offence … [My emphasis], prohibits me, however, from interpreting it in the manner that the respondent wants. The legislator's intention is unequivocal, and no liberal interpretation can be allowed to contradict such a clear text.

 

[47] In my opinion, the protection afforded by section 18.2 of the Charter does not extend to a dismissal the real reason for which was the employee's unavailability because of imprisonment, since, in that case, it cannot be concluded that the dismissal was the result of the mere fact that the employee was convicted. On the one hand, there is indeed another reason for the dismissal. On the other, there is no direct link between the dismissal and the conviction, a ground protected by the Charter, since they are separated by one element, i.e. imprisonment (B.C. Human Rights Commission v. B.C. Human Rights Tribunal, 2000 BCCA 584). [Emphasis in the text]

 

…

 

[50] Hence, unless it is proven that the conviction constitutes the only ground for dismissal, section 18.2 of the Charter can be of no help to an employee who is prevented from working because of his imprisonment. In that case, a liberal interpretation, similar to that allowed in regard to the grounds of section 10, would clash violently with the very wording of the provision. The fact that section 18.2 was inserted in the Charter a few years after it came into force clearly shows, in fact, that the legislator intentionally restricted its scope. It was [TRANSLATION] "after the courts had decided that criminal records were not covered by the terms "social condition" in section 10 of the Charter (Commission des droits de la personne v. Cie Price ltée, S.C. Roberval, No. 155-05-000044-819, July 24, 1981, Bernier J., J.E. 81-866 (S.C.); Commission des droits de la personne v. Montréal (Ville de), S.C. Mtl., No. 500-05-13180-805, June 25, 1982, Blanchet J., J.E. 82-662 (S.C.); Mercier v. Beauport (Ville de), [1981] P.C. 292) that the legislator added section 18.2 in the early 1980s" (Syndicat du textile de Montmagny inc.).

 

[73]        According to the teachings of the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal, section 18.2 constitutes a complete regime of prohibited discrimination that is distinct from that enshrined in section 10 of the Charter.

 

[74]        On the one hand, the relevant provisions clearly indicate that the ground was not incorporated into the exhaustive list in section 10, but into a provision that exempts it from the broad interpretation of the grounds developed under section 10. This means that, in regard to the ground contemplated by section 18.2, namely, a conviction for an offence, it can bear only on all the components of that conviction for a penal or criminal offence.

 

[75]        On the other, section 18.2 specifically governs the decisions of the employer, which can base a decision to dismiss an employee, to refuse to hire him or her, or to exact any penalty in the framework of the employment on section 18.2 only if the offence is directly connected with the employment. Furthermore, if the person has received a pardon, regardless of the type, the employer very simply cannot take into account the ground set forth in 18.2 in its decisions without practising prohibited discrimination in regard to that person. According to the Supreme Court itself in paragraph 145 of Therrien, in applying section 18.2 in the case of a pardon, no exception is possible. Therefore, one can in no way deviate from the rule prohibiting discrimination in such cases.

 

[76]        Hence, the hiring processes and other decisions of the employer surrounding consideration of the ground of discrimination protected by section 18.2 must be examined in light of the prescriptions of that section and of the rules of interpretation developed by the Supreme Court with a view to ensuring respect for fundamental rights. As Gonthier J. wrote in Béliveau-St-Jacques:[12]

 

Like the statutes that are its counterparts in the other provinces, the Charter … has a special quasi-constitutional status. Certain of its provisions thus have relative primacy, resulting from s. 52. By its very nature, such a statute calls for a large and liberal interpretation that allows its objectives to be achieved as far as possible. In this sense, not only the provisions at issue but the entire statute must be examined.

 

[77]        More recently, in Mercier, L’Heureux-Dubé J. recalled the whole of the principles of interpretation applicable to the Charter:

 

The Court has also held that because of its quasi-constitutional status, the Charter must be interpreted in light of both its context and objectives.[13]

 

[78]        Among the chief objectives of the Charter are the right to dignity, to equality and to protection from discrimination, as the Supreme Court[14] has recognized many times and as the preamble to the Charter indicates.[15]

 

[79]        The specific objective of section 18.2 is to provide protection from discrimination in employment because of a conviction unrelated to the employment. Hence, that specific objective is in keeping with the more general objectives of the Charter, particularly the protection of the right to equality.

 

[80]        In keeping with respect for those rules, it is appropriate to establish the scope and specific limits of the protection afforded by section 18.2 and to determine their application to the present case.

 

[81]        First, the question related to the third condition (merely because a person has been convicted of a penal or criminal offence) of the implementation of section 18.2 must be answered. What components of the conviction for a penal or criminal offence are protected by section 18.2 in the case of a refusal to hire (condition #1) in the framework of the employment (condition #2)?

 

[82]        That section sets forth two indissociable elements: the conviction and the offence at the root of that conviction. A distinction must be made between those two elements, since condition #3 mentions the conviction, whereas in condition #4, the prohibition of discrimination is lifted if there is a connection between the offence for which the person was convicted and the employment. Except in the case of pardons, the prohibition against discrimination provided for in section 18.2 is therefore lifted if the offence is related to the employment. Hence, it is important to take these two elements into consideration in examining that section.

 

[83]        That provision clearly shows that any conviction is a protected situation in the context of dismissal, hiring or other conditions of employment. Hence, the mere fact of being convicted cannot justify any decision by the employer concerning an employee or a candidate for a job without violating his or her right to equality in employment. Such a decision is discriminatory in regard to that person.

 

[84]        Within the meaning of section 18.2, a conviction is indissociable from the offence committed. They are two components of the protected ground, since had there been no penal or criminal offence, there would not have been a conviction. This is also the conclusion of the Court of Appeal of British Columbia in McLaughlin. That decision dealt with, among other things, the interpretation of the expression "that person has been convicted of a criminal offence or summary conviction offence that is unrelated to the employment" in section 13(1) of the Human Rights Code[16] of British Columbia. Hollinrake J. wrote:

 

[10]      With respect, I do not think one can separate "conviction" and "offence". Assuming the appellant is correct that words "unrelated to the employment" modify the word "offence", the fact is that it is the offence that the complainant must be convicted of, which is "unrelated to the employment". It cannot be said that the "offence" is unrelated or, to put it another way, not connected with the employment when it is the offence that leads directly to the conviction and the subsequent sentence of an individual. In other words, the "offence" must be one for which there is a conviction. ...[17]

 

[85]        The Québec legislator has also recognized that the employer has a ground of defence confined to the connection between the offence that resulted in the conviction and the employment. In the absence of a connection between the two, discrimination is prohibited.

 

[86]        Hence, the third condition of the application of section 18.2 (merely because a person was convicted of a penal or criminal offence), identified by Gonthier J., includes both the conviction and the offence committed. Those two essential elements are indissociable for the purpose of the implementation of the protection from discrimination guaranteed by section 18.2.

 

[87]        Accordingly, an employer cannot claim an absence of discrimination because it took into consideration in its decision only the offence, not the conviction. You can't have one without the other. If there has been a pardon, the employer cannot base its decision on the facts or the nature of the offence while claiming not to have considered the conviction.

 

[88]        In short, an employer can consider the nature of the offence provided that its decision is based on the fact that the offence is related to the employment. In accordance with section 18.2, an employer cannot invoke that justification in situations where a candidate or an employee has been granted a pardon.

 

3.2.      THE NOTION OF PARDON

 

[89]        But what about the notion of pardon? In Therrien, the Supreme Court examined its meaning and scope. Gonthier J. pointed out the following:

 

[113] At common law, a pardon is an expression of the sovereignty of the monarch, the result of the unilateral and discretionary exercise of the Royal prerogative of mercy or clemency. In Canada, a pardon is also derived from the powers of the Crown. ...

 

[114] Professor Dumont breaks the various types of pardon found in the Criminal Code down into the following categories: (1) the ordinary and partial pardon provided in ss. 748(1) and 748.1(1) of the Code, which consists of the remission, in whole or in part, of a sentence without reviewing the issue of the person's guilt; (2) the conditional pardon granted under s. 748(2) of the Code, which can amend the initial sentence imposed by the court and make it subject to certain conditions; (3) the free pardon also granted under subss. 748(2) and (3) of the Code, by virtue of which a person is deemed never to have committed the offence in respect of which it is granted, and (4) the pardon granted after a referral for hearing or referral to a court of appeal in accordance with s. 690 of the Code or s. 53 S.C.A., which results in a new trial or a new hearing.[18]

 

[90]        The Supreme Court noted that a pardon can also stem from federal jurisdiction over criminal law:

 

[115] Also, Parliament may legislate regarding pardons in the exercise of its jurisdiction over criminal law. For example, it has established a procedure for administrative pardons, under the exclusive authority of the National Parole Board, which is set out in the Criminal Records Act. A pardon of that nature may be granted, after inquiry, to any applicant who has been convicted of an offence under a federal Act or regulations thereunder. … What then is the effect of this kind of pardon? More specifically, did it mean [complete denial] of the existence of the conviction in respect of which it was granted …

 

[116] Sections 5 and 6(2) C.R.A. set out the effects of granting a pardon: (1) it is evidence that the National Parole Board, after making the inquiries specified in the Act, was satisfied that the applicant was of good conduct and that the conviction in respect of which it is granted should no longer reflect adversely on his character; (2) it vacates the conviction and removes any disqualification to which the person is subject by virtue of any federal Act or regulation made thereunder; and (3) it results in any record of the conviction being kept separate and apart: in other words, the criminal record is expunged. In and of themselves, these provisions do not persuade me that the pardon can operate to retroactively wipe out the conviction. Rather, they are an expression of the fact that it still exists, combined with a desire to minimize its future consequences. Section 5(a)(ii) C.R.A. provides that the pardon is evidence that "the conviction in respect of which the pardon is granted or issued should no longer reflect adversely on the applicant's character" (emphasis added), implying that it still exists and could so reflect. Second, the effects of the pardon are limited to the legal disqualifications created by federal statutes or the regulations thereunder and therefore exclude all the post-sentence consequences provided in provincial legislation, which also suggests that the pardon has only limited effect. Third, the information contained in the criminal record is not destroyed but is kept separate and apart, whence it may re-emerge should the pardoned person subsequently be no longer of good conduct.[19]

 

[91]        The Gonthier J. compared the French and English versions of the Criminal Records Act, and concluded as follows:

 

[117] … "to make void in law, to deprive of legal authority, validity, force, efficacy or value, to render inoperative or to annul or cancel", which does not necessarily involve retroactive effects. Thus, it is possible to make something void, deprive it of any effect or authority or annul it for the future only. It is therefore highly probable that the amendments to the French version were intended only to better convey the meaning of the English version.

 

[119] … Apart from the various disqualifications that a conviction may lead to under federal statutes or regulations, as referred to in s. 5, the other "conséquences" associated with the existence of a criminal record that the pardon sought to eliminate for the future are all covered by keeping the information associated with the existence of a criminal record separate and apart. That information then becomes private to the individual and cannot be disclosed without the prior approval of the Minister. [My emphasis]

 

[120] …The purpose of all these sections is to eliminate the potential future effects of the conviction, which would be pointless if the conviction were deemed never to have existed. Section 8 is particularly instructive for our purposes. If the very essence of the pardon were that the pardoned person could deny the existence of the conviction, Parliament would not have felt the need to enact that provision.

 

[121] …Thus, as I have already pointed out, s. 748(3) of the Criminal Code expressly provides that where the Governor in Council grants a free pardon to any person, that person shall be deemed thereafter never to have committed the offence in respect of which the pardon is granted. Furthermore, s. 36(1) of the Young Offenders Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. Y-1, expressly provides that the finding of guilt relating to a young offender for whom the court has directed an absolute discharge or for whom all the dispositions and all their terms have ceased to have effect shall be deemed never to have existed. The Criminal Records Act contains no provision of such scope. The fact that Parliament opted instead to use the term "réhabilitation" in the French version of the Act, thus stressing the future effects of the law, was therefore not random. [My emphasis][20]

 

[92]        And Gonthier J. again cited Professor Dumont:

 

[122] … [TRANSLATION] It seems clear that the Criminal Records Act grants a pardon which is designed only to put an end to the negative effects of a conviction. An administrative pardon, which adopts the features of a partial and conditional pardon, is not equivalent to a retroactive acquittal, as a free pardon may be, by virtue of the Royal prerogative or the Criminal Code; accordingly, an administrative pardon does not logically result in retroactive annulment or neutralization of the conviction.[21]

 

[93]        Lastly, Gonthier J. wrote:

 

[127] I therefore find that while a pardon does not make the past go away, it expunges consequences for the future. The integrity of the pardoned person is restored, and he or she need not suffer the effects associated with the conviction in an arbitrary or discriminatory manner, a situation which the Canadian and Quebec charters tend to protect against. ... [My emphasis]

 

[94]        What about sections 730 and 731 of the Criminal Code, which grant the judge the discretion required to directly discharge the convicted person by means of an order? More specifically, is a person discharged within the meaning of those provisions contemplated by the notion of pardon in section 18.2 of the Charter?

 

[95]        As the Supreme Court noted in Therrien, the federal legislator can legislate in matters of pardons pursuant to its jurisdiction in criminal law. The Court recognized that this is true in regard to the Criminal Records Act, which establishes administrative pardons. But in the case of discharges, the federal legislator intervened in 1992 through the adoption of provisions in the Criminal Code and the Criminal Records Act. Before that date, discharge existed[22] but was known as parole or conditional discharge.

 

[96]        Professor Dumont wrote on the subject of discharge that it constitutes: [TRANSLATION] "a sui generis institution with the characteristics of both acquittal and conviction".[23] She pointed out that:

 

[TRANSLATION]

… Daily practice before the courts shows that it is essentially to prevent certain offenders from suffering the consequences of a criminal record that judges grant a discharge. In fact, that impression is contemporary with the birth of that measure in 1972. Commentators vaunting the merits of the measure affirmed that it was intended precisely to prevent offenders from suffering the detrimental effects of a criminal record, then considered the most concrete stigma of a conviction. … the Criminal Records Act was actually amended when the discharge measure was created, and affirmed that the measure was deemed to be a conviction for the purpose of the existence of a criminal record.[24]

 

[97]        But the new section 730(3) of the Criminal Code now says that the offender is "deemed not to have been convicted to the offence". However, as Professor Dumont indicates, and despite that legal presumption tending to prevent the constitution of a criminal record:

 

[TRANSLATION]

However, in 1992, the legislator persisted in the intent to attribute a criminal record to a discharged person … It still had to be affirmed that, even if a person was discharged, he or she still had a criminal record, since the Criminal Code included this penological measure in its penalty and sentence concepts in Part XXIII.

 

But that same legislator decided to allow the automatic deletion, simply through the lapse of time, of the mention in a criminal record that a person was discharged absolutely or conditionally. The person to whom the penological measure applied no longer had to file a motion to have that mention deleted from his or her criminal record. ... Basically, the legislative amendment meant the automatic administrative deletion from the criminal record of the mention of absolute or conditional discharge, whereas for other types of convictions to be deleted from a criminal record, an application for a pardon for the convicted person had to be filed. Hence, the fact that a person has been granted a discharge is indicated in the criminal record administered by the CPIC. Deletion from the criminal record of that indication is the result of a legal pardon through the lapse of time. It must therefore be concluded that the presumption of non-conviction set forth in the Criminal Code for the benefit of the person who has been discharged does not prevent the police from maintaining a record and therefore does not protect the convicted person from the legal stigma of a criminal record. ...[25]

 

[98]        Given that a discharge is the most lenient sentence provided for in the Criminal Code, it is logical that it be contemplated by a legal pardon like that under section 6.1 of the Criminal Records Act. In short, that provision laid down by the federal legislator pursuant to the jurisdiction to legislate in criminal law matters supplements the discharge measure in order to give the presumption of non-conviction its true meaning, since, as Béliveau J. wrote in Rozon,[26] [TRANSLATION] "discharge is a measure that goes further than a pardon". Furthermore, and in accordance with the teachings of the Supreme Court, there is no doubt that that is one type of pardon covered by section 18.2.

 

 

3.3       SECTION 18.2 AND THE HIRING OF A POLICE OFFICER

 

[99]        In this case, a final question must be examined, to wit, what effect does section 18.2 have on hiring standards for police officers?

 

[100]     The SPCUM contended that that provision does not apply to police officers, since theirs is a function considered a "profession" by the Québec legislator in the Police Act, as amended in June 2000.[27] Apart from the principle of the non-retroactivity of a legislative amendment prior to the facts in the present case, and without ruling on the effect of the new legislation on section 18.2 of the Charter, the Tribunal notes that, in any case, two arguments result in the rejection of that claim. The first concerns the notion of "profession" and the second stems from the provisions of the Police Act and other legislation applicable to police officers.

 

[101]     Like authors Dumont[28] and Singleton,[29] Gonthier noted in Therrien the gaps in section 18.2 in regard to professions that may not be included in the expression "employment". He wrote the following:

 

[142] … I find it to be particularly instructive that s. 45 of the Professional Code provides, with respect to all the professions it governs, that any Bureau established within a professional order may refuse to issue a permit or to enter on the roll any applicant who has been the subject of a decision finding him guilty of a criminal offence which is related to the practice of the profession, unless he has obtained a pardon. This provision would seem to supplement the protection provided by the Quebec Charter in respect of employment and to fill the gaps it left. Thus, having regard to the choice to use the word "employment" in s. 18.2, I am of the view that it was not the legislature's intent to include the judiciary. ...[30]

 

[102]     Therefore, in contrast to the contentions of the SPCUM, the notion of "profession", allegedly excluded from the expression "employment", concerns a professional, autonomous and independent practice governed by the Professional Code[31] and other professional laws, and which results from the issuing of a permit to practice. Police officers are not contemplated by this set of legislative provisions. This is particularly conclusive given Gonthier J.'s analysis of the qualification for the office of judge in regard to the expression "employment":

 

[141] … These findings in fact reflect the constitutional reality of judicial office, which requires that judges not be subject to any bureaucratic higher authority, or be a party to any relationship of subordination such as is traditionally characteristic of the employer-employee relationship, … In addition, although judges perform their functions for remuneration, their financial security is one of the three essential conditions of judicial independence … allowing avoidance of any possibility of real or perceived interference by the Executive. … Finally, the hiring and firing process for judges is substantially different from the normal procedure used for most employments …

 

[103]     In short, a relationship of subordination through remuneration, associated with a hiring and dismissal process specific to labour relations, characterizes, according to Gonthier J., the term "employment" used by the legislator in the Charter. In the case of police officers, it is clear that that is what is involved, particularly under the Police Act and the other laws applicable to police officers.

 

[104]     Specifically in section 3, that legislation provides for the hiring conditions of police officers, which are supplemented by Regulation 14, which concerns hiring standards. In addition, police officers do not have absolute immunity during their employment, as recognized by the Court of Appeal in Péloquin:[32]

 

 

 

[TRANSLATION]

[68] A peace officer who commits a criminal offence does not have absolute immunity but is subject to other systems of control established specifically by the legislator.

 

[69] A peace officer (police officer) is subject to a disciplinary system and a system of ethics. The interpretation given section 3 of the Police Act must be in keeping with the other laws applicable to peace officers.

 

[70] Section 169 of the Act respecting police organization provides for the following:

 

Every municipality has the power required to adopt a body of rules relating to the internal discipline applicable to the members of its police force.

 

…

 

[71] In accordance with section 169 of the Act respecting police organization, the Communauté urbaine de Montréal adopted By-law 106-114, which provides for the following in particular:

 

At all times, the police officer shall demonstrate dignity and avoid any deportment tending to undermine the confidence or consideration his duties require or to compromise the efficiency of the department. ...

 

[72] Section 28 of the By-law provides for the following:

 

[28] A disciplinary committee is required to accept as proof of his guilt a duly certified copy of any final decision of a Canadian court finding a police officer guilty of a criminal offence; it may then impose one or several of the penalties provided for under Article 30.

 

This article also applies to any decision of a foreign court finding a police officer guilty of an act which, had it been committed in Canada, would have constituted a criminal offence.

 

[30] A disciplinary committee may impose one or several of the following penalties for each charge:

 

a) a warning;

 

b) a reprimand;

 

c) a disciplinary transfer;

 

d) a disciplinary suspension without pay for a period not exceeding 60 working days;

 

e) demotion;

 

f) dismissal.

 

[73] Sections 125 and 130 of the Act respecting police organization also provide for the following:

 

125. The commissioner shall submit to the committee, by way of a citation, every final decision of a Canadian court convicting a police officer of a criminal offence constituting a transgression of the Code of ethics.

 

…

 

130. Where the ethics committee comes to the decision that the conduct of a police officer is a transgression of the Code of ethics, it may, within 14 days after the date of the decision, impose on the police officer, for each count, one of the following penalties which may, where applicable, be consecutive:

 

(1) a warning;

(2) a reprimand;

(2.1) a rebuke;

(3) a suspension without salary for a period not exceeding 60 working days;

(4) a demotion;

(5) dismissal.

 

In addition, where a penalty cannot be imposed on a police officer because he has resigned, has been dismissed or has retired, the police officer may be declared disqualified from exercising the functions of a peace officer for a period of not more than five years.[33]

 

[105]     In short, it is clear from that series of provisions that a police officer holds "employment" within the meaning of section 18.2 of the Charter and that that section applies in the framework of dismissal or refusal to hire, or other conditions of employment of a police officer of the SPCUM. In fact, Robert J. of the Court of Appeal recognized that clearly in regard to section 3 of the Police Act:

 

[TRANSLATION]

[80] In regard to section 3 of the Police Act, I believe it must be concluded that, at the time of hiring, a conviction for a criminal offence by way of indictment is directly connected with the employment of a peace officer, unless the person was granted a pardon.[34]

 

[106]     Now the effect of section 18.2 of the Charter on the application of the Police Act and the By-law respecting standards of the Sûreté du Québec and municipal police forces for the hiring of constables and cadets remains to be examined. It should first be pointed out that neither the Act nor Regulation 14 is contested here under the Charter. The problem posed is that of a discriminatory effect, within the meaning of section 18.2, resulting from the hiring process carried out in application of paragraph (b) of section 2 of Regulation 14, since the SPCUM concluded that paragraph (c) and paragraph (3) of section 3 of the Act (c. P-13) were inapplicable because of the means of prosecution, i.e. by way of summary conviction, as shown in Exhibit D-12.

 

[107]     Therefore, the question now to be asked is what, in light of section 18.2, are the limits imposed on an employer by the legislator in the hiring process. To answer this question, we must bear in mind certain elements on which the Court of Appeal has already ruled in Péloquin. Apart from paragraph 80, quoted above, the Court of Appeal said the following in determining the role of a grievances arbitrator in the interpretation of section 18.2 as regards section 3 of the Police Act:

 

[TRANSLATION]

[81] During employment, the arbitrator in the case or the other administrative decision-makers must decide, taking into consideration the relevant factors, whether the conviction was directly related to the employment and therefore renders dismissal inevitable.

 

[82] In doing so, they must consider the gravity of the offence, whether the offence was committed in the course of duties or in other circumstances, and especially whether the guilty verdict allowed the peace officer to continue to merit the trust of the public, an essential element in his or her role in maintaining public order.[35]

 

[108]     First, the legislator explicitly establishes, in section 3 of the Police Act, that a conviction for a criminal offence prosecuted by way of indictment is directly related to the employment. In such a case, the employer's ground of defence is clear and leaves no room for interpretation. Therefore, the failure to hire a candidate who was convicted of a criminal offence prosecuted by way of indictment would inevitably be considered non-discriminatory under the Charter.

 

[109]     However, Péloquin[36] shows that the case of a person who received a pardon is an exception. In this regard, Robert J. wrote:

 

[TRANSLATION]

… conviction for a criminal offence through prosecution by way of indictment is directly related to the employment … except if the person has been granted a pardon.

 

[110]     That excerpt clearly shows the distinction that section 18.2 imposes between a case in which the defence ground of the connection with the employment applies and a case where it does not, i.e. where there has been a pardon.

 

[111]     That interpretation was confirmed by the Supreme Court in Therrien. Gonthier J. wrote in that regard:

 

[145] The second situation is one in which no exception to the application of that provision is possible. If a pardon has been granted and the other three conditions are also met, the probable finding will be that there was discrimination against the person who received the pardon. It should be noted that s. 20, which provides that a distinction based on the aptitudes or qualifications required for an employment is deemed non-discriminatory, has no application in respect of s. 18.2. Section 18.2 is a self-contained provision and has its own rules governing exceptions. This internal justification mechanism would otherwise duplicate the mechanism in s. 20 ... .[37]

 

[112]     In other words, a person convicted of a criminal offence prosecuted by way of indictment and who has been granted a pardon for that offence cannot be considered to fail to meet the hiring conditions provided for in paragraph (3) of section 3 of the Police Act or in paragraph (c) of section 2 of Regulation 14, which contains essentially the same conditions. Concluding otherwise would be in direct opposition to the Charter, especially since the legislator did not choose to exempt those provisions from application of the Charter, as he could have under section 52. That section of the Charter obliges the legislator to explicitly set forth his intention of derogating from the Charter.

 

[113]     In light of the above, it is entirely fair to maintain that an employer cannot automatically exclude a candidate from the hiring process for that reason alone. What about the case of a person who committed an offence other than those contemplated in paragraph (c) of section 2 of Regulation 14?

 

[114]     The response is found in regard to "good moral character", i.e. in application of paragraph (b) of section 2. That paragraph contains the employer's obligation to examine the judicial background of the candidate among other things. However, the employer must always do so according to the prescriptions of section 18.2 of the Charter. This means that, at that stage in the hiring process, the employer can reject a candidate because of an offence connected with the employment.

 

[115]     In contrast, [TRANSLATION] "if that offence has no connection with the employment", the refusal to hire the candidate is discriminatory and violates the Charter. The same is obviously true in the case of a person who was granted a pardon for the offence in question.

 

[116]      If the Charter provides "absolute" protection for a person who is granted a pardon for a criminal offence prosecuted by way of indictment, there is all the more reason to conclude that a person granted a pardon for a lesser offence is at least equally protected against the discrimination prohibited by the Charter.

 

[117]     Therefore, in the case of a person granted a pardon within the meaning of section 18.2 of the Charter, regardless of whether the offence that the person committed or of which he or she was convicted is contemplated by the hiring standards prescribed in Regulation 14, there very simply can be no examination of the connection between the offence and the employment without violating the right protected by section 18.2, since the offence and the conviction form an indissociable unit.

 

3.4       CONSENT TO THE INVESTIGATION AND THE PROTECTION OF SECTION 18.2

 

[118]     The SPCUM contended that, by signing the consent for the disclosure of information, S.N. waived the confidentiality of her criminal record and, hence, the protection of 18.2. Is this so?

 

[119]     In Dickason,[38] the Supreme Court declared that the fundamental rights set forth in human rights legislation cannot be waived because that would be contrary to public order:

 

This Court has consistently held that agreements which discriminate contrary to human rights codes are invalid.

 

 

[120]     It supported that statement by citing the following excerpts that have marked the consistent jurisprudence of the Supreme Court. The first is drawn from Etobicoke,[39] in which McIntyre J. made the following observations:

 

Although the Code contains no explicit restriction on such contracting out, it is nevertheless a public statute and it constitutes public policy in Ontario as appears from a reading of the Statute itself and as declared in the preamble. It is clear from the authorities, both in Canada and in England, that parties are not competent to contract themselves out of the provisions of such enactments and that contracts having such effect are void, as contrary to public policy. ….

 

The Ontario Human Rights Code has been enacted by the Legislature of the Province of Ontario for the benefit of the community at large and of its individual members and clearly falls within that category of enactment which may not be waived or varied by private contract; therefore this argument cannot receive effect.

 

 

[121]     In Heerspink,[40] Lamer J. wrote in regard to the British Columbia Human Rights Code:

 

Furthermore, as it [the Human Rights Code] is a public and fundamental law, no one, unless clearly authorized by law to do so, may contractually agree to suspend its operation and thereby put oneself beyond the reach of its protection.

 

 

[122]     In Craton,[41] McIntyre J. declared on behalf of the Court:

 

There is no merit in the argument raised below, but not pressed in this Court, that the parties by agreeing to article 14 in the Collective Bargaining Agreement have contracted themselves out of the provisions of s. 6(1). The Human Rights Act is legislation declaring public policy and may not be avoided by private contract.

 

[123]     In concluding this overview in Dickason, the Supreme Court added:

 

… the prohibition against waiver of human rights provisions arises not only from a concern about inequality in bargaining power, but also because the rights guaranteed by human rights codes are seen as inherent to the dignity of every individual within our society. As a matter of public policy, such rights are not the common currency of contracts, but values which, by their very nature, cannot be bartered.[42]

 

[124]     The above clearly shows that a protection guaranteed by the Charter cannot be waived. Therefore, it must be concluded that the authorization signed by S.N. cannot affect the protection guaranteed her under section 18.2. At most, that authorization allows the SPCUM to take cognizance of information dealing with the conviction because of a criminal offence.

 

[125]     In this regard, it is important to point out the limits and true scope of a pardon, as the Supreme Court wrote in Therrien:

 

[136]     … In my view, the provisions of the Quebec Charter are of no help in preventing an employer … from asking him a question of this nature during an interview. As the Court of Appeal points out, the Quebec Charter draws a clear distinction between the protection it affords against the discriminatory collection of information and protection against the discriminatory use of that information.

 

[126]     According to the above, the protection afforded by section 18.2 does not prevent an employer from obtaining information about a conviction for a penal or criminal offence. However, obtaining such information may not interfere with the application of section 18.2, which the employer must consider. Here again, it should be noted that section 18.2 constitutes a complete, self-contained, anti-discrimination regime.

 

4.         ANALYSIS

 

[127]     From the evidence as a whole, the Tribunal notes that the SPCUM adopted a hiring process as regards judicial background that, according to Ms. Cailler, automatically rejected any candidate for the mere fact of a criminal record or, in other words, a conviction for a penal or criminal offence, regardless of the type of offence or the means of prosecution. The existence of an exception in the case of an impaired driving offence, mentioned by witnesses Cailler and Gauthier, simply confirms the automatic rejection practised in the hiring process in regard to other offences, of any type whatsoever.

 

[128]     But the legislator prescribed automatic exclusion only in regard to offences prosecuted by way of indictment[43] and for which a pardon was not granted. What is more, even in those cases, the legislator did not adopt an exemption from section 18.2 of the Charter, as could have been done under section 52. Rather, the Police Act and Regulation 14 adopted under it determine that, in regard to offences prosecuted by way of indictment, no doubt exists and they are related to the employment within the meaning of section 18.2 and, in that case, the legislator required automatic exclusion.

 

[129]     Therefore, it must be concluded that, to the legislator's mind, the criminal records not contemplated by section 3 of the Police Act or by Regulation 14 must be examined taking into consideration a set of relevant factors with a view to determining whether rejection of the candidate is inevitable. That is how the Court of Appeal interpreted the protection afforded by section 18.2. The factors identified by the Court of Appeal include the seriousness and the circumstances of the offence. However, Robert J. added that the employer's decision must be made by verifying [TRANSLATION] "especially whether the guilty verdict allowed the [candidate] to continue to merit the trust of the public, an essential element in his or her role in maintaining public order".[44]

 

[130]     In the opinion of the Tribunal, that interpretation allows the objectives of the second paragraph of section 3 of the Police Act, supplemented by paragraph (b) of section 2 of Regulation 14, concerning the obligation to be of good moral character, to be achieved in keeping with the application of section 18.2. To clearly grasp that, it is important to recall here paragraph (b) of section 2 of Regulation 14:

 

2. To become a constable or a cadet of the Police Force or of a municipal police force, a person must:

 

(a) …

 

(b) be of good moral character according to the conclusions of an inquiry to be made by using the form prescribed for such purpose by the Commission particularly as regards his family, social, financial and judicial background;

 

[131]     According to that section, the investigation must deal with a set of factors, all of which are relevant to the hiring of future police officers.

 

[132]     The evidence adduced by the SPCUM shows that its hiring process is halted as soon as the commission of an offence is revealed by the preliminary check of police information systems. In the case of a discharge or pardon, mention of it in a candidate's record does not terminate the investigation. The investigator continues to investigate and takes cognizance of the information in police information systems related to the facts surrounding the offence. The candidacy is therefore evaluated essentially on the basis of an examination of the offence that is eliminatory in itself, as Ms. Cailler testified. It must therefore be concluded that such a decision to reject a candidacy is based on the "mere fact" of the conviction for a penal or criminal offence related to the employment.

 

[133]     According to the testimony heard, the SPCUM examined the offence according to the evidentiary facts as described in the police records or the court record. The person's circumstances are taken into account only if there is a doubt in the mind of the investigator. In S.N.'s case, the evidence showed that the investigation did not deal with any factor other than the elements in the police record that led to a conviction for a criminal offence.

 

[134]     In that regard, Mr. Gauthier confirmed that he noted that S.N.'s criminal record had been erased on December 30, 1992 because of a pardon, a result of the discharge obtained for the offence committed. Mr. Gauthier explained that, despite that observation, the investigation continued in order to take cognizance of the facts of the offence. In Mr. Gauthier's very words,  after taking cognizance of a mention of a pardon or discharge, exclusion [TRANSLATION] "is not automatic. I don't know what is in the record, whether there is a pardon. Hence, I must dig deeper and analyse the elements that resulted in the pardon".

 

[135]     The Tribunal observes that, by that action, the SPCUM violated the prescriptions of section 18.2 of the Charter and practised discrimination in regard to candidates when it rejected their candidacy because of their criminal records that were thereby protected. That is exactly what the SPCUM did in S.N.'s case.

 

[136]     Therefore, the Tribunal concludes that the SPCUM practised discrimination in violation of section 18.2 of the Charter in regard to S.N. by rejecting her candidacy merely because of the existence of a criminal record indicating that she had been convicted of a criminal offence for which she had received a pardon.

 

5.         APPLICATIONS

 

[137]     For the inconvenience caused the complainant, and for violation of her dignity and her right to equal treatment, the Tribunal claims from the SPCUM, on S.N.'s behalf, the sum of $5000 in moral damages.

 

[138]     The Tribunal is also seized of an application to prohibit the SPCUM from considering previous convictions from the standpoint of "good moral character".

 

5.1       DAMAGES

 

[139]     The following is established under section 49 of the Charter:

 

Any unlawful interference with any right or freedom recognized by this Charter entitles the victim to obtain the cessation of such interference and compensation for the moral or material prejudice resulting therefrom.

 

In case of unlawful and intentional interference, the tribunal may, in addition, condemn the person guilty of it to punitive damages.

 

[140]     The plaintiff said that she felt she was not given wholly equal treatment in her application for employment, and that she was devastated by the definitive rejection, whereas she believed she could be hired and have a career with the police force, since she had been granted a pardon.

 

[141]     Furthermore, she was considered not to be of good moral character even though she was not personally evaluated for the job she sought. The SPCUM preferred to exclude her automatically, without meeting her or investigating her acquaintances and without using the validated guides for determining whether she was or was not of good moral character.

 

[142]     In the Tribunal's decision in Claude Poirier v. Ville de Montréal,[45] the Tribunal awarded $5000 in moral damages to Claude Poirier for violation of the recognition and exercise of his right to wholly equal treatment without discrimination. His candidacy had been rejected for the simple reason of a handicap, with no other evaluation of his actual ability.

 

[143]     In Perrier,[46] following an agreement between the parties, the Tribunal ratified the payment of compensation of $8000 for violation of the right to equal treatment in seeking employment.

 

[144]     In Bichet,[47] the Court awarded the complainant $7500 for violation of her right to equal treatment, after she was not hired under a general exclusion, without being evaluated individually.

 

[145]     Taking into consideration the inconvenience caused the plaintiff and her feelings when not evaluated on an individual basis according to her abilities and merit, and when the SPCUM concluded, without an individual evaluation, that she was not "of good moral character", the Tribunal believes that the claim for $5000 in moral damages submitted by the Commission for the benefit of the complainant is justified and in keeping with the jurisprudence.

 

5.2       OTHER APPLICATION

 

[146]     The Commission is asking the Tribunal to order to SPCUM to cease to consider previous convictions from the standpoint of "good moral character". Although it is recognized that the Tribunal has the jurisdiction to issue the orders required in the exercise of its jurisdiction to halt a violation of human rights, I do not feel it is relevant to allow that specific application by the Commission.

 

[147]     The Charter expresses values that are necessary and must serve as a guide[48] in applying laws, without the Tribunal's being compelled to issue orders to that effect. If the SPCUM must consider previous convictions in evaluating a candidacy, it must do so in accordance with the Charter.

 

[148]     THEREFORE, THE TRIBUNAL:

 

[149]     ALLOWS the application in part;

 

[150]     ORDERS the SPCUM to pay the complainant, S.N., through the Commission, the sum of $5000 in moral damages, with interest from the service of the remedial measures, i.e. on February 4, 1998, at the rate set according to section 28 of the Act respecting the Ministère du Revenu (R.S.Q. c. M-31).
