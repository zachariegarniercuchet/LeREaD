Construction liens -- Trust fund -- Breach of Trust -- Corporation breaching trust obligations under s. 8 of the Construction Lien Act -- Defendant liable under s. 13 of the Act as director, officer or person with effective control of the corporation -- Statutory liability different and separate from liability under trust law -- Construction Lien Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. C.30, ss. 8, 13.

The plaintiff Baltimore Aircoil of Canada Inc. ("Baltimore") was a sub-subcontractor. It sued ESD Industries Inc. ("ESD"), a subcontractor, for breach of its trust obligations under s. 8 of the Construction Lien Act, and it sued TB, the sole officer, director or person with effective control of ESD as liable under s. 13 of the Act. Baltimore obtained a default judgment against ESD, and it was conceded that TB was liable to pay Baltimore $40,000 pursuant to s. 13 of the Act. The only issue was the form of the judgment as against TB. Baltimore submitted that the judgment should declare that TB was in breach of trust. TB submitted that the judgment should recite that he was statutorily liable for ESD's breach of trust.

Held, a judgment should issue that TB is liable to pay $40,000 by reason of liability imposed under s. 13(1) of the Construction Lien Act.

A breach of s. 13(1) does not lead to the conclusion that the person breaching the Act is a trustee. The elements for common law liability as a trustee or as a constructive trustee were different from the liability imposed by the Act. Section 13 of the Act was passed to impose liabilities for breaches of trust by a corporation under s. 8 upon the directors or officers or persons in control of the corporate activities when they conducted themselves in the ways described by s. 13. The standard under s. 13 was very different from the standard under trust law when a stranger to a trust can be found liable as a constructive trustee. The elements necessary for a plaintiff to be successful in an action under trust law were significantly more stringent than the elements under s. 13 of the Act. There is a difference between statutory liability and common law liability. Section 13 was not a restatement of the common law but the creation of an independent statutory scheme. The legislation did not require constructive trusteeship. Accordingly, judgment should issue that TB was liable to pay $40,000 by reason of liability [page291] imposed under s. 13(1) of the Construction Lien Act for the breach of trust of ESD Industries Inc.

[1] WILKINS J.: -- At the opening of trial I was advised by counsel that they had settled all of the issues with the exception of the form in which a judgment should issue. Counsel advised that they wished to argue that question given that the plaintiff had successfully established a factual case upon which to rely.

[2] At first blush, counsel's proposal seemed relatively straightforward. After hearing submissions it is clear that the issues involved are significantly broader than the simple question as to form and content.

[3] The plaintiff's action was founded on a claim under the Construction Lien Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. C.30 (the "Act"), as against the corporate defendant for breach of its trust obligations under s. 8 of the Act and as against the sole officer, director and/or person with effective control of the affairs of that corporation for breaches of s. 13.

[4] The plaintiff obtained default judgment against the corporate defendant. On the basis of the admitted factual circumstances before me, it was conceded that the plaintiff was entitled to a finding of liability under s. 13 of the Act as against the individual defendant. The issue to be determined was the nature of the basis upon which that judgment should rest. [page292]

[5] Put in its simplest form, the question is as to whether the form and content of the judgment should be a declaration that the individual defendant was in breach of trust and that the judgment against him was, by reason of his own breach of trust, or whether the judgment should recite that the corporation was in breach of its trust, and that the defendant was statutorily liable for the corporate breach of trust by operation of the Act.

[6] The corporate defendant had been a subcontractor on a project in Ottawa. The plaintiff had been a sub-subcontractor who had provided materials to the job site. Moneys had been paid to the subcontractor by reason of the materials provided to the job site by the sub-subcontractor. The subcontractor had juggled those funds, co-mingled them with other funds from other projects and disbursed all of the moneys without satisfying the debt to the sub-subcontractor. The amounts of money inappropriately disbursed by the subcontractor far exceeded the amount of the claims of the sub-subcontractor and some of the money was taken by the individual personally.

[7] Section 8(1)(b) of the Act provides in part:

8(1) All amounts,

. . . . .

(b) received by a contractor or subcontractor,

on account of the contract or subcontract price of an improvement constitute a trust fund for the benefit of the subcontractors and other persons who have supplied services or materials to the improvement who are owed amounts by the contractor or subcontractor.

[8] The parties agree that this trust provision applies with respect of the corporate defendant.

[9] Section 13(1) of the Act provides:

(1) In addition to the persons who are otherwise liable in an action for breach of trust under this Part,

(a) every director or officer of a corporation; and

(b) any person, including an employee or agent of a corporation, who has effective control of the corporation or its relevant activities,

who assents to, or acquiesces in, conduct that he or she knows or reasonably ought to know amounts to breach of trust by the corporation is liable for the breach of trust.

Section 13(3) provides:

13(3) Where more than one person is found liable or has admitted liability for a particular breach of trust under this Part, those persons are jointly and severally liable. [page293]

[10] The defendant argues that, on a clear reading of the words of those sections, the trust that is created under s. 8 is imposed upon the corporation, and that when one reads s. 13(1) closely, it constitutes a scheme whereby liability can be imposed upon other persons as a consequence of the breaches of trust on the part of the s. 8(1)(b) trustee.

[11] The defendant argues that the wording "In addition to the persons who are otherwise liable in an action for breach of trust under this Part", certain other persons as defined in subsections (a) and (b) who assent to, or acquiesce in, conduct that he or she knows or reasonably ought to know amounts to breach of trust by the corporation are "liable for the breach of trust" are specific words which are limited in their meaning. Counsel argues that the additional persons contemplated by s. 13 become statutorily liable for the corporation's breach of trust because they have assented to, or acquiesced in, conduct which they know or reasonably ought to know "amounts to breach of trust by the corporation" and they, therefore, become liable "for the breach of trust". That is to say, the breach of trust by the corporation (italics mine).

[12] In support of the above proposition, the respondent relies on the case of Patrick Harrison & Co. v. Devran Petroleum Ltd. (1993), 22 C.P.C. (3d) 285 (Ont. Gen. Div.), a decision of Mr. Justice Flinn. The nature of the issues before Justice Flinn are different from the nature of the issues before me. In the Harrison case, there was a motion for leave to add the directors of the defendant company as parties in an action where allegations of breach of trust were made against the corporation. The plaintiff in the motion before Flinn J. sought leave to amend the Statement of Claim more than seven years after the completion of a contract under circumstances where the limitation period would have applied in respect of contractual claims, but not in respect of claims made against the directors in breach of trust. Counsel for the defendant relies on portions of that decision wherein Justice Flinn set out [at pp. 288-89 C.P.C.]:

Counsel for the plaintiff takes the view that the directors are in fact trustees and that therefore those provisions upon which the counsel for the defence is relying with respect to limitations do not apply.

Liability of directors is set forth in s. 13 of the Construction Lien Act which says:

(1) In addition to the persons who are otherwise liable in an action for breach of trust under this Part,

(a) every director or officer of a corporation; . . . who assents to or acquiesces in conduct that he or she knows or reasonably [page294] ought to know amounts to breach of trust by the corporation is liable for the breach of trust.

It seems to me quite clear that such does not make the directors trustees but rather makes them liable for breach of trust in the same manner as the trustee corporation would be liable, i.e., for the [same] damages. As I find the directors were not trustees, this motion to amend and add parties would be caught by the Limitation Act.

[13] Counsel for the defendant argues that under the s. 8 provision creating the trust, the individual defendant was at no time a trustee. The trust created by the Act was imposed upon the corporate subcontractor and that s. 13 was designed to pierce the corporate veil and thrust liability for the corporate breaches of trust upon the directors, officers or persons in charge who had assented to, or acquiesced in the corporate conduct which they knew, or ought to have known, amounted to breach of trust by the corporation. On this basis, the defendant argues that the individual defendant should be liable for the corporate breach of trust which has taken place on the facts of this case, but that there is insufficient support for a finding that the individual defendant was in fact a trustee, or that the individual defendant's conduct is a de facto breach of trust. The nature of the liability imposed upon officers, directors or persons in effective control, as argued by the defendant, would be something akin to the concept of vicarious liability as opposed to liability directly imposed upon those persons by reason of them being trustees found accountable for the breaches of trust in their own right.

[14] This issue was canvassed at length in St. Mary's Cement Corp. v. Construc Ltd. (1997), 1997 CanLII 12114 (ON CTGD), 32 O.R. (3d) 595, 33 C.L.R. (2d) 234 (Gen. Div.). In that case, Construc was a one-man operation just as the corporate defendant is in the case at bar. In St. Mary's, the corporation's conduct constituted a breach of trust and that the individual defendant assented to, acquiesced, and knew or ought to have known, that the corporation's conduct amounted to breach of trust. Page 617 O.R. of the St. Mary's decision, set out as follows:

Mr. Salvatori, counsel for Mr. Paniccia, submits that s. 13(1) does not create liability for breach of trust per se but merely makes the individual liable for the damages flowing from the corporation's breach of trust. He relies on Patrick Harrison & Co. v. Devran Petroleum Ltd. (1993), 22 C.P.C. (3d) 285 (Ont. Gen. Div.) . . .

The learned judge quotes from Justice Flinn as set out above, and continues:

However, the issue before Justice Flinn was quite different from the one before me. In that case, the plaintiff sought to add individual directors to an already existing action against the corporate defendant based on an alleged breach of the trust provisions of the Act some seven years earlier. The proposed [page295] defendants argued that this claim was statute-barred against them, whereas the plaintiff argued that there was no limitation period problem because the action was being brought against those individuals as trustees. It was in this context that Justice Flinn found that the individuals were not trustees under the Act. I do not take this decision as having application as to whether or not a director may be found to be in breach of trust if the conditions of s. 13(1) of the Act are met, as this issue was not squarely before him. However, if that is the effect of his decision, then I must respectfully disagree.

[15] The judge continued further in the decision by citing St. Mary's Cement Co. v. MacDonald (having since been cited as [1995] O.J. No. 2179), wherein Pitt J. "found individuals liable under s. 13(1) and issued a declaration that they were in breach of the trust provisions of the Construction Lien Act". She further went on to cite Great Lake Brick & Stone Ltd. v. 837004 Ontario Ltd. (1993), 8 C.L.R. (2d) 104 (Ont. Gen. Div.) wherein Kennedy J. held a personal defendant liable for a corporation's breach of trust and stated, "In equity and also under s. 13 of the Construction Lien Act the personal defendant is liable to the plaintiff for breach of trust." Relying further on Minneapolis-Honeywell v. Empire Brass, 1955 CanLII 40 (SCC), [1955] S.C.R. 694, [1955] 3 D.L.R. 561; Andrea Schmidt Construction Ltd. v. Glatt et al. (1980), 1980 CanLII 2714 (ON CA), 28 O.R. (2d) 672, 112 D.L.R. (3d) 371 (C.A.), and Home Depot Inc. v. Fieder Painting Inc., [1995] O.J. No. 2263 (Gen. Div.), the judge went on to set out at p. 618 O.R.:

In my opinion, the language and intent of the legislation support the conclusion that a person found liable under s. 13(1) of the Act has in fact been found liable for breach of trust. Section 13 begins with the words, "in addition to the persons who are otherwise liable in an action for breach of trust under this Part" and then goes on to set out the circumstances in which others will be "liable for the breach of trust". It seems to me that the plain meaning of those words is that individuals who fall within s. 13 will be liable for breach of trust in addition to those whose liability is founded under other sections of the Act, with both categories of wrongdoers being in breach of trust. This interpretation is reinforced by s. 13(3) which provides that where more than one person is found liable "for a particular breach of trust under this Part" those persons are jointly and severally liable. It is clear that s. 13(3) applies to individuals whose responsibility is founded under s. 13(1) as well as those, including the corporate contractor trustee, whose responsibility is based on other provisions within Part II of the Act. It follows naturally from this section that if Construc is liable for breach of trust, then Mr. Paniccia is also jointly and severally liable with Construc for the breach of trust. He must, therefore, be liable himself for breach of trust. The intention of the legislation was to set up a trust with respect to moneys received from owners in favour of unpaid trades. It would be inconsistent with that intention if individuals who by their conduct defeat the trust were not liable for breach of trust in the same manner as the corporate vehicles they control.

[16] Counsel for the defendant before me takes the position that in deciding St. Mary's the judge erred and that she ought not to have made the jump from s. 13(1) finding individual persons to be liable into a finding that they are, in fact, trustees. [page296]

[17] Justice Flinn, in the Harrison decision, sets out the basis upon which he reached his conclusions. Reviewing the facts and information before him [at p. 288 C.P.C.] he set out:

Surely, one has to take a hard look at a case in which the plaintiff's claim is now being presented by the third firm of solicitors for the plaintiff, that the cause of action arose over 7 years ago, that the present solicitors have had control of the file since January, 1993, and that the basis of the claim of breach of trust arises out of the allegation that Devran failed to pay Harrison all amounts due pursuant to the work completed by Devran on the project, in the statement of claim described as extras and damages for delay. There is nothing new about those claims. They are itemized and set forth in invoices referred to in the statement of claim and there is no allegation in the material that any further information was obtained on the examination for discovery of representatives of the defendant. Further, counsel for the defendants saw fit to cross-examine the deponent on his affidavit in these matters and notwithstanding the discovery of the defendants, there is nothing in that affidavit which put any meat on the bones of a bald declaration that because Devran didn't pay, the deponent believes that Devran appropriated or converted to its own use part of the moneys.

[18] Due to an absence of information, Justice Flinn makes the comments above. He finds as a fact that the directors were not trustees. Having regard to Flinn J.'s findings, it would seem to me that he was of the opinion there was insufficient information before him to make a finding that the directors of Devran could have been liable pursuant to s. 13. Section 13 could not, under those circumstances, be interpreted as applying to those directors. The learned judge's comments to the effect that s. 13 did not make those directors trustees would, under those circumstances, be fact driven and those words would be, at best, obiter dictum.

[19] Looking at the wording of s. 13 of the Act, one could certainly say that on their face the actual words themselves do not declare the persons contemplated by subsections (a) and (b) to be trustees. Counsel for the defendant argues that there ought to be specific wording in the legislation in order to impose the liability of a trustee upon persons who are otherwise not trustees and protected by the corporate veil. It is the defendant's position that the wording of the section makes the persons contemplated in subsections (a) and (b) personally liable for the acts of the corporation, and that result is draconian having regard to the concepts of limited liability. It is the defendant's position that the legislature did not intend to impose a specific trust on officers and directors, or to make them trustees, or it would have clearly established those responsibilities in the wording of the section as much as those responsibilities have been clearly established in s. 8. [page297]

[20] The St. Mary's Cement decision relied on s. 13(3) in support of a finding that s. 13 imposed trust obligations on the persons set out in subsections (a) and (b). Subsection 13(3) provides:

13(3) Where more than one person is found liable or has admitted to liability for a particular breach of trust under this Part, those persons are jointly and severally liable.

[21] The above was interpreted as meaning that if directors were to be jointly and severally liable with their trustee company for the corporate breach of trust, then the director must be liable themselves as trustees for breach of the trust. The learned judge then went on to consider that it was the intention of the legislation to set up the trust provided for in s. 8 and that it would be inconsistent with that intention if individuals who, by their conduct defeated the trust, were not liable for breach of the trust in the same manner as the corporate vehicles they controlled.

[22] By logical extension, the position taken by the defendant in the matter would be that the wording of subsection (3) simply does not lead to the conclusion that if somebody is to be found jointly and severally liable with the trustee in a breach, then they themselves must be deemed to be a trustee. Similarly, it would be a logical extension of the defendant's position that the further comment by the learned justice on the intention of the legislature would not logically follow. It was the finding of that judge that it would be inconsistent with the intention of the trust provisions of s. 8 if "individuals who by their conduct defeat the trust" were not liable for breach of trust in the same manner as the corporate vehicles they control. It is the logical extension of the defendant's position that this would not be correct and that there is no provision contained in s. 13 that could lead one to that conclusion.

[23] A reading of s. 13 makes it quite clear that directors, officers and any person who assents to or acquiesces in the conduct which they knew, or reasonably ought to know, would defeat the trust are, in fact, statutorily held liable for the breach of trust. Looking at the Act, it is difficult to contemplate how such persons could act through the company to defeat the trust and escape personal liability whether or not they themselves are deemed to be trustees.

[24] The wording of s. 13 as it stands alone creates a clear and obvious statutory liability for identified persons who conduct their affairs in a described manner. If the individuals are not within the group of described persons, or if their conduct does not meet the standard required for the imposition of liability under s. 13, they escape liability. If, however, the individuals fall within the described group of persons and their conduct falls within the prohibited conduct under s. 13, then they do in fact become liable. [page298] Just who those individuals might be, that is, who are defeating the trust and escaping liability, are not readily identifiable.

[25] The plaintiff takes a very different and interesting position. Counsel for the plaintiff argues that the principles of trust law would apply to persons in the position of the individual defendant in the case at bar, and that when one reviews the considerations of the Supreme Court of Canada in Air Canada v. M&L Travel Ltd., 1993 CanLII 33 (SCC), [1993] 3 S.C.R. 787, 15 O.R. (3d) 804, Gold v. Rosenberg, 1997 CanLII 333 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 767, 152 D.L.R. (4th) 385, and Citadel General Assurance Co. v. Lloyd's Bank Canada, 1997 CanLII 334 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 805, 152 D.L.R. (4th) 411, the persons contemplated in the group created by subsections (a) and (b) would, by reason of their relationship to the corporation and the trust created under s. 8 of the Act, place themselves in the position of being capable of being found liable as constructive trustees. Those cases establish that a stranger to a trust can be held liable for a breach of trust if they knowingly participated in the breach of trust, either as persons in receipt and chargeable with trust property or as persons who knowingly assisted in a dishonest and fraudulent design on the part of the trustee. In the face of either circumstance, a constructive trust would be created and they could be found liable as either persons described as constructive trustees arising from "knowing receipt" or "knowing assistance". Mr. Justice Iacobucci in Air Canada considered at pp. 809-11 S.C.R. the concept of the creation of a constructive trust. He further considered the degree of knowledge that would have to be imputed to such a constructive trustee in order to find liability on either the concept of "knowing receipt" or "knowing assistance".

[26] It is not necessary for me to get into a detailed analysis of the process by which a stranger to a trust can be found liable for the breaches of trust as a constructive trustee. It is sufficient for me to say that the cases cited by the plaintiff establish that persons in the position of the group of people created by subsections (a) and (b), when directing the affairs of the corporation in the fashion contemplated by Mr. Justice Iacobucci and Madam Justice McLachlin, as she then was, in Air Canada, can become constructive trustees. These constructive trustees could, by their conduct and/or their degree of knowledge, be found liable for breaches of trust in respect of a trust in relationship to which they would otherwise be strangers.

[27] The wording of the provisions of s. 13 of the Act to a large extent, follows the format of the considerations of Justice Iacobucci in that decision. Although the persons considered in subsections (a) and (b) may be strangers to the trust created in s. 8, the statutory provisions in respect of their conduct contained in s. 13(1) are generally similar in nature to the considerations in respect of [page299] the creation of a constructive trustee. The wording of the section, however, is materially different from the descriptions of the conduct and knowledge contemplated by Mr. Justice Iacobucci in the Air Canada decision. It is clear from his decision that directors of corporations could be held personally liable as constructive trustees under the concept of "knowing assistance". The directors of corporations as "persons who 'assist with knowledge in a dishonest or fraudulent design on the part of [a trustee]' will be liable for the breach of trust as constructive trustees. This basis of liability raises two main issues: the nature of the breach of trust and the degree of knowledge required of the stranger."

[28] Justice Iacobucci sets out that the degree of knowledge required of the stranger is actual knowledge, recklessness or wilful blindness. With respect to the nature of the breach of trust to be considered as necessary to impose liability on a stranger,Justice Iacobucci, at pp. 813-28 S.C.R., considers the nature of conduct necessary to constitute such a breach of trust. After a detailed analysis of the different lines of cases, he commented at p. 826 S.C.R.:

Where the trustee is the corporation, rather than an individual, the enquiry as to whether the breach of trust was dishonest and fraudulent may be more difficult to conceptualize, because the corporation can only act through human agents who are often the strangers to the trust whose liability is in issue. Regardless of the type of trustee, in my view, the standard adopted by Peter Gibson J. in Baden, Delvaux case following the decision of the English Court of Appeal in Belmont Finance, supra, is a helpful one. I would therefore "take as a relevant description of fraud 'the taking of a risk to the prejudice of another's rights, which risk is known to be one which there is no right to take'." In my opinion this standard best accords with the basic rationale for the imposition of personal liability on a stranger to a trust which was enunciated in In re Montagu's Settlement Trust, supra, whether the stranger's conscience is sufficiently affected to justify the imposition of personal liability. In that respect, the taking of a knowingly wrongful risk resulting in prejudice to the beneficiary is sufficient to ground personal liability. This approach is consistent with both lines of authority previously discussed.

[29] It seems to me that the payment of moneys out of the trust account to the point that there are insufficient moneys to satisfy the claims of the beneficiary to the trust, could well be described as taking a wrongful risk resulting in prejudice to the beneficiary. The degree of knowledge, however, would appear to be actual knowledge, recklessness or wilful blindness.

[30] Having regard to the analysis performed by Justice Iacobucci in the Air Canada decision, it would seem to me that, in the absence of statutory intervention, a plaintiff, in order to be successful in imposing liability upon the director of a corporation for breaches of trust by that corporation, would have to demonstrate that the director in question had placed themselves in the position of being a constructive trustee who, with [page300] actual knowledge or by reason of recklessness or wilful blindness, took a knowingly wrongful risk resulting in prejudice to the beneficiary of the trust. Such considerations would apply to any action brought against the director of a corporation under circumstances where a trust had been imposed upon the company and the plaintiff sought to impose liability upon the director to make the plaintiff whole.

[31] The Construction Lien Act is a specific piece of legislation designed to remedy and rectify problems in the construction industry in Ontario. Section 8 creates trusts in respect of moneys in the hands of described persons under subsections 8(1)(a) and (b). The trusts imposed by that section do not extend to any other persons, other than those described therein, and the obligations of the trustee are set out in s. 8(2). Sections 9, 10, 11 and 12 deal with other aspects in respect of trusts in the construction industry. Section 13, however, deals with personal liability on the part of individuals.

[32] The persons upon whom trusts may be imposed by s. 8 of the Act may either be individuals or corporations. Section 13 of the Act, in my view, was passed to impose liabilities for breaches of trust by those corporations upon the persons who are directors, officers or persons in control of the corporate activities if and when a breach of trust occurred and the individuals had conducted themselves in certain ways. Section 13(1) of the Act sets out the circumstances under which personal liability would be imposed upon that group of people in subsections (a) and (b). Cases such as Air Canada, Gold v. Rosenberg and Citadel General Assurance make it clear that persons who place themselves in a specific relationship to moneys held in trust, and conduct themselves in a manner such that they fall afoul of the considerations of the Supreme Court in those decisions can, in accordance with the provisions of trust law, be found to be constructive trustees in breach of their trust. The elements necessary for the plaintiff to prove to be successful in such an action are significantly more stringent than the evidentiary burden under s. 13 of the Act.

[33] Under Canadian common law it is necessary to develop a factual basis sufficient to impose the obligations of a constructive trustee upon a corporate director, in order to find him liable as a constructive trustee for the breaches of trust which gave rise to the plaintiff's losses. Under s. 13(1) of the Act, however, it is sufficient to demonstrate that a person within the group defined in subsections (a) and (b) has either assented to or acquiesced in conduct that that person knew or reasonably ought to have known, amounts to a breach of trust by the company. In my view, this carries a significantly lesser burden and standard of proof [page301] than the requirements set out by Mr. Justice Iacobucci in Air Canada. Section 13(1) of the Act provides that negligence or simply permitting other directors to breach a trust can give rise to liability. The concept of simple knowledge or "reasonably ought to know" is a very different standard from that of actual knowledge, recklessness or wilful blindness.

[34] Under s. 13 of the Act, liability can be imposed upon the persons in a group defined by subsections (a) and (b) by simple acquiescence in conduct which that person reasonably ought to have known amounted to a breach of trust by the company. That, in my view, is a far cry from the test considered by Justice Iacobucci.

[35] In my view, the Act was enacted to create a set of circumstances where those described in subsections (a) and (b) would be held to a stricter code of conduct than that contemplated under the common law. The case law in which liability has been imposed upon officers, directors or persons in effective control under s. 13 of the Act demonstrates the differences between statutory liability and common law liability.

[36] The purpose and intent of the trust provisions of the Act is to impose the provisions of a trust on money owing or received, on account of a contract or sub-contract, which is for the benefit of the sub-contractors or other tradespeople who supplied services and materials to a job site. The legislation is clearly remedial in its effect. The legislation is clearly intended to rectify a circumstance in which persons who provide material and services to a job site, might find that money which was due to them in payment, has been used for other purposes. The persons described in subsections (a) and (b) as strangers to this statutory trust would be deemed to have knowledge of the existence of the trust; however, that would not, in and of itself, mean that as a stranger to the trust that person would have actual knowledge or knowledge imputed from recklessness or wilful blindness that the trust was, in fact, being placed in breach. Justice Iacobucci noted at p. 812 S.C.R. of Air Canada, supra:

If the trust was imposed by statute, then he or she will be deemed to have known of it. If the trust was contractually created, then whether the stranger knew of the trust will depend on his or her familiarity or involvement with the contract.

If the stranger received a benefit as a result of the breach of trust, this may ground an inference that the stranger knew of the breach . . . The receipt of a benefit will be neither a sufficient nor a necessary condition for the drawing of such an inference.

The reason for excluding constructive knowledge (that is, knowledge of circumstances which would indicate the facts to an honest person, or knowledge [page302] of facts which would put an honest person on inquiry) was discussed In re Montagu's Settlement Trusts, supra, at pp. 271-73, 275-85 Megarry V.-C. held, at p. 285, that constructive notice was insufficient to bind the stranger's conscience so as to give rise to personal liability. While cases involving recklessness or wilful blindness indicate a "want of probity which justifies imposing a constructive trust", Megarry V.-C. held at p. 285 that the carelessness involved in constructive knowledge cases will not normally amount to a want of probity, and will therefore be insufficient to bind the stranger's conscience . . .

[37] The standard of knowledge required to impose liability in s. 13 of the Act is, in my view, significantly less than that set out by Megarry V.-C. It is clear by reason of the decision of Mr. Justice Iacobucci in Air Canada, that a director who was found to have acquiesced under circumstances where he reasonably ought to have known that there had been a breach of trust by the corporation would, in all probability, not be found liable under the tests established in that decision. Under the Act, however, such a director could, in fact, be found liable.

[38] After consideration of the process by which liability is imposed on a constructive trustee as considered by Justice Iacobucci in the Air Canada decision, and the process by which liability is imposed under the Act, I am satisfied that they are different processes.

[39] Under the provisions of the Act, there is nothing establishing any requirement that a person must be a constructive trustee before liability can be imposed. Similarly, there is no requirement that the degree of knowledge of any actual breach of the trust must be tantamount to actual knowledge, recklessness or wilful blindness. The trust being statutory, persons in the group created by subsections (a) and (b) are deemed to have knowledge of its existence. In order to thrust liability upon them, however, it is only necessary to demonstrate that they assented to or acquiesced in conduct that they knew, or reasonably ought to have known, amounted to a breach of the trust by the corporation. This standard upon which liability is imposed is significantly less than the standard upon which liability is imposed upon a stranger under the common law.

[40] Section 13, in my view, is not a restatement of the common law but rather the creation of a separate and independent statutory scheme for the imposition of liability on certain persons. Breach of trust referred to in s. 13(1) is the breach of trust on the part of the corporation. In order to vest that liability upon officers, directors or persons with effective control, the common law is such that it is necessary to demonstrate that those persons are constructive trustees. Under the statutory provisions of s. 13, however, it is unnecessary to make that level [page303] of proof and it is sufficient to demonstrate that persons are within the defined group, and that their conduct falls within the conduct contemplated. The legislature does not require constructive trusteeship to impose liability. The bare statutory statement that certain persons may be held liable for the conduct of others is sufficient.

[41] With respect to s. 13(3), it is not essential for the imposition of joint and several liability that those persons must be trustees. More than one person can readily be found liable for breach of trust under the Act. An owner, a contractor, or a sub-contractor, can either be a corporation or an individual or a collection of individuals. A corporation in breach of its trust responsibilities under s. 8 would constitute such a person found liable under s. 13(3). A director, an officer, or a person in effective control found liable under s. 13(1) would also constitute a person found liable under subsection (3). If both the company and its director were found liable, then there would be more than one person found liable and both the company and its director would be jointly and severally liable to the plaintiff. The fact that the corporation was found liable by reason of breach of trust under s. 8 and a director was found liable by reason of the breaches of conduct contemplated by s. 13, would be irrelevant as to why they were jointly and severally liable. Joint and several liability would arise as a consequence of the straightforward operation of s. 13(3) of the Act. Put in simple terms, the legislature, having declared that such persons are jointly and severally liable ends the matter.

[42] With the greatest of respect to those who have reached a different opinion on these issues, I must say that I respectfully disagree with their conclusions.

[43] In order for the scheme under which liabilities are imposed for breaches of trust under the Act to properly operate, the concept of ultimate enforceability of judgment is not relevant. The sections of the Act applicable to the case at bar are those which impose trust obligations and those which impose liability for breach of statutory obligations. Considerations as to whether or not a person found liable under s. 13 can be obliged to make payment on a judgment or whether they can avoid payment by reason of the operation of the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. B-3 are inappropriate considerations for the interpretation of the Construction Lien Act. The scheme set out by the legislature leads to a method by which liability can be imposed upon a corporation for breach of trust and liability can be imposed upon persons in a defined group by reason of their conduct falling within the definition set out in s. 13. The Act, as I read it, deals with issues of the creation of trust and the imposition of liability [page304] and not with issues related to the collectability of the judgments or the effect of the BIA. These issues should be left to the Bankruptcy Court in a proceeding brought under that statute.

[44] Having regard to my findings in this matter, the form by which a judgment should be rendered in this matter should, in my view, be the traditional and standard form of judgment obliging the defendant, Thomas Brenton, to pay to the plaintiff $40,000 by reason of liability imposed upon him under s. 13(1) of the Construction Lien Act by reason of the breach of trust of the corporate defendant, ESD Industries Inc.

[45] I may be spoken to on the issue of costs.

Judgment accordingly.
