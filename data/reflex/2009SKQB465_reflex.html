[1]             The plaintiff claims damages for breach of a contract which required the defendant to deliver grain to the plaintiff’s ethanol production facility at Belle Plaine, Saskatchewan. The defendant denied that he was bound by the contract. At the conclusion of the trial of the action I held that the contract was binding on the defendant and that he had breached it by failing or refusing to deliver the grain. I reserved my decision on the issues of damages and costs. This decision deals with those issues.

 

                                                    DAMAGES

 

[2]             The production  contract obligated the defendant to deliver 16,000 bushels of Ac Andrew wheat to the plaintiff in each of the crop years 2007 and 2008 for a price of $3.67 per net bushel. It contained the following provision relevant to damages:

 

2.            OBLIGATIONS AND LIABILITY OF GROWER:  The Grower is required to sell and deliver the Minimum Quantity to Terra Grain Fuels. If the Grower fails to deliver the Minimum Quantity as required due to any cause except an act of God resulting in loss or degradation of crop dedicated to this contract, the Grower shall pay to Terra Grain Fuels immediately upon default liquidated damages (which the parties agree is a genuine pre-estimate of the damages Terra Grain Fuels will suffer as a result of such default) equal to the greater of $10.00 per tonne or the amount by which Terra Grain Fuels’ posted spot price on the failure date exceeds the contract price, for each tonne or portion thereof not delivered. If an act of God prevents delivery of the Minimum Quantity, the Grower must deliver all the production from the dedicated acres if it meets the specifications in this contract or if Terra Grain Fuels is prepared to take delivery and assess a discount as hereinbefore provided.

 

[3]             For reasons explained below, the plaintiff did not post a spot price (that is, the price at which it was prepared to purchase Ac Andrew grain on the open market) at any time during the crop years 2007 and 2008. However, the plaintiff contends that it should not be confined to damages of $10.00 per tonne (the equivalent of 27.22 cents per bushel, or $8,710.40 for 16,000 bushels) because the above provision is unenforceable as a penalty. The plaintiff argues that the court should award damages of between $60,000.00 and $75,000.00 by reference to Pool Return Outlook (“PRO”) prices published monthly by the Canadian Wheat Board (“CWB”).

 

[4]             Much of the evidence at trial related to the circumstances under which the contract was entered into by the parties and the purpose and relevance of CWB PRO prices. I will summarize that evidence.

 

[5]             Before constructing its facility at Belle Plaine, the plaintiff took steps to secure the supply of grain it would need to produce ethanol. The preferred grain was Ac Andrew wheat, a high starch, low protein grain especially well suited for the manufacture of ethanol. Farmers are not required to market wheat of that type through the Canadian Wheat Board (“CWB”) and may sell it directly to the end user.

 

[6]             To assess the availability of Ac Andrew wheat the plaintiff invited selected farmers to “focus group” meetings. The defendant, who carries on a large farming operation near Belle Plaine, attended one such meeting.

 

[7]             The majority of the farmers who attended the focus group meetings indicated that they would not commit to grow and deliver Ac Andrew wheat to the ethanol plant unless they were assured of receiving between $3.40 and $3.60 per bushel. Although that price was well above the 12 year average market price for Ac Andrew wheat, in due course the plaintiff offered producers a fixed price of $3.54 per bushel for a one year term contract and $3.67 per bushel for a two year term contract.

 

[8]             To set the fixed price it offered producers for Ac Andrew wheat, the plaintiff had relied on PRO prices published by the CWB. PRO prices represent the CWB’s best forecast of what the final price of listed crops will be. PRO pricing does not reflect the cost to the farmer of transportation from a local grain elevator to a maritime shipping port, which amounted to $1.63 per bushel for wheat. Thus, one way to estimate a farmer’s probable net return for wheat to be sold through an elevator was to deduct $1.63 per bushel from the PRO forecast price. Unlike deliveries to a grain elevator, by delivering their grain directly to the ethanol plant at Belle Plaine, farmers would not pay any additional charges for transportation.

 

[9]             Al McDonald, a former senior manager employed by the plaintiff, testified that when discussions with potential producers were taking place through focus group meetings in the late fall of 2006, the PRO forecast price for Ac Andrew, adjusted for shipping, was in the range of $2.85 per bushel. Even so, Mr. McDonald considered PRO prices meaningless unless the CWB had a buyer to sell to and rail cars available to transport the grain.

 

[10]           The plaintiff and the defendant signed their production contract on January 8, 2007. Construction of the ethanol plant was completed in the spring of 2007 and formal opening ceremonies took place in August of 2007. The plaintiff had been so successful in securing commitments from farmers for the delivery of Ac Andrew wheat that before the plant could begin producing ethanol, its entire 1.4 million bushels of grain storage facilities were filled to capacity and another 3.5 million bushels of grain were stored outside on the ground. Most of the grain was eventually used for production of ethanol, although some was spoiled. That grain was sold for feed at increasingly reduced prices. Eventually, when its value was so low that the cost of transporting it to a feedlot was not justified, it was worked into nearby fields. Al McDonald estimated the total amount of spoiled grain to be not more than two to four per cent of the total amount purchased by the plaintiff.

 

[11]           In the spring of 2007, the market price for Ac Andrew wheat started to rise. By the latter part of 2007, the published PRO prices for Ac Andrew wheat exceeded $7.00 per bushel. During the first eight calendar months of 2008, they were between $9.00 and $10.00 per bushel. The PRO forecast price of Ac Andrew wheat then began declining so that by September of 2009, it was $6.12 per bushel.

 

[12]           The plaintiff acknowledges that during the 2007 and 2008 crop years it did not post a spot price for Ac Andrew grain as contemplated by paragraph 2 of the General Terms and Conditions. There is no evidence that the plaintiff bought or sold any Ac Andrew wheat at prices equivalent to published PRO prices, or at prevailing “spot” prices, during the term of its contract with the defendant. With one anomalous exception involving a swap of grain for feed, the plaintiff’s only  purchases were from a relatively few farmers who wished to deliver more grain than the quantity specified by their contract. In those cases, the price was not a “spot price” reflective of the prevailing market price but the price at which that particular farmer had already contracted to deliver the grain under a production contract.

 

[13]           The production contract between the plaintiff and defendant contained a schedule for delivery of the grain to the ethanol plant in stages. In September of 2007, an employee of the plaintiff wrote to the defendant to confirm his intentions with respect to the delivery of his grain. The defendant responded by denying that he had ever entered into a contract with the plaintiff or that he was obligated to deliver grain to the ethanol plant. By November of 2007, it was clear that the defendant had repudiated the contract.

 

[14]           The defendant had, in fact, seeded 320 acres of Ac Andrew wheat during the summer of 2007. On March 7, 2008, he sold all of the grain harvested from that land (which exceeded 16,000 bushels) through a Parish and Heinbecker elevator in Moose Jaw for $267.10 per tonne (the equivalent of $7.27 per bushel). Deducting freight and elevation charges of $1.63 per bushel meant that the defendant’s net return for the Ac Andrew wheat sold was $5.64 per bushel. Thus, by selling his 16,000 bushels of Ac Andrew grain through the Parish and Heinbecker elevator for a net $5.64 per bushel rather than delivering it to the plaintiff for the contract price of $3.67, the defendant gained an additional $31,520.00 (16,000 x $1.97) for grain grown in the first year of the two year term.

 

                                   SUBMISSIONS OF THE PARTIES

 

[15]           Counsel for the plaintiff argues that para. 2 of the production contract’s General Terms and Conditions is an unenforceable penalty clause. Instead, he argues that damages should be calculated in accordance with s. 50 of The Sale of Goods Act, R.S.S. 1978, c. S-1 which states:

 

50(1)       Where the seller wrongfully neglects or refused to deliver the goods to the buyer the buyer may maintain an action against the seller for damages for non-delivery.

(2)           The measure of damages is the estimated loss directly and naturally resulting in the ordinary course of events from the seller’s breach of contract.

(3)           Where there is an available market for the goods in question the measure of damages is prima facie to be ascertained by the difference between the contract price and the market or current price of the goods at the time or times when they ought to have been delivered or if no time was fixed then at the time of the refusal to deliver.

 

[16]           Assuming that the clause is unenforceable, counsel for the plaintiff submits that the damages should be assessed under s. 50 of The Sale of Goods Act by referring to the CWB’s PRO pricing as published for each month the defendant failed to deliver the grain and then deducting anticipated shipping charges of $1.63 per bushel. Using that approach, the total damages would amount to $75,200.00. Alternatively, he submits that damages should be calculated using the published PRO price as at November 2007, being the month in which the defendant repudiated the contract, in which case damages would amount to $70,542.00, or as at March of 2008, being the date on which the defendant delivered the Ac Andrew wheat to the CWB through the Parish and Heinbecker elevator, in which case damages would amount to $63,000.00.

 

[17]           In the alternative, counsel for the plaintiff submits that even if paragraph 2 of the General Terms and Conditions is enforceable as a genuine pre-estimate of liquidated damages, the plaintiff should not be confined to recovering $10.00 per tonne. Instead, counsel submits the court should assess damages by reference to the CWB PRO prices with notional discounts for shipping costs. His submission, therefore, is that damages should be awarded based on CWB PRO prices whether or not the liquidated damages clause is valid and enforceable.

 

[18]           Counsel for the defendant submits that clause 2 of the General Terms and Conditions is not a penalty clause but a genuine pre-estimate of liquidated damages that means what it says and is binding upon both parties.

 

                                                    ANALYSIS

 

[19]           The first question is whether clause 2 of the General Terms and Conditions is a genuine pre-estimate of liquidated damages (in which case it is enforceable) or a penalty clause (in which case it may be unenforceable).

 

[20]           The test for differentiating between an agreement for “liquidated damages” and a “penalty” is whether the amount stipulated in the contract as payable is a genuine pre-estimate of damages. The provision will be void as a penalty where it requires the party in breach to pay amounts that are unconscionably high relative to the greatest loss that could arise from the breach. In those situations, payment will be considered unconscionable and extravagant if it produces a generally unreasonable result, either in amount or in the conditions under which payment is made, so that the court may find it unjust to allow such a sum to be recovered (Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Company Limited v. New Garage and Motor Company Limited, [1915] A.C. 79 at p. 87).

 

[21]           Where a contract expressly states that the parties have agreed upon a genuine pre-estimate of liquidated damages, courts have upheld the agreement unless it is clear that the stipulated damages constitute an unconscionable penalty (Elsley v. J. G. Collins Insurance Agencies Ltd. 1978 CanLII 7 (SCC), 1978 83 D.L.R. (3d) 1; [1978] 2 S.C.R. 916). The effect of the words “liquidated damages” is to limit liability for breach of contract if, taken in their context, they do amount to a genuine limitation

 

[22]           The plaintiff essentially argues that because it failed to post a spot price, the amount it can recover is unreasonably low and therefore the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable as a penalty. However, agreements for liquidated damages have regularly been enforced when the loss caused by breach proves to be greater than the amount fixed by the agreement (Gisvold v. Hill (1963), 1963 CanLII 657 (BC SC), 37 D.L.R.(2nd) 606 (B.C.S.C.); Maxwell v. Gibsons Drugs Ltd. et al. (1979), 1979 CanLII 702 (BC SC), 103 D.L.R. (3d) 433 (B.C.S.C.); Dorge v. Dumesnil et al. (1973), 1973 CanLII 1270 (MB QB), 39 D.L.R. (3d) 750 (Man.Q.B.); Cellulose Acetate Silk Company Limited. v. Widnes Foundry (1925), Limited, [1933] A.C. 20 (H.L.)).

 

[23]           In this case, the production contract was prepared by the plaintiff. It required payment of relatively nominal damages of $10.00 per tonne (27.22 cents per bushel) if the farmer failed to deliver the specified amount of grain. This minimum payment was required upon default without proof of any actual damages sustained by the plaintiff. If the plaintiff posted a spot price for the grain (that is, the price it was prepared to pay from time to time for Ac Andrew on the open market), the contract stated that its recoverable damages would be equal to the difference between the posted spot price and the fixed price contained in the contract. This approach was clearly a genuine attempt to pre-estimate the liquidated damages that would arise if the contract was breached. As a matter of evidence, the contract permitted and required the plaintiff to prove its loss by reference to (and only to) its own posted spot price. There was no reference to published CWB PRO prices.

[24]           It cannot be said that the provision was unconscionable for either party. It is true that the defendant realized significant profits by breaching the production contract with the plaintiff. However, liquidated damages under the contract were to be determined, not by what the defendant might gain, but what the plaintiff might lose. In most cases, once the plaintiff established liquidated damages by reference to its own spot price, some or all of whatever a producer might gain by breaching the contract would become payable to the plaintiff. In this case, however, the plaintiff cannot establish that it lost more than $10.00 per tonne because it did not post a spot price. The reason appears to have been that it had no need to buy replacement grain on the spot market.

 

[25]           I find that para. 2 of the General Terms and Conditions was a genuine pre-estimate of liquidated damages. As such, it was valid and binding upon both parties to the contract. There was no evidence that the defendant had posted a spot price during the two year term of its production contract with the plaintiff. To ignore the express terms of the production contract by using some other market measure to assess damages (such as CWB PRO forecast prices adjusted by notional discounts for shipping) would be to rewrite the contract.

 

[26]           The plaintiff is entitled to recover damages of $10.00 per tonne or 27.22 cents per bushel in accordance with the express terms of the production contract. For the 16,000 bushels deliverable by the defendant in each of the crop years 2007 and 2008, the recoverable damages amount to $8,710.40 (16,000 bushels x 27.22 cents x 2 years).

 

                                                      COSTS

 

[27]           The plaintiff seeks solicitor and client costs of the action, primarily because the defendant denied the existence of the production contract.

 

[28]           The production contract contained the following provision relevant to costs:

 

7.            COSTS: The Grower will be responsible for all legal and collection costs incurred by Terra Grain Fuels in performing and enforcing this contract.

 

[29]           As Matheson J. observed in Prairie Pulse Inc. v. Lacasse (2004), 2004 SKQB 30 (CanLII), 244 Sask. R. 126 (Q.B.), it is not every case where a contract provides for payment of solicitor and client costs that they will be awarded. A court continues to retain discretion to award costs pursuant to Rule 545(1) of The Queen’s Bench Rules.

 

[30]           The result of this dispute was divided. The defendant was unsuccessful in his claims that the production contract was incomplete when he signed it and that it was intended as nothing more than an indication of his intention to grow Ac Andrew wheat on 320 acres. The plaintiff was unsuccessful in its claim that the “pre-estimate of liquidated damages” clause was void or unenforceable. Both parties contributed to the cost and necessity of pursuing this matter through trial – the defendant by denying the validity of the contract; the plaintiff by pursuing a claim for damages in an amount that was not sustainable.

 

[31]           As a result, there will not be an order for solicitor and client costs. Rather, the plaintiff shall have party and party costs throughout under Column 2 of the Court of Queen’s Bench tariff of costs.
