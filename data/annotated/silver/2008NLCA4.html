Roberts J.A.:

[1]               This appeal raises the question of how to deal with an allegation of bias against a member of an arbitration board constituted by agreement pursuant to provisions of a collective agreement.  One would think that in 2007, after decades of labour arbitrations, the answer to this question should be readily ascertainable.  Surprisingly, it is not.

Background

[2]              Communications, Energy and Paperworkers Union of Canada, Local 60N (the Union) and Abitibi Consolidated Company of Canada (the Company) are parties to a Collective Agreement wherein the Company recognizes the Union as sole bargaining agent for all non-management Woodlands Division employees of the Company’s Grand Falls-Windsor paper mill.

[3]              The Union alleged breach of the Collective Agreement when the Company engaged the services of a non-union contractor to harvest wood, and filed a grievance to that effect on February 1, 2002.  The grievance was eventually submitted for arbitration to an Arbitration Board in accordance with Article 13 of the Collective Agreement, with the Union and the Company each selecting a nominee, and the nominees selecting a third party to act as chairperson. 

[4]              The Union, shortly before the date scheduled for the arbitration hearing, advised the Board that it would be objecting to the Company’s nominee on the basis of a reasonable apprehension of bias.  The Union’s objection was heard at the start of the hearing on December 5, 2003.

[5]              The Company argued that the Arbitration Board lacked jurisdiction to deal with the Union’s objection since the Board had already been constituted, and that, in any event, no reasonable apprehension of bias existed.  Notwithstanding, the Board heard evidence and submissions on the Union’s objection.

[6]              The majority of the Board, in a decision filed on July 29, 2004 upholding the Union’s objection, concluded:

The Board has jurisdiction to decide a question of bias.  The Union’s objection to the Employer nominee, Ron McKelvie, on the grounds of reasonable apprehension of bias, is allowed.  The Employer nominee is disqualified from continuing as a member of the Arbitration Board.  The Board is no longer properly constituted and lacks jurisdiction to hear the merits of the grievance.  To process the grievance to arbitration, the parties are required to appoint a new Arbitration Board.

[7]              The Company nominee, disagreeing with the majority, wrote, in part:

            What it really boils down to is can the board really literally throw off one of its own members bearing in mind the member had not been associated with their past employer particularly in union matters for 15 years.

            The union was notified months ahead of the hearing and its own representative agreed with the employer’s nominee.  Between them the chairman was selected if the person was available.  This should have been the basis on which the board rejected the unions [sic] objection. …

[8]              The Company filed an Originating Application (inter partes) in the Trial Division on September 27, 2004 asking for “an Order further to the powers vested in this Court by the Arbitration Act and otherwise through its inherent jurisdiction that the Arbitration Award dated 29 July 2004, the Employer Nominee dissenting, be set aside and the matter be remitted to the Board of Arbitration for reconsideration on the merits in accordance with the direction of this Court”.

[9]              The Originating Application was heard in early 2005.  In his decision filed on March 23, 2005[1], the applications judge reviewed the many cases and considerable jurisprudence submitted to him, and concluded as follows:

[30]  The issue of reasonable apprehension of bias is succinctly set out in the following principle enunciated by Rand, J. in Szilard v. Szasz, [1955] S.C.R. 3, where he stated:

Each party to an arbitration, acting reasonably, is entitled to a sustained confidence in the independence of mind of those who sit in judgment of him and his affairs.

                                                …..

[32]  As a secondary issue, the parties have asked the Court, if it should find that the Arbitration Board did have jurisdiction to rule on bias or reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie, to then go on and answer a second question.  The second question naturally is whether the Arbitration Board’s decision, reviewed according to the applicable standard of review, properly decided that there was in fact a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie. 

[33]  It is that second question that points out clearly the inappropriateness of the Board assuming jurisdiction over such a question.  The following issues arise from it so doing:

(1)        How can the Arbitration Board decide the issue without hearing from the impugned member?  Does the impugned Board member give evidence?

(2)        Is he/she to be sworn as a witness?

(3)        Is he/she subject to cross-examination?

(4)        What is the propriety of the remaining Board members ruling on the evidence of a fellow Board member, especially where there may be issues of credibility involved?

(5)        Are only the remaining two Board members the ones entitled to rule on the bias question?  What if it is a single member arbitration panel?

(6)        Who hears the bias challenge and rules upon it if two Board members are challenged for bias?

(7)        If the Board assumes jurisdiction and rules against the bias objection and then proceeds to hear the substantive portion of the matter, what constitutes the “record” for a judicial review of the “bias” portion of the Award?

[34]  I have great sympathy for the argument expressed by Mahoney, J.A. in Flamborough [Flamborough v. National Energy Board, Interprovincial Pipelines Ltd. and Canada (1984), 55 N.R. 95 (Fed. C.A.)]. ...A major principle in the establishment of an extrajudicial procedure for resolution of labour disputes by arbitration was that the procedure would be both summary and expeditious.  The passage of time has layered upon these processes many of the trappings of a full judicial proceeding thereby militating against the cost efficient and expeditious process initially envisaged by submitting such matters to arbitration.  There is, as Mahoney, J.A. correctly points out, a risk of the paralysis of tribunals at the whim of anyone willing to allege bias.  This is perhaps a greater risk at the Arbitration Board level where the same mechanisms as exist at the Court level are not available to punish frivolous objectors by awards of costs.

[35]  Similarly, I am empathetic to the notion expressed by Jones and de Villars [Jones, Q.C. and de Villars, Q.C., Principles of Administrative Law (Third Edition) (Carswell, 1999)] that perhaps the choice of whether or not to proceed with a ruling of bias should be left to the Board and that the Board may make its decision based upon a weighing of the availability, costs and delays inherent in each option, together with any statutory requirements about quorum and representation. 

[36]  However these realistic and practical considerations cannot, in my view, overcome the principles set out in Szilard v. Szasz as quoted in paras. [30] and [31] hereof.  The parties to an arbitration are entitled to have their dispute arbitrated by arbitrators with “as free, independent and impartial minds as the circumstances permit.”  If the question of a reasonable apprehension of bias exists with respect to an arbitrator deciding the substantive portion of an arbitration, a fortiori that apprehension of bias is even greater when it comes to the arbitrator ruling on or participating in a decision of the Arbitration Board to determine whether that apprehension is reasonable.  He or she becomes a judge in his or her own cause.

[37]  This concern, combined with the practical considerations evidenced by the questions set out in para. [33] hereof caused me to conclude that it is in fact inappropriate for an Arbitration Board to rule upon a challenge to its jurisdiction based upon a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of the Board or some member thereof.

[38]  Therefore the Award of the majority of the Arbitration Board on the question of a reasonable apprehension of bias with respect to Mr. Ronald McKelvie as a member of the Board is set aside.

[10]         The Union appeals the decision of the applications judge on the ground that he erred in law in finding that an arbitration board does not have jurisdiction to determine whether one of its members should be disqualified because of a reasonable apprehension of bias.  The Company cross-appeals on the question of who should bring the application before the court.  Both parties are in agreement that if this Court upholds the decision of the applications judge on the question of jurisdiction, it should find that he was in error in holding that the application to the court be by the member of the arbitration board who has been challenged.  Such an application, they contend, should be by the party raising the bias issue.  They also agree the issues raised are questions of law and that the standard of review is, therefore, correctness.

Standard of Review

[11]         The Board in this case, unlike the arbitrator in Voice Construction Ltd. v. Construction and General Workers’ Union, Local 92, [2004] 1 S.C.R. 609, and the arbitration board in Newfoundland and Labrador (Treasury Board) v. Newfoundland and Labrador Nurses’ Union (2006), 258 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 21 (NLCA), was not interpreting the terms of a collective agreement, although it was constituted to do so.  The Board never got beyond the preliminary allegation of a reasonable apprehension of bias and whereas the interpretation of a collective agreement is, as was expressed by Major J. in Voice Construction, “at the core of an arbitrator’s expertise and … points to some deference” (para. 29), the question of the proper procedure to follow in dealing with an allegation of bias is a purely legal one and of fundamental importance to the parties.  It is a question which goes to jurisdiction.  The standard of review, as counsel have indicated, is correctness, and can only be correctness.  Therefore, the normal pragmatic and functional analysis to determine the appropriate standard of review is not required.  The contextual factors of the presence or absence of a privative clause or statutory right of appeal, the tribunal’s expertise, the legislative purpose and the nature of the question are, in the circumstances, irrelevant.

Analysis

[12]         The parties agree that arbitration boards, whether structured pursuant to a collective agreement, the Arbitration Act[2], or otherwise, are bound by the rules of natural justice.  The comment of Goodridge J., as he then was, in Re C.U.P.E. Local 597 and St. Patrick’s Mercy Home (1982), 139 D.L.R. (3d) 740 (NLTD), is as accurate today as it was when it was written:

While the Board may determine its own procedure and may reconvene to clarify its decision, it is expressly bound by s. 12.03 of the agreement to “give full opportunity to all parties to present evidence and to make representations”.

Such a provision is superfluous.

The Board and, in fact, any quasi-judicial or judicial tribunal, must comply with all the rules of natural justice except to the extent that they, or any of them, may be excluded or varied by express agreement between the parties or by statute.  [Emphasis added; p. 744.]

[13]         A fundamental principle of natural justice is that a party should receive a hearing before a tribunal which not only is independent, but which also appears independent.  Thus, where a party has a reasonable apprehension of bias, it should not be required to submit to the tribunal giving rise to that apprehension: Matsqui Indian Band v. Canadian Pacific Ltd., [1995] 1 S.C.R. 3, at para. 80.

[14]         The test for reasonable apprehension of bias, which is an objective one, was stated by de Grandpré J. in Committee for Justice and Liberty v. Canada (National Energy Board), [1978] 1 S.C.R. 369:

            The proper test to be applied in a matter of this type was correctly expressed by the Court of Appeal. … the apprehension of bias must be a reasonable one, held by reasonable and right minded persons, applying themselves to the question and obtaining thereon the required information.  In the words of the Court of Appeal, that test is “what would an informed person, viewing the matter realistically and practically – and having thought the matter through – conclude.  … [p. 394.]

De Grandpré added in the following paragraph that the apprehension of bias must also be substantial.

[15]         Thus, both the principle and the test are clear.  What is not clear is who should apply them in the first instance.  A review of some of the cases and commentary shows a divergence of opinion. 

[16]          In MacDonalds Consolidated Ltd. v. Saskatchewan Joint Board, Retail, Wholesale and Department Store Union and Dickinson (1988), 65 Sask. R. 224, an employer sought to quash an arbitration award on the ground, among others, that the union nominee on the board should have been disqualified because of bias; he had acted in the past as the union solicitor.  Scheibel J. wrote:

[8]  The initial reaction is to quickly conclude that it was improper for Mr. Kowalchuk to sit as a member of the board which was to decide whether he has any bias or whether there was reasonable apprehension of bias.

[9]  Generally speaking, the issue of bias of one of the board members cannot be determined by that board.  However, where the parties agree that the board is to make the determination, the general rule does not apply.  [Emphasis added; p. 226.]

[17]         In Beno v. Letourneau (1996), 207 N.R. 76 (FCA), Brigadier-General Robert Meating had applied for leave to intervene in an application by Brigadier-General Ernest Beno for judicial review of the Somalia Inquiry’s refusal to remove its chairman because of alleged bias.  His application was dismissed.  Meating appealed.  The Federal Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal and the following footnote appears in the transcribed version of Pratte J.A.’s oral decision:

            The judge of first instance seems to have assumed that the Commission had the jurisdiction to rule on the ability of its Chairman to participate in the inquiry and that the only question raised by Beno’s application for judicial review related to the legality of that decision.  He accordingly held that the judicial review proceedings would be decided only on the basis of the evidence that the commission had before it.  We doubt the correctness of those assumptions and of that conclusion.  We incline to think that the Commission lacked the jurisdiction to rule on the disqualification of its Chairman and that, on an application for judicial review and prohibition based on a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of a member of a tribunal, the applicant is always entitled to adduce in support of his application any evidence tending to show the alleged bias.  [Emphasis added.]

[18]         Brigadier-General Beno’s application for judicial review (Beno v. Canada, [1997] 1 F.C. 911) was heard by Campbell J., who referred with approval to Pratte J.A.’s footnoted comment.  He wrote:

On the analysis I have just provided, Pratte J.A.’s obiter comment rings true to me.  The fact that BGen Beno made the application to the Commission to have Commissioner Létourneau disqualified is irrelevant.  It was for the Commission to decide whether it was the appropriate authority to rule on the motion.  During the February 12 meeting, in the presence of the other Commissioners, Commissioner Létourneau made his decision not to withdraw vis-á-vis BGen Beno.  In the face of this, what would a knowledgeable objective observer have expected the results of the formal motion to be?  It would merely be a faint hope to think that Commissioner Létourneau would change his opinion after hearing formal argument.  And what about the other Commissioners?  By allowing the application to proceed they left themselves open to criticism, whether properly placed or not, that they could not be independent in their judgment of the question when it was their colleague, Commissioner Létourneau, who was being challenged.  [Emphasis in original.]

            To have such doubts about the outcome being created proves the point that the Commissioners should never have attempted to decide the question in the first place.  Why?  Because they could not be perceived as independent.  That is, how can a tribunal hear and decide an application of bias against one of its members, with that member participating, and having already communicated a decision on the issue?  Applying an apparent bias in deciding an apparent bias, for me, means that no jurisdiction exists to decide.  [Emphasis added; pp. 33-34.]

[19]         Campbell J. concluded that the Somalia Inquiry Commissioners were without jurisdiction to rule on the allegation of reasonable apprehension of bias against the chairperson, and then went on to find that the chairperson had, indeed, demonstrated a disabling bias against Brigadier-General Beno.

[20]         Earlier in his decision, Campbell J. suggested a process for dealing with an expressed bias concern.  He wrote in that regard:

There are no precedents which clearly set out the procedure to follow in bringing a bias concern to a decision maker.  However, I believe that by applying and extending the approach adopted in Duncan, Re [[1958] S.C.R. 41], sound guidelines are provided.

            Clearly, if any person involved in an adjudicative process feels that the decision maker holds a bias against him or her, that person should bring the concern to the decision maker’s attention.  In the usual course of trial work, this does not occur in a formal way, but is done by merely stating the bias concern to the decision maker and asking the decision maker to stand aside.  As can be easily imagined, common reasons for a bias concern being raised are that the decision maker is personally familiar with one of the litigants or key witnesses, has dealt with one of the litigants in previous litigation or has said or done something which causes one of the litigants to feel that he or she will not get a fair hearing.

            Upon hearing the bias concern, it is for the decision maker to decide whether to stand aside as requested.  Mr. Justice Locke had no difficulty in making his decision on the most tenuous argument possible to protect the integrity of the process.  I believe trial practice shows that when faced with a bias concern that is not unsupported or frivolous, and therefore, is capable of placing a cloud of doubt over the case, a decision maker will usually opt to do the same as Mr. Justice Locke.  It might very well be that reluctance to do so arises from concern for disruption to the expeditious movement of the case through the system.  But this concern is usually resolved by focussing on the larger potential damage that will be done to the case, and to the image of the administration of justice, by a decision being overturned on a bias concern which was known from the outset.

            I also do not consider the position adopted by a decision maker to be a “decision” which is subject to challenge on judicial review.  It is merely a statement of opinion which allows the parties in the case to determine if some further action is needed to test whether the decision maker has reached the right conclusion.

            That is, if the decision maker decides not to stand down, common sense dictates that the litigant must have a right to take the bias concern to a superior authority to have an independent objective evaluation of the merits of the complaint.  If the superior authority decides that the bias concern has merit, the decision maker can be prohibited from proceeding with the case. [paras. 21-25] 

[21]         On appeal (Beno v. Canada, [1997] 2 F.C. 527), the matter of jurisdiction was not contested, the parties focusing rather on whether a case of reasonable apprehension of bias had been made out.  The Federal Court of Appeal reversed the decision of Campbell J., finding that there was no reasonable apprehension of bias in the circumstances.  An application for leave to appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada was dismissed, without reasons, on October 2, 1997: see Beno v. Canada, [1997] S.C.C.A. No. 322.

[22]         None of the judges in the Beno cases mentioned Flamborough v. National Energy Board, Interprovincial Pipelines Ltd. and Canada (1984), 55 N.R. 95 (FCA), where Mahoney J.A., dismissing an appeal from the National Energy Board, commented on the question of procedure:

[43]  I should have added that the proposition that a member of a tribunal against whom an allegation of an apprehension of bias has been made cannot, himself, dispose of or participate in disposing of that allegation is utterly fatuous.  The practical effect, if that were the law, would be the paralysis of tribunals, and trial courts, at the whim of anyone willing to allege bias.  The availability of judicial review and appeal ensures that such charges will, ultimately, be dealt with by a disinterested judiciary. [Emphasis added; para. 43.]

[23]         The opinion of the British Columbia Court of Appeal in Eckervogt v. British Columbia (Minister of Employment and Investment) (2004), 241 D.L.R. (4th) 685, is in the same vein as that of Mahoney J.A.  The issue, raised for the first time before the Court of Appeal, was whether a decision of the B.C. Expropriation Compensation Board should be set aside because of a lack of independence or a reasonable apprehension of bias.  Donald J.A., after dismissing the appeal on behalf of a five member panel, supplemented his decision with a “Procedural Observation”:

[46]  I do not wish to leave these reasons without commenting on what I see is the most unsatisfactory course these proceedings took on the question of apprehension of bias.  This is the second time in recent years (Golden Valley was the other [Golden Valley Golf Course Ltd. v. B.C. (Minister of Transportation and Highways) (2001), 200 D.L.R. (4th) 248)] we have had to grapple with such an allegation in the first instance.  Our primary mandate is to correct error and develop the law on the record of a prior proceeding – not to make original findings of fact or mixed fact and law.

[47]  If, during the course of a proceeding, a party apprehends bias he should put the allegation to the tribunal and obtain a ruling before seeking court intervention.  In that way the tribunal can set out its position and a proper record can be formed.  This, of course, would not apply when the ground of disqualification is discovered after the tribunal has completed the case and rendered a decision on the merits of the dispute.  There is, however, a more fundamental problem with the approach taken by the appellants.

[48]  I do not think it is proper for a party to hold in reserve a ground of disqualification for use only if the outcome turns out badly.  Bias allegations have serious implications for the reputation of the tribunal and in fairness they should be made directly and promptly, not held back as a tactic in the litigation.  Such a tactic should, I think, carry the risk of a finding of waiver.  Furthermore, the genuineness of the apprehension becomes suspect when it is not acted on right away.  [Emphasis added.]

[24]         Slatter J.’s view in Robertson v. Edmonton (City) Police Service (No. 10), [2005] 11 W.W.R. 656 (ABQB), is similar to that expressed in Eckervogt.  Under the heading “Bias of the Presiding Officer”, he wrote:

[117]  The Applicant argues that the Respondent Presiding Officer, Inspector Gibbs, is biased, or that his appointment is tainted with a reasonable apprehension of bias.

           Procedure

[118]  The procedure adopted by the Applicant to challenge the Respondent Presiding Officer was improper.  When a party wishes to challenge an adjudicator on the basis of bias or a reasonable apprehension of bias, the appropriate procedure is to apply first to the adjudicator to ask him or her to recuse himself or herself.  In Arsenault-Cameron v. Prince Edward Island [[1999] 3 S.C.R. 851] an application was made to the Chief Justice to disqualify Bastarache, J.[3] from sitting on the appeal.  At the opening of the hearing Lamer, C.J. stated:

            The motion is an irregular motion, it is not the proper way.  I informed the counsel it is not the proper way to ask for a judge to disqualify himself or herself.  The application should have been made to the judge himself.  Rather than dispose of the matter on a technical basis, then triggering probably and at the proper application and triggering also the right of reply and delays with the obvious result or probable result that the case would have to be postponed and traversed at great cost to all to the winter term, I took the unusual step of converting and considering the application to me as having been a proper application to disqualify, for disqualification made to Mr. Justice Bastarache.  Now I find no blame in the respondent’s mistake since our rules are not very clear on that point as to how one  deals with a court of last resort in a situation such like this.  I then gave everybody an advance copy of the judgment that now Mr. Bastarache is going to read.

(see B.A. Crane and H.S. Brown, Supreme Court of Canada Practice, 2002, pg. 56).  Bastarache, J. then read the decision which is now reported at [1999] 3 S.C.R. 851.  The same procedure was followed in Wewaykum Indian Band v. Canada, [2003] 2 S.C.R. 259.  I note that in two recent cases the Alberta Court of Appeal has also followed the same procedure:  see R. v. Tremblay, 2004 ABCA 102, and Hansraj v. Ao, 2004 ABCA 130. 

                                                            …..

[120]  The advantages of applying first to the adjudicator are obvious.  First of all, such an application respects the jurisdiction of the tribunal and the adjudicator over the issue; it prevents unnecessary interference by the Court of Queen’s Bench in the work of the tribunal.  Secondly, in most cases, an application to the adjudicator will be faster and cheaper than a judicial review application.  Thirdly, having to confront the adjudicator directly with the allegations of bias has a tempering effect on the type of allegations of bias that are made. Fourthly, and most importantly, an application directly to the adjudicator allows him or her to place on the record the facts relevant to the bias application, as happened in the appellate decisions mentioned in the previous paragraphs.  An example of this in an administrative context can be seen in Committee for Justice & Liberty v. Canada (National Energy Board), [1978] 1 S.C.R. 369 at p. 375 where the Board was able to place on the record a detailed history of Mr. Crowe’s involvement with the pipeline, giving the reviewing courts a factual record from which to work.  [Emphasis added.]

[25]         It is interesting to note that Slatter J. used  Arsenault-Cameron as his template.  Arguably, and the Union suggests this, different considerations apply to judges than to members of arbitration boards and other administrative tribunals, e.g., standards of judicial ethics, the independence and dignity associated with the judiciary, and the presumption of impartiality.  Notwithstanding, it is instructive to see how the Supreme Court of Canada, albeit a court of last resort, deals with allegations of bias against one of its judges: see also SOS-Save Our St. Clair Inc. v. Toronto (City)[4] (2005), 78 O.R. (3d) 331 (Div. Ct.).

[26]         Some labour law commentators are as definitive as Justices Donald and Slatter.  Brown and Beatty, in Canadian Labour Arbitration, 4th ed. (Aurora, Ont.: Canada Law Book, updated July 2007), state:

            A party seeking to challenge an arbitrator for bias or reasonable apprehension of bias ought to do so at the outset of the hearing as a preliminary matter; otherwise, failure to object with knowledge of the facts will likely be taken as acquiescence.  And when such an objection is taken, the usual procedure is for the arbitrator or board to give a ruling, and if the challenge is upheld, to withdraw from the arbitration.  [Emphasis added; para. 1:5210.]

The authors mention in a footnote to the underlined statement the contrary view of the applications judge in the present case.

[27]         David Mullan and Martha Boyle, in their article “Raising and Dealing with Issues of Bias and Disclosure” (2005), 18 C.J.A.L.P. 37, express a similar view:

As for responding to a challenge, the general position is that the tribunal should deal with the matter and make a ruling, and not, for example tell the challenger to apply for an order in the nature of prohibition from the court.  The point is made very cogently in a recent judgment of Slatter J. of the Alberta Court of Queen’s Bench in Robertson: … [Emphasis added; p. 48.]

[28]         The learned authors then suggest how to deal with multi-member tribunals, with particular reference to Arsenault-Cameron:

In the case of a multi-member tribunal or panel of a tribunal, when the challenge is to one of its number, the situation is by no means clear.  Our preferred view is that the obligation to deal with the motion for disqualification rests (at least initially) on the member challenged, and not the Chair or all other members of the panel or tribunal.  Indeed, as a matter of law, there may be warrant for this position if the way the Supreme Court proceeded recently in the instance of a challenge to one of its own members is applicable to tribunals.  Thus, in Arsenault-Cameron v. Prince Edward Island, where the issue was whether Bastarache J. should recuse himself from sitting, Lamer C.J., to whom the motion had been made initially, stated the application should have been made to the impugned judge himself.  Thereafter, however, the whole Court listened to the argument, but, nonetheless, Bastarache J. alone made the ruling dismissing the motion. [pp. 50-51]

[29]         Gary Yee, in an article entitled “Procedures in Dealing with Bias – The Adjudicator’s Perspective” (1997), 3 A.A.P. 54, citing Flamborough, states:

It is generally accepted that the adjudicator or hearing panel which faces a possible bias issue is competent to hear and decide the issue itself.  In other words, even though it may reasonably be seen that the adjudicator might be biased in deciding the challenge against him or her, the law allows this initial decision to be made by the challenged adjudicator, rather than requiring that a separate adjudicator decide the motion. …  [Emphasis added; p. 59.]

[30]         And lastly, Geoffrey S. Lester, who in “Bias, How and When to Raise the Objection” (1997), 3 A.A.P. 49, opines, at p. 50, that “[t]here is nothing to prevent the member against whom the allegation is made disposing of or participating in disposing of the allegation on the spot”.  Lester also cites Flamborough as his authority.

[31]         A contrary view, and one akin to that expressed by Pratte J.A. in Beno, is found in Palmer’s Collective Agreement Arbitration in Canada, 3d ed. (Toronto: Butterworths, 1991):

1.20.  A party seeking to disqualify an arbitrator on the grounds of bias must adduce sufficient evidence to meet the common law burden of proof.  One assumes that decisions as to bias are made by the courts or the individuals involved.  In recent cases, however, there has been a trend to have the decision made by the board itself.  It would seem that this is an improper procedure as it would appear inappropriate to rule on an accusation against one’s self. [Emphasis added; p. 12.]

[32]         The authors cite as authority for their statement City of St. John (1986), 24 L.A.C. (3d) 152 (Collier).  The arbitrators in City of St. John, in turn, rely, in part, on the same Palmer statement from an earlier edition.  They also feel themselves bound by the particular wording of s. 74(1) of the Industrial Relations Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. I-4, which provided:

     Any person is eligible to be appointed an arbitrator, or chairman or member of an arbitration board, in any arbitration proceeding under the provisions of section 55, but no person shall serve who is directly affected by the matter in an arbitration or who has been involved in an attempt to negotiate or settle the matter. [Emphasis added.]

[33]         The Board wrote, at p. 155:

     It could be argued very strenuously that a member of this board and in particular Mr. Levine could raise a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of the employer by his participation in the board’s decision on this issue.  But, it also goes one step further.  It could also be strenuously argued that, in participating in the board’s decision as he must, Mr. Levine is a person “who is directly affected by the matter in arbitration” and therefore is in violation of s. 74(1) of the Industrial Relations Act.

     It is because of this latter difficulty I do not feel a board of arbitration can assume jurisdiction over an issue such as this even with the concurrence of the parties who appointed it.  The Act places a clear prohibition upon the appointment of an arbitrator who is directly affected by the matter in arbitration. … [Emphasis added.]

[34]         Jones and DeVillars in their Principles of Administrative Law, 4th ed. (Toronto: Carswell, 2004), give a more nuanced opinion.  They write, under the rubric ‘Can the rule against bias be waived’:

            Which view is the better?  In our opinion, the better view is that the court has a discretion depending on the circumstances of the particular case and tribunal.  It should not be an inflexible rule that bias must be raised before the tribunal, and neither should it be an inflexible rule that bias need not be raised before the tribunal.  The principal value at stake is the efficient administration of justice.  Sometimes it will be more efficient for a bias allegation to be heard and decided by the tribunal, other times by the court.  The following factors might be taken into account:

(a)        The time and expense, and the effect on the parties, the tribunal, and the public interest, involved in continuing a proceeding that may later be quashed.  Evaluation of this factor will depend, for example, on the nature of the proceeding and its anticipated length.

(b)        The strength of the applicant’s case.  The stronger the case for a disqualifying bias, the more efficient it is for the court to decide the matter as quickly as possible.

(c)        The completeness of the record.  The court should consider whether the evidence will be more complete if the matter is heard and decided by the tribunal.

(d)        Whether the allegation of bias is, by its nature, something that could be remedied by the tribunal.  For example, an allegation of personal bias on the part of a potential panel member can generally be resolved internally.  An allegation of institutional bias may require judicial intervention.

(e)        Whether the tribunal is sophisticated enough to apply correctly the law on bias.  Many smaller tribunals will have no legally-trained members and no access to legal advice.

(f)         Whether the court believes there is any value in having the tribunal’s own opinion on the allegations of bias.

(g)        Whether the threshold test for discovery of the tribunal has been met.  If it has, and discovery is ordered, the tribunal will be in the untenable position of having to adjudicate a dispute in which it has, in effect, become one of the parties.

The application of these factors will generally be enough to differentiate between cases where bias ought to have been raised before the tribunal, and cases where it may be raised for the first time on judicial review.  There is no need to add an additional hurdle, namely whether the objection was raised in a timely way.  [Emphasis added; pp. 412-414.]

[35]         This completes my review. The lack of consensus and the variety of opinions are, as I said at the beginning, somewhat surprising.  Nevertheless, the most persuasive point of view, and the one which I adopt, is that the question of bias, when raised, should be dealt with by the person against whom the allegation is made.  The theoretical problems noted by the applications judge in para. 33 of his Reasons (para. 9 above) are outweighed, in my opinion, particularly in the case of consensual labour arbitration boards, by the practical considerations of efficiency and speedy resolution of employee/employer grievances.  The prompt resolution of grievances in the work place is what the arbitration procedure is designed to effect.  Grievances which are allowed to fester do nothing for labour peace and good working conditions.  Obliged recourse to the court as soon as an allegation of bias is raised would have the effect of causing long and unnecessary delays.

[36]         There are also the advantages, as noted by Slatter J. in Robertson, of respect for the tribunal and the prevention of unnecessary interference by the court, cost savings and the tempering effect of having to confront the board member with an allegation of bias, thereby also placing on the record the facts relevant to the bias application.  Most allegations of bias or reasonable apprehension of bias will resolve themselves either by the party alleging bias being satisfied with the explanation given, or by the person challenged recusing himself or herself.  If, however, the person challenged decides there is no good reason for recusal and the party alleging bias is not satisfied, the bias issue can be dealt with by the court by way of judicial review after the arbitration has been heard and a decision filed, together with, if relevant, a judicial review of the decision on its merits.  Proceeding in this way fosters timeliness in the resolution of grievances while ensuring that the allegation of bias or reasonable apprehension of bias can, if necessary, be dealt with ultimately by an impartial judiciary.

[37]         There may be reasons, statutory or otherwise, why allegations of bias before administrative tribunals, other than consensual labour arbitration boards, should be referred immediately to the court.  I would not foreclose any such argument.  Nonetheless, it seems to me that in the majority of cases the procedure described above should be followed, i.e., the allegation be dealt with immediately by the person challenged and only later by the court.

[38]         In the case of consensual arbitration boards, allegations of bias or reasonable apprehension of bias are rare and will continue to be so by the very fact that they are consensual.  They will usually arise where, as in the instant case, new information comes to light between the date of selection and the date of hearing.

[39]         As for the allegation of bias against the Company nominee in the instant case, the record of the arbitration board is not sufficiently complete to allow us to deal with it.  The Company nominee should have the opportunity to fully place on the record his response to the union’s concerns.  The arbitration would then proceed in the manner indicated.

Disposition

[40]         The appeal is allowed.  The matter is referred back to the Arbitration Board.  In the circumstances, because it was to the benefit of both parties to have the uncertainty as to the procedure to follow clarified, there will be no order as to costs either in this Court or in the court below.
