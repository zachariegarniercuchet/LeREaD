[1]                      Hughes C.J.N.B.:— I have perused the reasons for judgment prepared by my brother La Forest J.A. and I agree with the conclusion which he has reached, namely, that the special lien created by s. 19(1) of the Social Services and Education Tax Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. S-10, does not apply to any of the items concerned in these appeals.

[2]                      The difficulty encountered in interpreting s. 19(1) lies in determining the scope to be given to the words "a special lien . .. upon all the property used in or in connection with . . . the business of the vendor". When considered in isolation and apart from its legislative history, the subsection would appear to create a special lien on property other than property of the vendor since the words which precede the quoted words already create such a lien on the vendor's entire estate and the quoted words would therefore be superfluous.

[3]                      The reasoning of my brother La Forest, based on the legislative history of the subsection is, however, persuasive of a different interpretation which incidentally is consistent with the French version and supports the view that "the property used in or in connection with . . . the business of the vendor" is restricted to property of the vendor by the use of the words "sur tous ses biens".

[4]                      Counsel for the appellant submitted that the English version best expresses the intention of the Legislature. I am not convinced that such is the case. Counsel also submitted that the court should avoid any construction having the effect of reducing the quoted words to "futility". I think as a general rule there is merit in the submission that such an interpretation should be avoided where possible. When applied to the instant case, however, it is apparent that the French version is incapable of including any property other than that of the vendor whereas the English version may not be so restricted. The Official Languages of New Brunswick Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. O-1, provides that in construing any statute both versions in the official languages are equally authentic. While the words of both versions respecting the property used in or in connection with the business of the vendor may be redundant, the French version is to be preferred for its clarity and because it avoids results which the Legislature probably did not anticipate or intend.

[5]                      I agree with the disposal of the appeal as proposed by my brother La Forest.

[6]                      Stratton J.A.:—I have had the benefit of reading the reasons for judgment prepared by the Chief Justice and Mr. Justice La Forest and agree with them that these appeals should be dismissed.

[7]                      It has long been settled that an intention to take away the property of a subject without giving him a legal right to compensation for the loss of it is not to be imputed to the Legislature unless that intention is expressed in unequivocal terms: see E. A. Driedger, The Construction of Statutes (1974), p. 138. Where, as here, the expression of the legislative intent is unclear and the statute is susceptible of two meanings, it is my opinion the court must make a choice on the assumption that the Legislature did not intend to disturb existing rights.

[8]                      Accordingly, I concur in the conclusion reached by my brothers that the lien created by s. 19(1) of the Social Services and Education Tax, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. S-10, does not apply to any of the items of property concerned in these appeals.

[9]                      La Forest J.A.:—

Background

[10]                  These two appeals raise similar issues and were argued together. Broadly, they raise the question of the extent to which the lien created by s. 19(1) of the Social Services and Education Tax Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. S-10, for the amount of tax collected by a vendor under the Act applies to property owned by persons other than the vendor.

[11]                  The facts of the first case may briefly be stated as follows. Estabrooks Pontiac Buick Ltd. carried on the business of retailing automobiles at 92 Lorne St. in Sackville. Being engaged in retail sales it was registered as a vendor as required by s. 12 of the above Act. Other sections of the Act require a vendor to collect the tax imposed by the Act on behalf of the province. By s. 19, the tax so collected is deemed to be held in trust for the province and the amount so collected forms a lien on certain property described in the section.

[12]                  On March 19, 1981, an audit was carried out on behalf of the province which, according to counsel for the province, established a tax liability in the amount of $16,350.17, being the amount collected by the company as vendor and not remitted to the provincial Minister of Finance as required by the Act. (This latter fact is disputed by counsel for the respondents but this is not material for present purposes.) As a result of the audit a notice of assessment dated April 16, 1981, was issued requiring the company to remit the amounts owing by it under the Act for the period from November, 1978 to December, 1980. On default by the company, judgment was obtained pursuant to s. 38 of the Act on behalf of the province. The judgment not having been satisfied, a certificate was issued under s. 19.1 of the Act authorizing the sheriff to seize the property subject to the lien created by s. 19(1). Acting on this authority the sheriff seized the property at 92 Lorne St. which, it is alleged, was used in connection with the company's business as a car dealer.

[13]                  The property seized included the real property at that address but it was not owned by the company. It belonged to Mr. Larry N. Estabrooks, the secretary-treasurer and a part owner of the company. The company held the land from him under a one-year renewable lease dated April 28, 1980. The lease had been terminated at the time of the audit, but this factor is of no significance in light of the view I take of the issues.

[14]                  On an interpleader action by the sheriff to determine the competing claims to the property, Leger J. held that the property, not being owned by the company, did not fall within the ambit of the lien created by s. 19(1) and so could not be sold to satisfy such lien [40 N.B.R. (2d) 172]. He based his decision primarily on the fact that the French version of s. 19(1) clearly restricts the lien to property belonging to a vendor, but he also relied on the reasons for judgment of Dickson J. in The Queen in right of New Brunswick v. Fisherman's Wharf Ltd. (1982), 135 D.L.R. (3d) 307, 40 N.B.R. (2d) 42.

[15]                  The latter case, as mentioned, raises similar issues. Fisherman's Wharf Inc. which operated Fisherman's Wharf, a fast-food takeout on King St., Fredericton, on December 31, 1981, owed the province $5,350.10 in sales tax, interest and penalties. Accordingly a certificate was issued authorizing the sheriff to seize all the property used in or in connection with or produced by the business. Among the goods seized by the sheriff were the following:

(a) 5 fryers and a broiler owned by Superior Propane Co., the former leased to "Fisherman's Wharf (Jack Jardine)" and the latter simply to Fisherman's Wharf;

(b) a soft-drink dispenser, claimed to be owned by Brunswick Bottling Ltd., but subject to a conditional sale under which title is retained by the seller made by Grandale Company Limited as seller to Jack Jardine (not Fisherman's Wharf despite Dickson J.'s statement to that effect);

(c) a stand-up cooler owned by McKay's Dairy Ltd. on loan to Fisherman's Wharf under an oral arrangement which provided that the latter would handle the former's products; and

(d) a cigarette machine owned by Mr. William D. Cook which was installed on the premises of Fisherman's Wharf pursuant to an oral arrangement under which the latter was paid a commission on merchandise purchased through the machine.

[16]                  On an interpleader action by the sheriff seeking a resolution of the validity of the various claims in respect of the above-listed property, Dickson J. held that the lien created by s. 19(1) of the Social Services and Education Tax Act did not apply to this property and directed that it be released to its owners.

[17]                  In his view, s. 19(1) was not worded sufficiently clearly to warrant seizure of property not owned by the vendor. If that result had been intended the section would have spoken of property "regardless of ownership" or used words to like effect. He noted that s. 19(1) only gave the lien priority over "a claim, privilege, lien or encumbrance", words denoting third party claims rather than ownership, and that s. 19(5), which gave some protection to persons other than the vendor whose property was affected by the lien, was limited to persons like mortgagees and other encumbrancers. In his view, s. 19(1), if interpreted as applying to property of persons other than the vendor, would in effect amount to confiscation of private property by the Crown without compensation. The statute being a taxing measure and encroaching on private rights should be restrictively interpreted. Finally, as a corollary ground, he relied on the broad rights of the individual guaranteed by the recently proclaimed Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

[18]                  From these decisions, the province appeals.

The problem

[19]                  The English and French versions of s. 19(1) read as follows:

19(1) Every vendor who collects any tax under this Act shall be deemed to hold the tax in trust for Her Majesty in right of the Province and for the payment over of the tax in the manner and at the time provided under this Act, and notwithstanding subsection 72(2) of the Workers' Compensation Act, the amount shall, until paid, form a special lien upon his entire estate, on the entire assets of his estate in the hands of any trustee, and upon all the property used in or in connection with or produced in or by the business of the vendor, in priority to every claim, privilege, lien or encumbrance, whenever created, subject only to taxes levied under the Real Property Tax Act.

19(1) Tout vendeur qui perçoit une taxe en vertu de la présente loi est réputé la détenir en fiducie pour le compte de Sa Majesté du chef de la province, dans le but de lui remettre cette taxe de la manière et à l'époque prévues en vertu de la présente loi; et, par dérogation au paragraphe 72(2) de la Loi sur les accidents du travail le montant de cette taxe constitute, tant qu'il n'a pas été remis, un privilège spécial sur tout son avoir, sur tous les actifs de celui-ci qui sont confiés à un fiduciaire et sur tous ses biens utilisés directement ou indirectement dans ses affairs ou qui y sont produits, en priorité sur toute réclamation droit, privilège ou charge quelle que soit l'époque de leur création sous la seule réserve des taxes perçues en vertu de la Loi sur l'impôt foncier.

[20]                  Both versions make it clear that the lien on property other than that "used in or in connection with or produced in or by the business of the vendor" (which latter property I shall for ease of reference at times refer to as the business property) is restricted to property owned by or held in trust for the vendor. But there is an important difference between the portions of the English and the French versions dealing with the business property. The English version speaks of the property; the French speaks of his property ("ses biens"). Since by virtue of the Official Languages of New Brunswick Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. O-1, the two versions are equally authoritative, both must be examined to determine the intention of the Legislature. As we saw, Leger J. came to the conclusion that the English version being ambiguous, the clear words used in the French version should determine the issue.

[21]                  Counsel for the province argues, however, that to limit the business property to property owned by the vendor makes that portion of the section entirely redundant, and the use of unnecessary words is not to be imputed to the Legislature. The language of the Act, he argued, is clear and explicit; that being so it must be given effect whatever the consequences. Within its legislative jurisdiction, the Legislature is supreme and may enact provisions that have the effect of destroying vested rights. Section 19(1) was consistent with other provisions in the Act. It had been enacted to create a "special lien" to ensure collection of the tax, and the legislative history of the section revealed that the Legislature has given it a continually expanding scope. Though taxing statutes were once subject to restrictive interpretation that approach has now been displaced by s. 17 of the Interpretation Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. I-13, which provides that all statutes should be construed as remedial.

General principles

[22]                  There is no doubt that the duty of the courts is to give effect to the intention of the Legislature as expressed in the words of the statute. And however reprehensible the result may appear, it is our duty if the words are clear to give them effect. This follows from the constitutional doctrine of the supremacy of the Legislature when acting within its legislative powers. The fact that the words as interpreted would give an unreasonable result, however, is certainly ground for the courts to scrutinize a statute carefully to make abundantly certain that those words are not susceptible of another interpretation. For it should not be readily assumed that the Legislature intends an unreasonable result or to perpetrate an injustice or absurdity.

[23]                  This scarcely means that the courts should attempt to reframe statutes to suit their own individual notions of what is just or reasonable. However, legislative supremacy is not all there is to the Constitution. In determining whether a statute is just or reasonable, the courts can derive considerable assistance from the nature and origins of our political organization as a parliamentary democracy. Those who struggled to wrest power from the Stuart kings and placed it in the hands of the elected representatives of the people were not of a mind to replace one despot by another. Rather they were guided by a philosophy that placed a high premium on individual liberty and private property and that philosophy continues to inform our fundamental political arrangements — our Constitution. Indeed, for a time after the English Revolution, the English courts appeared to be prepared to hold statutes that invaded rights of this kind invalid; for an account, see Corry, "The Interpretation of Statutes" reproduced as app. I in E. A. Driedger, The Construction of Statutes (1974), p. 203 at p. 212 et seq.

[24]                  With the complete realization of the implications of parliamentary supremacy, this type of judicial approach, of course, disappeared. But the original foundations of our governmental organization remained as a legacy in a number of presumptions designed, as Driedger notes at p. 137, "as protection against interference by the state with the liberty or property of the subject". "Hence", he adds, "it was 'presumed' in the absence of clear indication in the statute to the contrary, that Parliament did not intend to affect the liberty or property of the subject": see also D. L. Keir and F. H. Lawson, Cases in Constitutional Law, 3rd ed. rev. (1948), pp. 3-4.

[25]                  This approach of the courts to legislative action permitted them to exercise an important role in the protection of individual liberties even in the absence of an entrenched Bill of Rights. If the legislation is clear, of course, the intent of the Legislature must be respected. But what these presumptions ensure is that a law that appears to transgress our basic political understandings should be clearly expressed so as to invite the debate which is the life-blood of parliamentary democracy.

[26]                  One of these presumptions is that a statute should not, in the absence of clear words, be construed as taking the property of an individual without compensation. This, of course, frequently arises in expropriation cases but it is not limited to these. Thus, in Manitoba Fisheries Ltd. v. The Queen (1978), 88 D.L.R. (3d) 462, [1979] 1 S.C.R. 101, [1978] 6 W.W.R. 496, the Supreme Court of Canada applied the principle under the following circumstances. The plaintiff company had for many years been engaged in buying fish from fishermen who fished in various lakes in Manitoba and in processing and selling the fish to customers in other provinces and in the United States. In doing so it had built up a clientele in what had become a highly competitive business. In 1969 the Fresh Water Fish Marketing Act, a federal statute, gave a statutory corporation exclusive rights to export fish from Manitoba to provinces that participated in the scheme set up by the Act. The effect was to put the plaintiff out of business and it sued the federal government for its loss of goodwill. The Supreme Court of Canada held that the plaintiff was entitled to compensation.

[27]                  Ritchie J., giving the judgment of the court, relied heavily on the leading case of Attorney-General v. De Keyser's Royal Hotel, Ltd., [1920] A.C. 508, and particularly the judgment of Lord Atkinson. In that case Lord Atkinson had this to say at p. 542:

The recognized rule for the construction of statutes is that, unless the words of the statute clearly so demand, a statute is not to be construed so as to take away the property of a subject without compensation. Bowen L.J. in London and North Western Ry. Co. v. Evans, [1893] 1 Ch. 16, 28, said: "The Legislature cannot fairly be supposed to intend, in the absence of clear words shewing such intention, that one man's property shall be confiscated for the benefit of others, or of the public, without any compensation being provided for him in respect of what is taken compulsorily from him. Parliament in its omnipotence can, of course, override or disregard this ordinary principle ... if it sees fit to do so, but, it is not likely that it will be found disregarding it, without plain expressions of such a purpose."

[28]                  If the courts lean against confiscation of property without payment of compensation when the fact of taking is clear, it follows that they will in an appropriate case lean against the taking: see Attorney-General ex rel. Whitechapel Board of Works v. Homer (1884), 14 Q.B.D. 245, where Brett M.R. refused on this basis to construe a statute as interfering with the plaintiff's property rights to a market; affirmed 11 App. Cas. 66. At times, a similar approach is expressed in terms of a presumption against interference with common law rights: see Turton v. Turnbull, [1934] 2 K.B. 197 at p. 201, and East Coast Amusement Co. Ltd. v. British Transport Board ("Re 'Wonderland' Cleethorpes'"), [1965] A.C. 58 at p. 71, per Viscount Simonds. That is not surprising because these cases are all merely special applications of the same general principle: see Maxwell on the Interpretation of Statutes, 12th ed. (1969), pp. 251-2 and Keir and Lawson, p. 4.

[29]                  A recent Supreme Court of Canada decision, Board of Industrial Relations v. Avco Financial Services Realty Ltd. et al. (1979), 98 D.L.R. (3d) 695, [1979] 2 S.C.R. 699, 33 C.B.R. (N.S.) 34, provides a convenient example in the present context. There the court was asked to interpret s. 5A of the British Columbia Payment of Wages Act. Subsection (1) provided that wages certified as having been unpaid by an employer constituted a lien "payable in priority over any other claim or right. .. including . . . every mortgage of real or personal property" and s-s.(2) went on to say that a certificate of such unpaid wages "shall constitute a lien and charge under s-s.(1) from the date wages were earned". In an action to determine the priority of the lien in relation to mortgages registered against the employer's land before the wages were earned, the Supreme Court held that in the absence of specific statutory indication of the property to which the lien attached, s. 5A "should not be construed in a manner which could deprive third parties of their pre-existing property right".

[30]                  In coming to this conclusion, the court stressed the inequitable results that would flow from an interpretation that included property other than the employer's. It quoted from the judgment of Robertson J.A. in the British Columbia Court of Appeal words that are apt in the present context. The section, if broadly interpreted, would, he said, "confiscate in order to pay wages owing by an employer, not only land in which he might have an equity, but also, for example, land of which he was only a lessee for years": see 81 D.L.R. (3d) 289 at p. 294, 5 B.C.L.R. 289, sub nom. Homeplan Realty Ltd. v. Avco Financial Services Realty Ltd. et al.

[31]                  The principle I have described is fundamental. It is a constitutional principle in the British sense: see Keir and Lawson, p. 4. The Legislature may modify or even abolish it in certain circumstances, but it is not to be readily assumed that it has done so.

[32]                  Like other constitutional principles, its precise content will vary over time and the Legislature undoubtedly has the major burden of adjusting its application to changing circumstances. Accordingly, the courts must in applying it do so with great caution to ensure that its application conforms to changing social values and affords the Legislature the widest possible scope in the performance of its task of adjusting private rights to meet evolving social realities.

[33]                  The courts should not, for example, place themselves in the position of frustrating regulatory schemes or measures obviously intended to reallocate rights and resources simply because they affect vested rights. For legislation almost inevitably affects vested rights. They must similarly have great deference for legislative schemes establishing priorities among creditors and encumbrancers and, in particular, those that favour the Crown over other creditors; indeed the common law itself has always recognized certain Crown priorities flowing from the prerogative: see The Queen v. Bank of Nova Scotia et al. (1885), 11 S.C.R. 1.

[34]                  It is probably to avoid difficulties of this kind that the security of property was not expressly protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. But when we are asked to construe a statute as taking the property of one person to pay the debts of another, as we are here, it is our duty to scrutinize the legislation with great care to see whether the Legislature really intended to do this. That has always been an important role of the courts and the Charter, by s. 26, carefully provides that it is not to be construed "as denying the existence of other rights and freedoms that exist in Canada".

[35]                  In performing this duty, the courts should not be looked upon as being at odds with the Legislature. Rather they are working along with the Legislature to ensure the preservation of our fundamental political values. All branches of government have an interest and a duty in doing so. Lord Radclife in Belfast Corp. v. O. D. Cars Ltd., [1960] A.C. 490 at p. 523, put it this way:

On the one hand, there would be the general principle, accepted by the legislature and scrupulously defended by the courts, that the title to property or the enjoyment of its possession was not to be compulsorily acquired from a subject unless full compensation was afforded in its place. Acquisition of title or possession was "taking." Aspects of this principle are found in the rules of statutory interpretation devised by the courts, which required the presence of the most explicit words before an acquisition could be held to be sanctioned by an Act of Parliament wihout full compensation being provided, or imported any intention to give compensation and machinery for assessing it into any Act of Parliament that did not positively exclude it. This vigilance to see that the subject's rights to property were protected, so far as was consistent with the requirements of expropriation of what was previously enjoyed in specie, was regarded as an important guarantee of individual liberty. It would be a mistake to look on it as representing any conflict between the legislature and the courts. The principle was, generally speaking, common to both.

[36]                  It may be that some of the beneficiaries of the principle in the present cases may not appear deserving of protection or that some of the interests involved are of small consequence. But a jewel does not lose its value by being placed in a trivial or unworthy setting. For if, for example, we were to apply the lien to property of a landlord whose identity might, if we were to pierce the corporate veil, be found to be the same as the owner of a company to which he has leased the property, we would have to apply it to any landlord who leases premises to a vendor though he had no other relationship with the landlord. If the Legislature wishes to include landlords (and others for that matter) who are not at arm's length with the vendor, it has many legislative techniques available by way of inclusion or exclusion or by resort to ministerial or official discretion. And if it really wishes to override the interests of an owner who has no connection with the vendor apart from a landlord-tenant relationship and thereby make him pay the tenant's liabilities under the tax system, it can say so expressly, as it appears to have done in s. 72(2) of the Workers' Compensation Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. W-13.

[37]                  I might add in passing that s. 17 of the Interpretation Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. I-13, which deems all statutes remedial and requires "a fair, large and liberal" interpretation of all statutes, has nothing to do with this principle. That provision was enacted to correct an earlier but vanishing distinction between the "strict" approach of the courts to penal and taxation statutes and the "liberal" construction given other statutes: see Driedger, p. 148 et seq. The courts, be it noted, still require that a penal or taxation statute clearly bring within their ambits those made subject to a penalty or tax either on the theory that Parliament intends to act reasonably or because it is difficult to determine a purpose beyond the words of the statute. In any event, the former dichotomy between strict and liberal construction was something quite separate from the presumption referred to earlier.

Analysis of the section

[38]                  Apart from the application of the principle I have described, there is considerable evidence that the Legislature did not by the provision here in question intend to attach the property owned by persons other than the vendor. To begin with, there is the equally authoritative French version of the statute which is clearly limited to the vendor's property. And though, as we saw, this raises some problems of redundancy this version cannot be ignored. Especially is this so in this instance, since the English version, too, gives some indication that the application of the lien created by s. 19 was limited to property of the vendor. Thus the section gives the lien priority over "every claim, privilege, lien or encumbrance whenever created", words that ordinarily comprise third party claims, mere security interests rather than ownership.

[39]                  It is true, as counsel for the province argued, that "claim" is a broad general word that is capable in certain contexts of including ownership. But words, particularly broad, flexible words like this one, are coloured by the context in which they appear and all the other associated words refer to claims against goods owned by another: see Driedger, pp. 85-6. What is more the section refers to s. 72(2) of the Workers' Compensation Act which gives a lien for assessments levied against an employer against "property . . . used in or in connection, with or produced in or by the industry . . . though not owned by the employer". With that section clearly in contemplation, one would think the draftsman would have used similar words if it was desired to attach property other than that owned by the vendor.

Legislative history

[40]                  But it is the legislative evolution of the section that most strongly militates against the view that the Legislature intended to cover property owned by a person other than the vendor under the Act. Resort to legislative history is, of course, a well-known device for determining the intention of the Legislature: see R. v. Popovic and Askov (1975), 62 D.L.R. (3d) 56, 25 C.C.C. (2d) 161, 32 C.R.N.S. 54 (S.C.C.).

[41]                  Though counsel for the province argued that the legislative history of the section clearly established an ever-widening ambit of the lien created by s. 19(1), I cannot so view it. What it shows, rather, is a somewhat confused picture. Let us begin with the section as it originally appeared in the 1973 revision in which form it had existed since its enactment in 1955, as s. 17A (1955, c. 75, s. 3). That revision read as follows:

19. Every vendor who collects any tax under this Act is deemed to hold the same in trust for Her Majesty in right of the Province and for the payment over of the same in the manner and at the time provided under this Act, and the amount shall, until paid, form a lien and charge on his entire estate or on the entire assets of his estate in the hands of any trustee, and such lien or charge has priority over all other claims of any person.

One would have thought the vendor's "entire estate" would cover "the entire assets of his estate in the hands of any trustee", so the latter words seem surplusage, but it appears to show a clear intention on the part of the Legislature to limit the section to the property owned by the vendor whether at law or in equity, and it is in respect of such property that the lien takes priority over the claims of others.

[42]                  In 1978 [c. 55, s. 6], the section was repealed and substituted by the following:

19. Every vendor who collects any tax under this Act is deemed to hold the same in trust for Her Majesty in right of the Province and for the payment over of the same in the manner and at the time provided under this Act, and the amount shall, until paid, form a lien and charge on his entire estate, on the entire assets of his estate in the hands of any trustee or on any assets used in or in connection with or produced in or by the estate and such lien or charge has priority over all other claims of any person.

[43]                  The section, therefore, was extended to include "assets used in or in connection with or produced in or by the estate". The latter words, unlike the present situation, applied to assets used in the entire estate of the vendor ("patrimoine" in the French version), not just the relevant business property. However, the additional matters attached are "assets", a term also employed in the section in relation to trust property where it is confined to the interest of the owner, so that it is by no means certain whether the section was intended to apply to the property of all who might have the misfortune to in any manner share an interest in the property of the vendor, however unconnected with the business.

[44]                  Happily, this ill-constructed provision was itself repealed the following year, 1979 [c. 67, s. 8], and in its place the following was substituted:

19(1) Every vendor who collects any tax under this Act shall be deemed to hold the tax in trust for Her Majesty in right of the Province and for the payment over of the tax in the manner and at the time provided under this Act, and notwithstanding subsection 72(2) of the Workmen's Compensation Act, the amount shall, until paid, form a special lien upon all the property used in or in connection with or produced in or by the business of the vendor, in priority to every claim, privilege, lien or encumbrance, whenever created, subject only to taxes levied under the Real Property Tax Act.

[45]                  This version is particularly important, since it remains the present law and constitutes the major portion of the s. 19 as it exists today. It has been consolidated with later amendments in the loose leaf edition of the revised statutes, but that, of course, does not change its meaning: see Le Gassick et al. v. Continental Automotive Inc. (1981), 127 D.L.R. (3d) 675 at pp. 687-8, 35 N.B.R. (2d) 657, at p. 672, and 37 N.B.R. (2d) 284, per Limerick J.A.

[46]                  Far from indicating an ever-expanding ambit, s. 19, as enacted in 1979, was clearly reduced in its application to what I have described as the business property. Counsel for the province argued, however, that it would include the property of persons other than the vendor. But this proposition runs counter to Crown Trust Co. et al. v. Workmen's Compensation Board et al. (1975), 55 D.L.R. (3d) 498, 7 O.R. (2d) 466, where Houlden J. of the Ontario High Court confined the application of a very similar section in the Ontario Workmen's Compensation Act to the property of the employer.

[47]                  Even in the absence of authority, I would have come to the same conclusion. Quite apart from the presumption I have already discussed one would not, in the absence of a clear indication of such an intention, construe a provision that creates a lien for the purpose of satisfying a debt owing by one person as applying to the property of another. It is trite knowledge that a legislative provision must be interpreted in context in the light of its purpose.

[48]                  This natural assumption is confirmed by the French version which, by speaking of "ses biens", expressly confined the operation of the lien to the property of the vendor. It is worth underlining that it was in this context — when the ordinary reading of the English version would also confine the lien to the vendor's property — that the expression "ses biens" ("his property") appeared in the French version.

[49]                  Nor is this all. The English version contains other clues that tend to confirm this interpretation. It is in this 1979 version of s. 19 that the reference to s. 72(2) of the Workmen's Compensation Act first appeared and the lien in that section, then as now, expressly provided that it applied to non-owners. The section reads as follows:

72(2) The amount of an assessment including provisional amounts and any judgment with respect to the assessment is a first lien upon all the property, real, personal or mixed used in or in connection with or produced in or by the industry with respect to which the employer is assessed, though not owned by the employer, subject only to taxes levied under the Real Property Tax Act, and the amount levied under execution upon ay such judgment to the extent of the amount due upon that execution shall forthwith be paid by the sheriff or the deputy sheriff to the Board.

(Emphasis added.) The draftsman would have known that this related section would be referred to in clarifying any doubts there might be about the interpretation of s. 19(1).

[50]                  As already mentioned, the types of property made subject to the lien on the vendor's property appear to be various kinds of encumbrances and security interests. These, it is true, would include the interest of a mortgagee, who though technically the legal owner, in fact really owns an encumbrance, for s. 19(5), which was enacted at the same time, clearly contemplates that interest. (This might have been debatable before: cf., Crown Trust Co. v. Workmen's Compensation Board, supra; Dinning v. Workmen's Compensation Bd., [1932] 1 D.L.R. 373, [1932] 1 W.W.R. 136, 13 C.B.R. 256; and Workmen's Compensation Board v. Sumas Oil & Gas Co., [1933] 3 D.L.R. 489, [1933] 2 W.W.R. 121, 47 B.C.R. 12.) I am inclined to think the lien would extend to other situations where the vendor is the owner in a practical sense, if not in abstract legal theory, as for example where the vendor is a purchaser under a conditional sale.

[51]                  But that is a far cry from making the lien apply to property that is really owned by another person. And s. 19(5), by providing express remedies for mortgagees and other encumbrancers whose property is made subject to the lien, appears not to contemplate property owners other than the vendor.

[52]                  The lien under the 1979 version of s. 19(1), therefore, clearly appears to have been limited to property owned by the vendor although, of course, it took priority over other claims to that property. When to the reasons already given one adds the presumption that the Legislature does not intend to take the property of one person to pay for the debts of another, that interpretation becomes irresistible.

[53]                  The 1979 version was then amended by 1980, c. 52, s. 5(a):

(a) by adding immediately after the words "special lien upon" where they appear in subsection (1) thereof the swords "his entire estate, on the entire assets of his estate in the hands of any trustee, and upon";

By doing this, we get the present s. 19(1).

[54]                  The 1980 amendment, it will be noted, is expressly confined to property of the vendor; it applies only to "his estate". But by adding this additional portion of the vendor's property to the 1979 enactment which, we saw, is also limited to the vendor's property, we are asked to come up with the result that the property of a third person is now included, and this in the face of clear words in the French version expressly restricting the lien to the vendor's property. This proposition appears wholly untenable. Had the reverse taken place — if to all the property owned by the vendor the business property had been added — a different issue would have been raised. It would be difficult to explain why the Legislature thought fit to add extra words unless it intended them for a different purpose.

Surplusage

[55]                  But it is said that the words that were in the section in 1979 become surplusage when the new words are inserted into the section unless one, in effect, stretches their original meaning to include, because of the new context in which they appear, property other than that of the vendor. While counsel rightly asserted that one must attempt to give meaning to every word in a statute, the fact is, as I have already pointed out, that surplusage does not appear to be new to the section. There was some from the beginning, and it continues to exist in the very words added in 1980. As already mentioned, I am unable to see what is comprised in "the entire assets of the vendor's estate in the hands of any trustee" that is not covered by "his entire estate".

[56]                  The probability is that when the 1980 amendment was enacted the Legislature intended nothing more than to bring back within the scope of the section property that had been covered by the section as originally enacted in 1955 and that it overlooked the fact that this made superfluous some of the words enacted in 1979. That is at least as probable as the idea that the Legislature was, probably for the first time, attempting to cover property of a person other than a vendor in this covert manner, a device I would not lightly impute to the Legislature. And I do not think this possibility is made more persuasive by the fact that the 1978 enactment may have included property owned by a person other than the vendor, especially so when the Legislature had moved to retract this step in 1979.

[57]                  Whatever might have been the result had the whole section been enacted at one time, the least that can be said in the light of its legislative history is that one is left in considerable doubt about the intention of the Legislature. Under those circumstances, it seems clear that one should exercise the presumption against a gratuitous taking by the Legislature of property owned by a person other than the person directly aimed at by the Legislature.

[58]                  But what of the surplusage? There is no magic in this. Though courts do try to give meaning to all the words of a statute, they will ignore excess verbiage where doing so appears to be more consonant with the legislative intent: see Driedger, pp. 72-3. In Salmon v. Buncombe et al. (1886), 11 App. Cas. 627, for example, the Privy Council in effect deleted the last nine words from a short section to arrive at what otherwise appeared to be the intention of the Legislature. Such a course is especially likely to be required in the case of amendments, for in these circumstances the draftsman and the Legislature may not have squarely focused on all the words used in the pre-existing statute. In such cases, the courts have sometimes ignored some of the words or in effect read some in: see Driedger, at pp. 105-6. We need not go that far here. All we need do is treat the surplusage as just that — merely repetitive.

Workers' Compensation Act cases

[59]                  Counsel for the province relied on several cases relating to the Worker's Compensation Act and particularly on two cases decided by this court, Workmen's Compensation Board v. White Motor Co. of Canada Ltd. et al. (1971), 19 D.L.R. (3d) 551, 3 N.B.R. (2d) 565, and Le Gassick et al. v. Continental Automotive Inc., supra, which deal with the predecessor to s. 72(2) of that Act cited above. That section, it will be remembered, also applies to property used in or in connection with the industry.

[60]                  It must be said at the outset that these cases are of very limited use for present purposes. They are based on a statute that is completely different, both in its wording and legislative history, from the one in question here. What is more there is such a wide diversity in the opinions of the judges who sat on these cases that they can only be considered as binding authority on the specific questions decided.

[61]                  In the White Motor case, supra, the Workmen's Compensation Board claimed a lien on equipment sold under a conditional sales contract by the defendant to an employer who used it in connection with his business. The case really turned on whether the lien attached in respect of assessments made against the employer to support the workmen's compensation fund during the year when the contract was made or whether it also attached in respect of arrears from previous years. On that question, a majority decided that the lien extended to property used by the employer during the year of assessment.

[62]                  But the court also had to deal with the ambit of the lien. Hughes J. A. (now C.J.N.B.) in dissent was of the view that the lien attached to all property at any time used by the employer regardless of ownership. Limerick J.A., however, while agreeing that the lien extended to a conditional buyer and other situations where the employer was a joint owner with others, was of the view that for the lien to attach the employer had to have some property, right or interest in the reversion at the time the assessment was made. The other majority judge, Bugold J.A., held that the lien attached to "the property of the employer only which is used in or in connection with the industry" the year the employer is assessed.

[63]                  Taking the view of the case most favourable to the province's position, it should be noted that both Hughes and Limerick JJ.A. based their decisions that the lien extended to the interest of a person other than an employer on the effect of s. 68(2) of the Act which provided that where such a person had an interest in property on which the lien attached, the property was to be deemed that of the employer only. Hughes J.A., for example, had this to say [at p. 564]: "Without the provision contained in s. 68(2) the Board in enforcing its judgment for an assessment could not levy upon property now in the employer's industry unless it was owned by the employer or was property in which the employer had an exigible interest." There is no provision similar to s. 68(2) in the Act under consideration in the present case, so I find it difficult to see how counsel for the province can draw comfort from the White Motor case.

[64]                  In the Continental Automotive case, supra, the court, again by majority, held that the lien under the Workmen's Compensation Act extended to the property owned by the employer at the time the assessment was made though subsequently sold under a conditional sales contract to a third party. The majority this time consisted of Hughes C.J.N.B. and Limerick J.A. with Bugold J.A. in dissent. But there was no change in their previous views of the law; each maintained his original position. Accordingly, this case affords no support for the province's position either.

Subsidiary arguments

[65]                  Counsel for the province also relied on the fact that the lien in s. 19(1) is referred to as a "special lien". I do not know what is special about it except the fact that it ranks in priority to the "first lien" created by the Workers' Compensation Act.

[66]                  The attempt to equate the lien to that attaching under legislation respecting municipal rates does not impress me either; it is well known that municipal rates are taxes on property rather than on a person and the major subject of municipal rates is land, which creates a context wholly different from a lien that applies to the entire estate of a vendor.

Conclusion

[67]                  For the above reasons I do not think the lien created applies to any of the items of property concerned in these two cases. Though, as I mentioned, I would be inclined to think it would apply to the property of a buyer under a conditional sales contract, the vendor was not the conditional buyer under the conditional sales contract in question in the Fisherman's Wharf appeal.

[68]                  Accordingly, I would dismiss the appeals with costs. Such costs are fixed at $600 in respect of the Estabrooks appeal and at $600 in respect of the Fisherman's Wharf appeal.

[69]                  Appeals dismissed.
